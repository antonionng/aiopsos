# Experrt Academy: Choose a task you do every day or week

This lesson is from module 1 of Always-On Agents: Foundations. It is for people who are new to working with AI agents. You will learn how to choose a small, regular task, explain what a correct result looks like and record how long the task takes today. You will finish with a list of three possible tasks and a plan for trying one of them. You can complete the exercise on paper without connecting any accounts or buying another tool.

## Start with work you already understand

Think about something you do every day or week. You might prepare a project report, check deadlines or sort new requests. An AI agent is software that can carry out agreed steps using the information and tools you give it. An always-on agent can keep working on a task between your conversations with it. You still need to explain the task, give it the right information and check what it produces. Some tools can work while you are away; others depend on particular apps or computers being available. The word “always-on” does not mean every tool will work in every situation.

Choose a task you know well enough to check. If you already prepare a weekly report, you probably know which records to read, what the reader needs and which mistakes would matter. That knowledge helps you judge an agent's draft. A request such as “manage my business” leaves too many decisions unexplained. A smaller request, such as “prepare a draft of this week's project report from these records”, gives you something specific to review.

## Meet Northstar Studio

Northstar Studio is a fictional six-person design business used in this course. The owner needs a weekly report showing which projects need attention. A project tracker is simply a list of the studio's projects, their progress and their deadlines. Northstar's practice tracker contains five project records.

In this example, the agent can read the supplied tracker and prepare a draft report. The owner checks the draft and decides what to do next. The agent has not been given permission to contact customers, change project records or change prices. Preparing information and acting on that information are separate tasks.

This distinction matters because a draft can be checked and corrected before anyone relies on it. An email sent to a customer has already reached someone else. Changing a price can affect what a customer is charged. You need to make more decisions before allowing those actions.

## Compare three possible tasks

Northstar could ask for help with preparing the weekly project report, chasing customer payments or choosing prices. All three may involve regular work, but the first task to try should be small enough to check and correct.

Preparing the report is a useful starting point. The information is in a known tracker, and the owner can compare each statement in the draft with the original record. The draft stays in the practice output folder until the owner has checked it. If a deadline is missing, the report can identify the gap rather than guessing a date.

Chasing payments could involve sending messages to customers. Before an agent sends anything, someone would need to check the payment information, decide whether contact is appropriate and approve the message. You could separate out preparation of a draft reminder, but Northstar's exercise gives the agent permission to prepare the project report only.

Choosing prices involves decisions about what the business charges. A wrong decision could affect both the customer and the studio. In this exercise, the owner keeps that decision. These examples help you distinguish work an agent can prepare for review from actions that affect other people.

## Ask three questions before choosing

First, do you have the information the task needs? For Northstar's report, that means the supplied project records. If the agent cannot find the tracker, it should report the problem to the simulated owner instead of inventing a current report.

Second, can you check the result? A polished paragraph is not enough. You need to be able to compare its claims with the original records. Decide what a correct draft should include before you try the task. Northstar's report should identify the reporting date and project IDs, and list overdue work, blocked work and missing information separately. A project ID is a short label that helps you find the exact record behind a statement.

Third, can you fix a mistake before it affects anyone? A draft kept for review gives you time to correct it. Automatically sending messages or changing records removes that opportunity. For your first attempt, keep those actions separate and name the person who checks the draft. In the course, this person may be called the reviewer; it simply means the person responsible for checking the work.

## What checking looks like

The fictional Northstar example uses a reporting date of 1 October 2026, at 09:00 Europe/London. For this exercise, a project is overdue when it is not complete and its due date is earlier than the reporting date. A missing due date needs to be flagged separately; it does not prove the project is overdue.

The Harbour brochure, record P101, is open and was due on 25 September, so it is overdue at the reporting date. The Elm identity project, P102, is complete even though its recorded due date has passed, so it should not appear as overdue. The Larch website, P103, is open and has no recorded due date. That missing information should be listed for the owner to check, rather than turned into an invented deadline.

You do not need to memorise these project names. The lesson is to choose work where you can find the information behind the result and explain why a statement is correct. Checking an agent's work means checking against the source, not judging whether the writing sounds confident.

## Record how long the task takes today

Before trying an agent, write down how you do the task now. Include time spent preparing the work, checking it and fixing mistakes. This gives you a starting point for comparison, sometimes called a baseline. A baseline is simply a record of how the task works before you change it.

Later, compare at least three agent-assisted attempts with your usual method. Include your own checking and correction time, and use the same standard of quality in both comparisons. A quickly written draft only saves time if it is also quick enough to check. This course does not promise a particular time saving; you will use your own records to decide whether the approach helps.

## Your practical exercise

Write down three tasks you do every day or week. For each task, explain which information you use, who needs the result and how you know the work is correct. Ask whether you have the information, can check the result and can fix a mistake before anyone relies on it.

Choose one task where the agent can prepare a draft for you to check. Write down who will check it, what a correct draft should include and which actions remain with you. Record how long the task takes today, including preparation, checking and corrections. Your finished work should contain the three candidate tasks, the one you have chosen, your reasons, the person who checks it, a description of a correct result and your current timing record.

If you prefer to practise with the fictional studio, compare preparing Northstar's report, chasing payments and choosing prices. Choose the report draft, explain why you can check it and keep customer contact and record changes with the owner. Describe how you would record the time if you were doing the report yourself; do not present imagined timings as measured results.

Before moving on, read your choice back to yourself. Could someone else understand which task you chose, which information it needs, who checks it and what a correct result includes? If anything is unclear, make the description more specific. You are ready to continue when you have a small task you understand well and a clear way to check it.
