# HR transformation track: course plans

This file plans the ten courses in the HR transformation track of the Experrt learning academy. Each plan is written to the standard in `.cursor/skills/experrt-course/SKILL.md`. The modules listed for each course in `lib/self-serve/catalog.ts` are the spine, and each plan expands those modules into lessons that teach one move at a time.

These are plans, not shipped lessons. Under the standard, an outline stays an outline until someone commissions it and writes it at this depth. Each plan is detailed enough for a writer to build the lesson data and for a reviewer to judge whether the checks test the outcome.

## How to read these plans

Every lesson follows the lesson shape in the skill. It has a place, an idea, a worked example, a practice, a check, and a bridge. The idea teaches the concept in full sentences and says what the concept is not. The worked example uses a realistic workplace input, the output a model or a colleague produced, and a reading of what happened.

Checks use only the four kinds of interaction the skill allows:

- **Mark.** The learner marks each sentence of a new piece of work with one of two labels. The lesson defined both labels in its idea.
- **Choose.** The learner chooses between two real pieces of work, named by the kind of thing the lesson taught (for example, Brief A and Brief B).
- **Edit.** The learner edits a document on the screen. The check looks for the parts the lesson named, not for a length.
- **Write.** The learner writes the artefact in the final lesson. That artefact is what appears on the signed record.

An ordering control is used only where the sequence is the skill being learned. None of the ten HR courses needs one, so none is planned.

For a Mark check, the plan gives the feedback a learner sees for a correct mark and the feedback for the one wrong mark on each sentence. For a Choose check, it gives the feedback for the correct choice and for the wrong one. For an Edit or a Write check, it lists the parts the check looks for and the note the learner sees for each missing part.

## Rules that apply to every course in this track

A record from any of these courses names the person, the course, the date, and the work they wrote. It never says that the person, their team, or their organisation is compliant with the EU AI Act, UK data protection law, employment law, or any other regulation. Where a course touches Article 4 of the EU AI Act, it says plainly that the learner is preparing evidence of measures their organisation took, and that the course does not make anyone compliant.

No lesson promises a change in pay, and no lesson contains a salary figure. No lesson names a reviewer. Worked examples use invented first names only where a real prompt would contain one, and the lesson then teaches why that name should not have been there.

The artefact checks look for the parts of the work. Across the track, a Write check will not pass if the artefact contains an email address, a telephone number, a National Insurance number, or a phrase that claims compliance or certification. These are pattern checks that run before the part checks, and each has its own note so the learner knows what to remove.

## Slugs

Slugs are generated by `slugify` in `lib/self-serve/catalog.ts`. That function lowercases the title, turns an ampersand into the word "and", replaces every run of other non-alphanumeric characters with a single hyphen, and trims hyphens from the ends. For nine of the ten titles this is the same as simply replacing non-alphanumerics with hyphens. For "EU AI Act Literacy for HR and L&D", the ampersand becomes "and", so the live slug is `eu-ai-act-literacy-for-hr-and-l-and-d`. The plan uses the slug the site actually serves.

## 1. AI for HR and People Teams

**Slug:** `ai-for-hr-and-people-teams`

**Hours and price in the catalogue:** 2.5 hours, £99.

**Promise in the catalogue:** Three HR patterns from your own week, and a hold list for what you never paste.

### Who it is for

This course is for HR advisers, HR business partners, people operations staff, and HR generalists in small organisations who have started to use a generative AI tool, or have been told they may, and want to use it on real HR work without putting a colleague's information at risk. It also suits an office manager who carries the HR role alongside other duties.

The learner must already be able to open an AI chat tool their organisation provides and type a request into it. They should know their organisation's main people policies well enough to find the approved text. They do not need to have taken Prompt Engineering for Professional Work, although it helps, and they do not need any knowledge of data protection law beyond knowing that it exists.

### Outcome

When they finish, the learner can look at their own week of HR work and say which tasks a model may draft and which tasks a person must decide. They can write three reusable briefs for the drafting tasks, each naming the approved source, the reader, the limit on what must not be added, and the person who reads the draft before it leaves. They can also produce a hold list of the information they will never paste into an AI tool, with a reason and an alternative for each line, and they can repair a prompt that already contains held information.

### Learning objectives

1. Classify an HR task as safe to draft or as a decision a person must make, and give the reason in the terms of the lesson.
2. Identify a sentence in a model's draft that adds a policy, an entitlement, or a promise that the source did not contain.
3. Write a reusable brief for a rewrite, a summary, or a template task that names the source, the reader, the limit, and the owner.
4. Identify held information in a prompt, and repair the prompt so the task can still be done without it.
5. Produce a signed hold list and three patterns taken from the learner's own week.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: Safe to draft

**Id:** `safe-to-draft`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson, and it gives the learner the two labels used for the rest of the course. Before anyone writes a brief, they need to know which pieces of their week a model should touch at all.

**Core idea.** A task is safe to draft when three things are true at once. The output is words that a named person will read, change, and own before anyone else sees them. The input contains nothing the tool is not approved to receive. The draft decides nothing about a person, which means it does not rank, reject, rate, score, or reach a conclusion about someone's conduct, performance, or future. Safe to draft is not the same as safe to send, because every draft is still yours to check against the source before it leaves. It is also not a judgement about how easy a task is: a two-line letter telling someone their probation has been extended is easy to write, but it records a decision about a person. When a task decides something about a named worker, it belongs under the second label, a person decides, and the most a model may do is help with the wording after the decision has been made and recorded by someone with the authority to make it.

**Worked example.** An HR adviser lists five tasks from Monday morning. The first is to rewrite the approved hybrid working policy into a short guide for new starters. The second is to draft a template invitation to a first-stage interview. The third is to decide which of two internal applicants goes forward to the panel for a team leader role. The fourth is to turn a list of exit interview themes, already stripped of names by a colleague, into a one-page summary for the leadership team. The fifth is to write the outcome letter for a grievance that the adviser has not yet discussed with the hearing manager.

Marked with the two labels, the first, second, and fourth tasks are safe to draft. Each produces words that the adviser will read and own, each uses a source the adviser is allowed to share, and none of them decides anything about a person. The third task is a person decides, because choosing between two applicants is a selection decision. The fifth is also a person decides, because the outcome of a grievance has not been reached yet. If the adviser asked a model to write that letter now, the model would have to invent the outcome, and the letter would look finished while containing a decision nobody made.

**Practice.** The learner types five tasks from their own last working week into five short fields. For each one they choose "Safe to draft" or "A person decides". The three tests from the idea stay on the screen above the fields: a named person reads and owns the words, the input is approved for the tool, and the draft decides nothing about a person. After each choice, the page shows which of the three tests the learner should be able to answer yes to, so they can change their mind before moving on.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Here are five tasks from a different HR team's week. Mark each task as safe to draft or as a decision a person must make."

Controls: "Safe to draft" and "A person decides".

1. "Draft a welcome message for new starters in the finance team that explains where to find the expenses policy." Correct: Safe to draft. Feedback if correct: "Right. This produces words a person will check and own, from a policy you are allowed to share, and it decides nothing about anyone." Feedback if marked "A person decides": "Look again at the three tests. A welcome message uses an approved policy and does not rank, reject, or judge anyone, so it is safe to draft."
2. "Write the reason we are not extending a named colleague's fixed-term contract." Correct: A person decides. Feedback if correct: "Right. Not extending a contract is a decision about a person, and the reason has to come from the manager who made it." Feedback if marked "Safe to draft": "This task asks the model to supply the reason for a decision about a named person. That fails the third test, so a person decides."
3. "Turn the approved parental leave policy into a one-page question-and-answer sheet for managers." Correct: Safe to draft. Feedback if correct: "Right. The source is approved, the reader is a manager, and you will check every answer against the policy before it goes out." Feedback if marked "A person decides": "Nobody is being judged here. The sheet explains an approved policy, so it is safe to draft, although you still check each answer against the text."
4. "Score the five internal applications for the team leader role against the criteria." Correct: A person decides. Feedback if correct: "Right. Scoring applications is part of selection, and a score is a judgement about a person." Feedback if marked "Safe to draft": "A score is not a draft. It is a judgement about each applicant, so this task fails the third test and a person decides."
5. "Suggest which of the two shortlisted candidates we should make an offer to." Correct: A person decides. Feedback if correct: "Right. A suggestion about who gets the offer is the decision itself, written as advice." Feedback if marked "Safe to draft": "The word suggest does not make this a draft. Choosing who receives an offer is a decision about a person."

A pass shows: "You kept every task that judges a person with a person, and you let the model draft only words you will own."

**Bridge.** The next lesson looks inside a task that is safe to draft and shows how the draft can still add a policy nobody gave it.

#### Lesson 2: What the draft cannot know

**Id:** `what-the-draft-cannot-know`

**Where it sits.** The learner can now pick tasks that are safe to draft. This lesson shows the most common way an HR draft goes wrong even when the task was a sensible one.

**Core idea.** A model writes from the words you gave it and from patterns in the text it learned from. When your request is silent about a policy detail, the model does not stop and ask. It fills the gap with the kind of sentence that usually appears in HR letters, such as a number of days, a rate of pay, a timescale, or a named service. In HR writing this matters more than in most work, because a sentence that states an entitlement can be read by the employee as a promise, quoted back in a grievance, or relied on when they plan their leave. This lesson uses two labels. A sentence stays within what we gave it when every fact in it can be found in the source you supplied, or when it contains no fact at all, such as a greeting or a word of sympathy. A sentence adds a policy we did not give it when it states a number, a right, a process, or a commitment that is not in the source. The second label is not about tone or style. A warm sentence can stay within what we gave it, and a dry sentence can add a policy.

**Worked example.** The adviser writes: "Write a friendly email to an employee returning from long-term sickness absence. Our policy says their manager will hold a return-to-work meeting on their first day." The model returns four sentences. "Welcome back, and I hope you are feeling better." "Your manager will meet you on your first day for a return-to-work conversation." "You will have a phased return over four weeks on full pay." "Occupational health will contact you within five working days."

Reading the draft, the first sentence contains no fact, so it stays within what we gave it. The second sentence repeats the one fact in the source, so it also stays within what we gave it. The third sentence adds a policy we did not give it: a four-week phased return on full pay is a specific entitlement, and the adviser never supplied it. The fourth sentence also adds a policy: it names a service and a timescale that were not in the source. If the email went out as written, the employee could reasonably expect four weeks on full pay and a call from occupational health, and the organisation would then have to honour or retract both.

**Practice.** The learner sees the same four sentences with the source above them. For each sentence they choose "Stays within what we gave it" or "Adds a policy we did not give it", and for each sentence marked as adding a policy they tap the words that carry the added fact. The page then shows the reading from the worked example next to their marks.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "The source said only this: our policy gives five days of paid compassionate leave for the death of a close family member, and the employee should tell their manager which days they will take. Mark each sentence of the model's draft."

Controls: "Stays within what we gave it" and "Adds a policy we did not give it".

1. "I am so sorry for your loss." Correct: Stays within what we gave it. Feedback if correct: "Right. A word of sympathy contains no policy fact, so it cannot add one." Feedback if marked "Adds a policy we did not give it": "This sentence states no number, right, or process. Sympathy on its own stays within what we gave it."
2. "You are entitled to five days of paid compassionate leave." Correct: Stays within what we gave it. Feedback if correct: "Right. Five paid days is exactly what the source says." Feedback if marked "Adds a policy we did not give it": "Check the source again. Five days of paid compassionate leave is the one entitlement it states."
3. "If you need more time, you can take up to ten further days of unpaid leave." Correct: Adds a policy we did not give it. Feedback if correct: "Right. Ten further unpaid days is a new entitlement, and the source said nothing about it." Feedback if marked "Stays within what we gave it": "The source never mentions further leave. Ten unpaid days is a policy the model supplied, and the employee may rely on it."
4. "Please let your manager know which days you will take." Correct: Stays within what we gave it. Feedback if correct: "Right. Telling the manager which days is the process the source describes." Feedback if marked "Adds a policy we did not give it": "This is the process in the source, written as a request. It stays within what we gave it."
5. "We will also cover your travel costs if the funeral is abroad." Correct: Adds a policy we did not give it. Feedback if correct: "Right. Paying travel costs is a financial commitment that appears nowhere in the source." Feedback if marked "Stays within what we gave it": "Nothing in the source mentions travel costs. This sentence commits the organisation to paying money."

A pass shows: "You found both sentences that add a policy we did not give it, and you let the sympathy and the stated entitlement stand."

**Bridge.** The next lesson turns this into a habit by writing briefs that give the model the source and the limit before it starts.

#### Lesson 3: Three patterns

**Id:** `three-patterns`

**Where it sits.** The learner can now choose a safe task and read a draft for added policy. This lesson gives them three reusable briefs for the kinds of HR drafting that come round every week.

**Core idea.** A pattern is a brief you can reuse every time a certain kind of task comes round, with the same four parts each time. The source is the approved text or the prepared material the model may use, and nothing else. The reader is the person the draft is written for, such as a new starter, a line manager, or the leadership team. The limit says what the draft must not add, most often a number, an entitlement, a date, or a judgement about a person. The owner is the person who reads the draft against the source before it leaves. In HR work, three patterns cover most safe drafting. A rewrite pattern turns approved policy text into words for a named reader. A summary pattern turns material that has already been stripped of anything identifying into themes for a named reader. A template pattern produces a blank document with placeholders and no real person in it. A pattern is not a library of fifty clever prompts, and it is never a brief that asks the model to make a decision, because a pattern that decides would fail the tests from the first lesson every time it ran.

**Worked example.** The adviser writes a rewrite pattern for the sickness absence policy. "Source: the text of our sickness absence policy, pasted below, and nothing else. Reader: a line manager who has never handled a long-term absence. Limit: do not add any number of days, any rate of pay, any service, or any timescale that is not in the source; if the source does not answer a question, write 'Check with HR'. Owner: I will read the draft against the policy before it goes to managers. Output: a one-page guide with five headings." The model returns a guide with five headings. Under the heading about phased returns, it writes "Check with HR", because the policy says nothing about them.

Reading what happened, the limit did its work. In the previous lesson, the same silence in the policy produced a four-week phased return on full pay. Here, the limit told the model what to write when the source was silent, so the gap is visible instead of filled. The owner line matters as well, because it makes it plain who is accountable for the guide.

**Practice.** The learner chooses one of the three patterns and fills in the four parts for a task from their own week, with a line of help under each part. The help under the limit reminds them of the added policies they found in the previous lesson. The page does not judge the practice, but it shows the worked example beside their draft so they can compare.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Both of these are summary briefs for this month's exit interviews. Choose the one you would reuse as a pattern."

Controls: "Brief A" and "Brief B".

Brief A: "Here are this month's exit interview notes. Summarise why people are leaving and tell me which teams are most at risk of losing more people."

Brief B: "Source: the list of themes below, which a colleague has already stripped of names, job titles, and dates. Reader: the leadership team. Limit: report only themes that appear in the list, do not estimate numbers, and do not name any team with fewer than ten people. Owner: the head of people reads the summary against the list before it is sent. Output: five bullet points and one sentence on what the list cannot tell us."

Correct: Brief B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Brief B names a prepared source, a reader, a limit on what must not be added, and the owner who checks it, so it can be reused every month." Feedback if Brief A is chosen: "Brief A pastes raw exit notes, which may identify people, and asks which teams are most at risk, which is a judgement the model cannot make from notes. It has no limit and no owner, so it is not a pattern you can reuse."

A pass shows: "You chose the brief with a prepared source, a reader, a limit, and an owner."

**Bridge.** The source is the part of a pattern most likely to carry something that should never be in the tool, and the next lesson names what that is.

#### Lesson 4: What you never paste

**Id:** `what-you-never-paste`

**Where it sits.** The learner now has patterns with a source. This lesson draws the line around what may never be part of that source, whatever the task.

**Core idea.** Some information about workers must not go into an AI tool at all, and this lesson calls it held information, marked with the label Hold. The first group is what UK data protection law calls special category data: information about a person's racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data used to identify them, health, sex life, or sexual orientation. The second group is information about criminal convictions and offences. The third group is information that is not special category in law but causes real harm if it leaks or is misused in HR: grievance, disciplinary, and investigation detail about identifiable people, individual pay and bank details, identity documents, and anything connected to legal advice or a live claim. Information can go in, and is marked Can go in, when it is approved policy text, a prepared source with nothing that identifies a person, or a blank template, and when it goes into the tool your organisation has approved for this work. Removing a name does not turn held information into something that can go in, because in a small team a role, a date, and a condition together can identify someone as surely as a name. Your organisation's data protection lead may set a stricter list than this one, and where they do, their list wins.

**Worked example.** A line manager sends the adviser the prompt they were about to use. "Help me write to Priya about her absences. She has had six days off this quarter. She told me it is related to her IVF treatment, and she is in the union, so I want to be careful. Our policy says a review meeting is triggered after five days in a rolling twelve months."

Marking each part, the name Priya is Hold, because it identifies the person. The six days this quarter is Hold, because an individual's absence record is health-related information about an identifiable person. The IVF treatment is Hold, because it is health data and special category data. Union membership is Hold, because it is special category data. The policy trigger of five days in a rolling twelve months is Can go in, because it is approved policy text and says nothing about any person. Only one part of the prompt could go into the tool. The rest was the manager's context, and the letter the manager needs can be drafted from the policy alone.

**Practice.** The learner sees the manager's prompt with each part as a tappable phrase. They mark each phrase "Hold" or "Can go in", and a note under each held phrase asks them which group from the idea it belongs to. The three groups stay visible at the top of the screen.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "A different manager wrote this prompt. Mark each sentence as information that can go in or information you hold."

Controls: "Can go in" and "Hold".

1. "I need a short summary of our flexible working request process for new managers." Correct: Can go in. Feedback if correct: "Right. This describes a task and names no one." Feedback if marked "Hold": "Nothing in this sentence is about a person. It describes the task, so it can go in."
2. "The request from the warehouse supervisor mentions her son's autism diagnosis." Correct: Hold. Feedback if correct: "Right. This is health information about a child, and the supervisor is identifiable by her role." Feedback if marked "Can go in": "There is no name, but the warehouse supervisor is one identifiable person, and her son's diagnosis is health information. It is held."
3. "Our policy says we respond to a request within two months." Correct: Can go in. Feedback if correct: "Right. This is policy text and says nothing about anyone." Feedback if marked "Hold": "This is a sentence from the policy. It identifies no one, so it can go in."
4. "He has had two written warnings for lateness in the past year." Correct: Hold. Feedback if correct: "Right. Disciplinary detail about an identifiable person is held, even without a name." Feedback if marked "Can go in": "Warnings are disciplinary detail about a person. The third group in the lesson holds that, whether or not a name is given."
5. "The team has twelve people across two sites." Correct: Can go in. Feedback if correct: "Right. A headcount across two sites does not identify any individual." Feedback if marked "Hold": "Twelve people across two sites does not point to anyone, so this can go in."

A pass shows: "You held the health information and the disciplinary detail, and you let the task and the policy go in."

**Bridge.** Marking held information is half the move, and the next lesson repairs the prompt so the task can still be done.

#### Lesson 5: Repair the prompt

**Id:** `repair-the-prompt`

**Where it sits.** The learner can now spot held information. This lesson teaches them to rewrite a prompt so the task survives and the held information does not.

**Core idea.** When a prompt contains held information, deleting it is not always enough, because the task may still need something in its place. There are three repairs, and the lesson asks the learner to use the smallest one that works. Remove the detail when the task does not need it at all, which is more often than people expect, because a letter that follows policy rarely needs the reason behind an absence. Generalise the detail when the task needs a type of fact but not the fact itself, for example "an employee" instead of a name and "the absence trigger in the policy has been reached" instead of a count of days. Move the task out of the tool when the held information is the task, as it is when the letter has to respond to what the person disclosed; in that case the adviser writes it, or drafts it with the tool from a blank template and adds the personal part by hand afterwards. A repair is not a pseudonym. Calling Priya "Employee P" and keeping her treatment and her union membership in the prompt changes nothing that matters.

**Worked example.** The adviser repairs the manager's prompt from the previous lesson. The repaired prompt reads: "Source: our sickness absence policy, pasted below. Reader: an employee who has reached the review trigger in the policy. Limit: do not mention any reason for absence, any health condition, or any number of days; do not add any outcome of the meeting. Output: a short, kind letter inviting them to a review meeting, with placeholders for the name, the date, and the time." The model returns a letter with three placeholders and no health information. The manager adds the name and the date by hand in the organisation's own letter system, and adds nothing about the treatment, because the meeting is where that conversation belongs.

Reading what happened, the name and the dates were generalised into placeholders, the treatment and the union membership were removed because the letter never needed them, and the policy stayed as the source. The letter is more useful than the one the manager first asked for, because it cannot be read as a judgement about why the employee was absent.

**Practice.** The learner sees the original prompt and the repaired prompt side by side. For each held phrase in the original, they choose which repair was used: "Remove", "Generalise", or "Move out of the tool". The page confirms each choice with a sentence from the idea.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "This prompt contains held information. Edit it so that the task can still be done and nothing held remains."

The document on screen: "Draft a letter to Tom Reid in payroll confirming his return to work on 3 March after his hernia operation. He is paid on grade 4 of the payroll pay scale and he asked us not to tell his team about the operation. Our policy says a return-to-work meeting takes place on the first day back."

The check looks for these parts in the edited prompt:

- The name is gone or replaced with a placeholder. Note if missing: "The prompt still names the employee. Replace the name with a placeholder that you fill in outside the tool."
- The health condition is gone. Note if missing: "The operation is health information. The letter does not need it, so remove it."
- The pay grade is gone. Note if missing: "Individual pay is held. A return-to-work letter does not need it, so remove it."
- The date is a placeholder rather than the real date. Note if missing: "A real return date and a team together can identify him. Use a placeholder for the date."
- The request for privacy is not in the prompt. Note if missing: "His request not to tell the team is about his health. Keep it in mind when you send the letter, but do not put it in the prompt."
- The policy about the return-to-work meeting is still there. Note if missing: "You removed the source as well. Keep the policy sentence, because the letter has to follow it."
- The task, a letter confirming the return to work, is still there. Note if missing: "The prompt no longer says what to write. Keep the task so the model knows it is a return-to-work letter."

A pass shows: "You removed the health, pay, and privacy details, generalised the name and date into placeholders, and kept the task and the policy."

**Bridge.** The final lesson puts the patterns and the hold list together into the artefact that appears on the record.

#### Lesson 6: The hold list

**Id:** `the-hold-list`

**Where it sits.** This is the last lesson. The learner writes the three patterns and the hold list they will use at work, and signs them.

**Core idea.** The artefact has two halves that work together. The three patterns say what the learner will use the tool for, each written with a source, a reader, a limit, and an owner, and each taken from a task that really came up in their own week. The hold list says what will never go in, written as lines that each name a kind of information, the reason it is held in the terms of lesson four, and what the learner will do instead when a task seems to need it. A hold list is not a copy of the three groups from the lesson. It is written about the information that actually crosses the learner's desk, so an adviser who handles occupational health referrals writes a line about referral forms, and one who handles payroll queries writes a line about pay slips. The record shows both halves so that a verifier can see the learner has named their own work and their own limits, not repeated a generic rule.

**Worked example.** A complete artefact from an adviser in a manufacturing company begins with a rewrite pattern for the shift swap policy, written for new team leaders, with the limit that no pay rate or notice period may be added. Its summary pattern uses themes from the annual engagement survey that the analytics team has already grouped, with the limit that no site with fewer than ten respondents is named. Its template pattern produces a blank induction checklist for agency workers. The hold list has six lines. One reads: "Occupational health referral forms. Held because they contain health data about a named worker. Instead I write the referral myself from the blank form." Another reads: "Screenshots from the HR system. Held because they show names, pay, and absence together. Instead I copy only the policy text I need."

Reading it, every pattern comes from a real task, every limit names the kind of fact that pattern is likely to invent, and every hold line has a reason and an alternative. A colleague could pick this up and use it on Monday.

**Practice.** The learner drafts one hold list line with help on the screen. The help shows the three parts of a line (the kind of information, why it is held, and what you do instead) and the three groups from lesson four. The page tells them which part their line is missing, if any, before they start the artefact.

**Check.** Kind: Write the artefact.

Question: "Write your three patterns and your hold list for the work you really do. A colleague should be able to use them without asking you what you meant."

Fields: for each of Pattern 1, Pattern 2, and Pattern 3, the fields are "The task from my week", "Source", "Reader", "Limit", and "Owner". Then "Hold list", with at least five lines, each with "Information", "Why it is held", and "What I do instead". Then "Your name as it should appear on the record".

The check's requirements are described under "How learning is validated" below.

**Bridge.** There is no next lesson. The learner signs the record and can open it from My courses.

### Artefact and record

The learner produces a signed sheet with three patterns and a hold list of at least five lines. The signed record names the learner, the course title, the date they signed, and shows the three patterns and the hold list exactly as written. It states that it is a record of work completed on an Experrt self-serve course. It does not state that the learner or their organisation complies with data protection law.

### How learning is validated

Every check must pass before the artefact opens, and there is no skip. For the artefact, a pass requires all of the following, and each has its own note when it is missing:

- Each pattern names a task and a source, and the source is described as policy text, prepared material, or a blank template. A source that says "notes", "emails", "the file", or "the case" produces the note: "This source sounds like raw material about people. Name the approved text or the prepared material you will use."
- Each pattern names a reader by role and an owner by role.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: What Article 4 asks

**Id:** `what-article-4-asks`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. Before anyone maps roles or designs a record, they need to know what the text actually says, because much of what circulates about Article 4 is not in it.

**Core idea.** Article 4 of the EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, says that providers and deployers of AI systems shall take measures to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy of their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf. It says those measures should take into account the people's technical knowledge, experience, education, and training, the context the AI systems are used in, and the persons or groups of persons on whom the systems are used. Article 3 defines AI literacy as the skills, knowledge, and understanding that allow providers, deployers, and affected persons to make an informed deployment of AI systems and to gain awareness of the opportunities, the risks, and the possible harm. The obligation has applied since 2 February 2025. What the text does not do is equally important: it does not require an exam, a named qualification, an accredited certificate, or the same training for everyone, and it does not say that delivering training makes an organisation compliant. In this lesson a statement is In the text when the words of Article 4 or the definition in Article 3 support it, and Not in the text when it adds a requirement, a format, or a guarantee that the text does not contain. Whether the Act applies to a particular organisation, including one based in the United Kingdom, depends on the scope rules in Article 2, and that is a question for the organisation's lawyers, not for this course.

**Worked example.** An L&D lead receives an email from a training vendor. It says: "Under Article 4, every employee must complete a certified AI literacy course by the deadline. Our two-hour module makes your organisation fully compliant. The Act requires annual testing with a pass mark."

Reading it against the text, the first sentence is Not in the text: Article 4 does not require a certified course, and it covers staff and others dealing with AI systems on the organisation's behalf, not every employee regardless of their work. The second sentence is Not in the text, because no training module makes an organisation compliant, and the text speaks of measures to the best extent, taking context into account. The third sentence is Not in the text, because the Article contains no testing schedule and no pass mark. None of these claims is necessarily a bad idea as a measure, but none of them is what the law asks.

**Practice.** The learner reads the full text of Article 4 and the Article 3 definition, shown in a panel they can keep open. They then read three sentences from a real-looking intranet announcement and mark each one "In the text" or "Not in the text", with the relevant words of the Article highlighted after each mark.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each statement about Article 4 as in the text or not in the text."

Controls: "In the text" and "Not in the text".

1. "Every employee must pass an AI literacy exam." Correct: Not in the text. Feedback if correct: "Right. The Article asks for measures to ensure a sufficient level of literacy, and it does not mention an exam." Feedback if marked "In the text": "Read the Article again. It speaks of measures and a sufficient level, and it never mentions an exam or a pass."
2. "Measures should take account of the context in which the AI systems are used." Correct: In the text. Feedback if correct: "Right. The Article names the context the AI systems are to be used in." Feedback if marked "Not in the text": "The Article says measures take into account the context the systems are to be used in. This is in the text."
3. "Staff must hold an accredited AI qualification." Correct: Not in the text. Feedback if correct: "Right. No qualification or accreditation is named anywhere in the Article." Feedback if marked "In the text": "Look for the word qualification or accredited in the Article. Neither appears, so this is not in the text."
4. "The duty can cover contractors who use AI systems on the organisation's behalf." Correct: In the text. Feedback if correct: "Right. The Article covers staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on the organisation's behalf." Feedback if marked "Not in the text": "The Article is not limited to employees. It includes other persons dealing with AI systems on the organisation's behalf."
5. "Once the training has been delivered, the organisation is compliant with Article 4." Correct: Not in the text. Feedback if correct: "Right. A pass shows: "You kept to the words of the Article and rejected the exam, the qualification, and the claim of compliance."

**Bridge.** The Article says the measure depends on the people and the context, and the next lesson shows how that changes from one HR role to another.

#### Lesson 2: The context sets the level

**Id:** `the-context-sets-the-level`

**Where it sits.** The learner knows what the Article asks. This lesson teaches them to judge what a sufficient measure looks like for a particular role, using the factors the Article names.

**Core idea.** The Article does not define one level of AI literacy for everyone. It asks the organisation to weigh the people's existing knowledge, experience, education, and training, the context in which the AI system is used, and the people on whom it is used. In HR that last factor carries particular weight, because the people affected are often job applicants and employees, and decisions about them can shape their livelihood. The Act itself recognises this. Annex III lists AI systems used for recruitment and selection, and for decisions about terms of work, promotion, termination, task allocation, and monitoring or evaluating performance, as high-risk. Article 26 requires deployers of high-risk systems to assign human oversight to people who have the necessary competence, training, and authority. Article 5 prohibits AI systems that infer the emotions of a person in the workplace, except for medical or safety reasons. A sufficient measure is therefore specific to the system and the use: it tells the person what this system does, where it is known to fail, what they must do when they disagree with it, and how to record what they did. It is not a general awareness session about what AI is, although that may be a useful first step for people with no background at all.

**Worked example.** An organisation lists three uses. A recruiter uses a screening feature in the applicant tracking system that ranks applications. A learning designer uses a general chat assistant to draft course outlines. A payroll officer uses no AI system at all. The L&D lead writes one sentence on the measure for each.

For the recruiter, the measure is a session on this screening feature: what it ranks on, the limits the vendor has documented, how to review an application the ranking placed low, and how to record the reason when they override it, with practice on anonymised cases and a review whenever the feature changes. For the learning designer, the measure is guidance on checking drafts against source material and on what must not be pasted into the tool, because the output is reviewed before anyone sees it and affects no individual directly. For the payroll officer, the lead writes that no measure is needed under Article 4 for now, because they do not deal with the operation or use of an AI system, and that the map will be reviewed if that changes. Reading it, the three measures differ because the three contexts differ, and the heaviest measure sits where the output affects applicants.

**Practice.** The learner picks one role in their own organisation that uses an AI system. They answer three prompts on the screen: what the system is used for, who its output affects, and what the person already knows. The page then shows the three factors from the Article beside their answers and asks them to write one sentence describing a measure.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "A recruiter uses an AI feature that ranks job applications. Choose the measure that fits the context the Article describes."

Controls: "Measure A" and "Measure B".

Measure A: "All staff, including the recruiter, watch a thirty-minute video called What Is AI and confirm they have watched it."

Measure B: "The recruiter attends a session on this ranking feature covering what it ranks on, its documented limits, how to review an application it ranked low, and how to record an override. They practise on five anonymised applications, and the session is repeated when the vendor changes the feature."

Correct: Measure B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Measure B is built around this system, the applicants it affects, and what the recruiter must do when they disagree with it, which is the context the Article asks you to weigh." Feedback if Measure A is chosen: "Measure A is the same for everyone and says nothing about the ranking feature or the applicants it affects. The Article asks for measures that take the context of use and the affected people into account."

A pass shows: "You chose the measure that fits the system, the use, and the people affected."

**Bridge.** One role at a time is a start, and the next lesson puts every role on one map.

#### Lesson 3: The role map

**Id:** `the-role-map`

**Where it sits.** The learner can now describe a sufficient measure for one role. This lesson turns that into a role map for the organisation, which is the first half of the artefact.

**Core idea.** A role map is a table with one row for each role that deals with the operation or use of an AI system. Each row has seven parts. The role names a group of people by what they do, not by their names. The system and use says which AI system they use and for what task. Who is affected names the people on whom the output is used, such as applicants, employees, or customers. What they must be able to do is written as observable work, such as "explain to a candidate how their application was reviewed" or "spot and correct an invented figure in a draft". The measure is what the organisation will provide, such as a session, guidance, practice, supervision, or a change to the process. The owner is the role accountable for the measure. The review date is when the row will be looked at again, and a row should also be reviewed whenever the system or the use changes. A role map is not an inventory of software licences and it is not a training calendar. It is the reasoning that links each role to its measure, and a row with no one affected and nothing the person must be able to do is a row that has not been thought through.

**Worked example.** A row for line managers reads: "Role: line managers. System and use: the writing assistant built into the office software, used to draft emails and meeting notes. Who is affected: their team members and, occasionally, customers. What they must be able to do: check a draft for invented facts before sending, and keep personal information about their team out of the tool. Measure: a forty-five-minute practical session and a one-page guide, with a check in the session. Owner: head of L&D. Review date: six months after the session, or sooner if the tool changes."

Reading it, every part answers a question someone could ask later. The affected people explain why the measure includes keeping personal information out of the tool. The "must be able to do" line tells whoever designs the session what the check should test. The review date means the row will not quietly go out of date.

**Practice.** The learner writes one row for a role in their own organisation, with the seven parts as labelled fields and the worked row visible beside them. Each field shows a line of help, and the "must be able to do" field reminds them to start with a verb that describes work someone could see.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "This row from a role map is incomplete. Edit it so that each of the seven parts is there and says something specific."

The document on screen: "Role: HR advisers. System: chat assistant. Measure: training."

The check looks for these parts:

- A use for the system, naming a task. Note if missing: "Say what the advisers use the chat assistant for, such as drafting policy summaries."
- Who is affected, naming a group of people. Note if missing: "Name the people on whom the output is used, such as employees or managers."
- What they must be able to do, starting with a verb that describes observable work. Note if missing: "Write what an adviser must be able to do, as work someone could see, such as checking a summary against the policy."
- A measure more specific than the word "training". Note if missing: "Training on its own does not say what the organisation will provide. Name the session, the guide, the practice, or the change."
- An owner, named by role. Note if missing: "Name the role accountable for this measure."
- A review date or a review trigger. Note if missing: "Add when this row will be reviewed, or what change will trigger a review."

A pass shows: "Your row now links the role to its use, the people affected, what they must be able to do, and a measure with an owner and a review."

**Bridge.** A map says what the organisation intends, and the next lesson describes the record that shows what it actually did.

#### Lesson 4: What a record contains

**Id:** `what-a-record-contains`

**Where it sits.** The learner has a role map. This lesson designs the record that sits beside it, which is the second half of the artefact.

**Core idea.** A record of a measure is the evidence that a measure in the role map was actually taken. For each measure, it shows who took part, identified by name inside the organisation's own system and by role in any summary; which role map row the measure belongs to; what the measure was; the date; what the person showed they could do, where the measure included a check; when it will next be reviewed; and who holds the record. In this lesson a line of a record is Evidence of a measure when it states a fact about what was provided, to whom, and when, or about work the person did. A line is A claim the record cannot support when it says something no set of attendance and work can prove, such as that a person is AI literate in every context, that the organisation is compliant, or that a regulator has approved the approach. A record is not a certificate of compliance, and it is not a percentage of staff who clicked through a module. Keep it in the organisation's own systems, and apply the same data protection care as any other training record.

**Worked example.** A draft record reads: "Recruitment team, row 2 of the role map. Session on the applicant ranking feature, delivered on 14 May. Seven of seven recruiters attended. Each reviewed five anonymised applications and recorded an override with a reason. Next review when the vendor releases the next version, or by November at the latest. Record held by the head of talent. The recruitment team is now fully AI Act compliant."

Reading it line by line, every line up to the last is Evidence of a measure: each states what was provided, to whom, when, what they did, and who holds the record. The last line is A claim the record cannot support. The session shows that a measure was taken, and it cannot show that anyone is compliant.

**Practice.** The learner reads the worked record with each line as a tappable phrase and marks each one. The page then asks them to rewrite the last line so it states what was done rather than what it proves, and shows an example rewrite after they try.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each line of this draft record as evidence of a measure or as a claim the record cannot support."

1. "Line managers in the operations division, row 4 of the role map." Correct: Evidence of a measure. Feedback if correct: "Right. It ties the record to a role and a row in the map." Feedback if marked "A claim the record cannot support": "This line only says whom the record is about and which row it belongs to. That is evidence of a measure."

#### Lesson 5: What you will not claim

**Id:** `what-you-will-not-claim`

**Where it sits.** This is the last lesson. The learner writes the artefact that appears on their record: a role map, a record outline, and a statement of what the work does not claim.

**Core idea.** The most common harm in this area is not a missing record but an overclaiming one. When a slide or a policy says that the organisation "is compliant with Article 4", it invites a question nobody inside the organisation can answer with certainty, and it can stop people doing the work of keeping the measures current. The statement the learner writes uses verbs that describe action: we identified these roles, we provided these measures, on these dates, and we will review them on this date. It avoids words that describe a legal status, such as compliant, certified, approved, or meets the requirements. It also says who to ask about whether and how the Act applies, which is the organisation's legal adviser. This statement is not modesty for its own sake. It is accurate, and an accurate statement is the one a professional can defend if someone asks what the work shows.

**Worked example.** The L&D lead writes: "This role map and record outline describe the AI literacy measures we have planned and taken for staff who use AI systems on our behalf. They are evidence of measures and of when we took them. They are not a statement that we comply with the EU AI Act, and they have not been reviewed by any regulator. Questions about whether and how the Act applies to us go to our legal team. We will review the map every six months and whenever a system or its use changes."

Reading it, every sentence can be supported by the map and the record. The one sentence about compliance says what the work is not, which is the only safe thing to say about compliance in a document like this.

2. Rewrite a vague learning aim as a skill, a task, and a check.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: Why people finish nothing

**Id:** `why-people-finish-nothing`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. It names the problem the course solves before offering a way to solve it.

**Core idea.** Many workplace programmes are built as libraries: a set of modules, videos, and documents that people are asked to work through. A library describes content to consume, which is what the programme gives the learner to read, watch, or listen to. A programme built for work describes work the learner does, which is what the learner produces, decides, or practises while they are in it. When a programme is all content, people have no reason to finish it other than a deadline, and when they do finish, the programme has no way to show that anything changed. Research on the transfer of training, including the review by Baldwin and Ford, has long pointed to the design of the task and the work environment as factors in whether learning is used on the job. This lesson is not an argument against content. Good content is part of good learning, but it serves the task instead of being the programme.

**Worked example.** An induction programme for new customer service advisers is described like this. "Module 1: Our history and values, a twelve-minute video. Module 2: Product overview, a thirty-page guide. Module 3: The complaints policy, a slide deck. Module 4: A quiz of twenty questions." Read with the two labels, all four modules describe content to consume, including the quiz, which asks the learner to recall facts from the deck. Nothing in the programme asks the new adviser to do the thing they will do on their first day, which is to answer a customer who is unhappy.

A redesigned version might read: "Handle three real complaints from last month, with names removed, using the complaints policy. For each one, write the reply you would send and choose whether to escalate. Your team leader reviews the three replies with you." That describes work the learner does, and the policy becomes the content they need in order to do it.

**Practice.** The learner types the module list of their own programme into short fields, one module per field. They mark each one "Describes content to consume" or "Describes work the learner does". The page then shows how many of their modules describe work, as a plain count, and asks them which single module they would change first.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Here is the outline of a management development programme. Mark each line as content to consume or work the learner does."

Controls: "Describes content to consume" and "Describes work the learner does".

1. "Watch a webinar on the principles of effective delegation." Correct: Describes content to consume. Feedback if correct: "Right. Watching a webinar gives the learner content, and nothing is produced." Feedback if marked "Describes work the learner does": "The learner watches, but does not delegate anything. This describes content to consume."
2. "Delegate one real task from your own list this week and write down what you handed over and what you kept." Correct: Describes work the learner does. Feedback if correct: "Right. The learner delegates a real task and produces a note about it." Feedback if marked "Describes content to consume": "This asks the learner to delegate a real task and write about it. That is work the learner does."
3. "Read the chapter on situational leadership." Correct: Describes content to consume. Feedback if correct: "Right. Reading a chapter is content to consume." Feedback if marked "Describes work the learner does": "Reading is how the learner takes in content. Nothing is produced, so this describes content to consume."
4. "Run a one-to-one with a team member using the agenda you drafted in session two, then note what you would change." Correct: Describes work the learner does. Feedback if correct: "Right. The learner runs a real conversation and reflects on it." Feedback if marked "Describes content to consume": "Running a one-to-one is the work itself. This describes work the learner does."
5. "Browse the leadership library for further resources." Correct: Describes content to consume. Feedback if correct: "Right. Browsing a library is the pattern this lesson warns about." Feedback if marked "Describes work the learner does": "Browsing produces nothing and changes nothing at work. It describes content to consume."

A pass shows: "You separated what the programme gives people to read and watch from what it asks them to do."

**Bridge.** The next lesson gives the three parts a programme needs if it is going to describe work: a skill, a task, and a check.

#### Lesson 2: Skill, task, check

**Id:** `skill-task-check`

**Where it sits.** The learner can now see when a programme is only content. This lesson gives them the structure that replaces it.

**Core idea.** A redesigned programme is built from three parts, and each depends on the one before. A skill is something a person can do at work that someone else could see, written with an action verb and a setting, such as "give a customer a delay update that states the new date, the reason, and what happens next". A skill is not a topic, such as "customer communication", and it is not a quality, such as "confidence". A task is a realistic piece of work in which the person uses the skill, taken from the real work wherever possible, such as "reply to three delay enquiries from last week's inbox". A check is the evidence that the person did the task to the standard the skill describes, such as "the team leader confirms that each reply states the date, the reason, and the next step". Cathy Moore's action mapping and similar approaches start from the same place: decide what people need to do before deciding what they need to know. When the three parts line up, the content the programme needs becomes obvious, because it is whatever the person must know to complete the task.

**Worked example.** A learning aim for a finance team reads: "Improve understanding of the expenses policy." Rewritten, the skill becomes "approve or reject an expense claim correctly under the policy and give the reason". The task becomes "review ten real claims from last quarter, with names removed, and decide each one". The check becomes "at least nine of the ten decisions match the finance manager's decisions, and every rejection cites the policy clause". Reading it, the original aim could never be checked, because understanding is not visible. The rewrite names work the finance team does every week, and the check can be run by the person who already knows the right answers.

**Practice.** The learner writes the skill, task, and check for one aim from their own programme. Each field has a line of help: the skill starts with a verb and names a setting, the task is real work, and the check says what someone else would see.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Both of these rewrite the aim 'Build confidence in difficult conversations' for new line managers. Choose the one that gives a skill, a task, and a check."

Controls: "Rewrite A" and "Rewrite B".

Rewrite A: "Skill: confidence in difficult conversations. Task: attend the workshop and take part in the discussion. Check: participants rate their confidence before and after the workshop."

Rewrite B: "Skill: open a conversation about missed deadlines by stating the facts and asking for the team member's view before offering a solution. Task: run that opening in a role play with a colleague, using a case from your own team with details changed. Check: the observer confirms you stated the facts, asked for their view, and did not offer a solution first."

Correct: Rewrite B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Rewrite B names a skill someone could see, a task that uses it, and a check that says what the observer looks for." Feedback if Rewrite A is chosen: "Rewrite A keeps confidence as the skill, which nobody can observe, and its check measures how people feel rather than what they did."

A pass shows: "You chose the rewrite in which the skill can be seen, the task uses it, and the check says what to look for."

**Bridge.** The check is the part people most often get wrong, and the next lesson looks at it closely.

#### Lesson 3: A check that tests the task

**Id:** `a-check-that-tests-the-task`

**Where it sits.** The learner has the three parts. This lesson teaches them to tell a check that tests the task from one that only tests memory.

**Core idea.** A check tests the task when it asks the person to do the work the skill describes, or something very close to it, and judges the result against the standard. A check tests recall when it asks the person to remember a fact, a definition, or a step, without doing the work. Recall has a place: someone who cannot remember the escalation route will struggle to escalate, and research on retrieval practice, summarised in Make It Stick by Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel, shows that being asked to recall material helps people remember it. But a recall question cannot show that the person can apply the fact in a real case, and a programme whose only check is a recall quiz will report success while the work stays the same. A good check also has a standard that another person could apply, so two reviewers looking at the same reply would reach the same answer. A check is not a satisfaction survey and not a completion tick, because neither says anything about whether the task was done.

**Worked example.** The customer service induction from lesson one has two proposed checks. The first is: "Which of these is the correct escalation route for a complaint over £500? A, B, C, or D." The second is: "Here is a complaint about a £650 refund. Write your reply and say whether you would escalate it, and to whom." Read against the idea, the first checks recall, because the learner only chooses a remembered route. The second checks the task, because the learner writes the reply and makes the escalation decision in a realistic case. The second can still be marked against a clear standard: the reply is courteous, it states what will happen next, and the escalation is to the right person.

**Practice.** The learner writes down the check their own programme currently uses and marks it "Checks the task" or "Checks recall". If they mark it as recall, the page asks them to write one sentence describing a check that would test the task instead.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "These are proposed checks for a programme on running safe lifting in a warehouse. Mark each as checking the task or checking recall."

Controls: "Checks the task" and "Checks recall".

1. "List the five steps of a safe lift in the right order." Correct: Checks recall. Feedback if correct: "Right. Listing the steps shows the person remembers them, not that they can lift safely." Feedback if marked "Checks the task": "The person writes a list and lifts nothing. This checks recall."
2. "Lift a boxed item from floor to shelf while the supervisor observes against the five steps." Correct: Checks the task. Feedback if correct: "Right. The person does the lift and the supervisor applies a clear standard." Feedback if marked "Checks recall": "This asks the person to do the work while someone watches against the standard. It checks the task."
3. "Answer ten multiple-choice questions on manual handling regulations." Correct: Checks recall. Feedback if correct: "Right. Questions about regulations check what the person remembers." Feedback if marked "Checks the task": "No lifting happens in a question. This checks recall."
4. "Walk the supervisor through how you would move an awkward load in bay four, then move it." Correct: Checks the task. Feedback if correct: "Right. The person plans and carries out a real move in the real setting." Feedback if marked "Checks recall": "The person plans and then does the move, so this checks the task."
5. "Write down the name of the person you report an injury to." Correct: Checks recall. Feedback if correct: "Right. Knowing who to tell matters, but writing a name shows memory, not a safe lift." Feedback if marked "Checks the task": "Nothing is lifted and nothing is reported. The person writes a remembered name, so this checks recall."

A pass shows: "You kept the checks where the person does the lift, and you set aside the checks that only test memory or feeling."

**Bridge.** Once the task and the check are set, much of the old programme no longer has a job, and the next lesson cuts it.

#### Lesson 4: Cut the library

**Id:** `cut-the-library`

**Where it sits.** The learner has a skill, a task, and a check. This lesson asks them to remove what the programme no longer needs.

**Core idea.** A piece of content earns its place in a redesigned programme only if the learner needs it to complete the task or to pass the check. Everything else is a candidate to cut, move, or make optional. Cutting means removing it from the programme altogether. Moving means putting it where people will find it when they need it, such as a reference page linked from the task. Making it optional means keeping it available for people who want more, without making it part of completion. Cutting is not the same as saying the content is bad or that nobody should read it. The history of the company may be a fine video, but a new adviser does not need it to answer a complaint on day one. Cutting is often the hardest part politically, because every module has an owner, so the redesign sheet records what was cut and why in one sentence per item. That sentence refers to the task, which makes the decision easier to defend than a matter of taste.

**Worked example.** The customer service induction, after redesign, has a task of handling three real complaints and a check reviewed by the team leader. The designer goes through the old modules. The history and values video is made optional, because it does not help with the task, although the values do shape the tone of a reply, so one paragraph on tone is moved into the task instructions. The thirty-page product guide is moved to a reference page linked from the task, because advisers need to look up products while replying. The complaints policy deck is cut and replaced by the policy itself, open beside the task, because the deck summarised a document the learner can now use directly. The twenty-question quiz is cut, because the check now tests the task.

**Practice.** The learner lists the modules of their own programme and marks each one "Keep", "Move", "Make optional", or "Cut", writing one sentence for each that refers to the task. The page shows the worked example beside their list.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "The task in this programme is: write a first-month plan for a new starter in your team, using the onboarding checklist. Edit the outline so that it keeps only what the task needs, and add a one-sentence reason for each change."

The document on screen lists six items. "1. Video: welcome from the chief executive. 2. The onboarding checklist. 3. A guide to booking equipment and system access. 4. Article: the psychology of first impressions. 5. Quiz: ten questions about company history. 6. Task: write the first-month plan and review it with your HR business partner."

The check looks for these parts in the edited outline:

- The onboarding checklist is kept. Note if it was removed: "The task says to use the onboarding checklist. Keep it."
- The guide to equipment and access is kept or moved to a reference link. Note if it was cut: "A first-month plan needs equipment and access dates. Keep the guide or link it from the task."
- The task itself is kept. Note if it was removed: "The task is the programme. Keep it."
- The history quiz is cut. Note if it remains: "The quiz tests company history, which the task does not need. Cut it or say why the task needs it."
- The chief executive video and the article are cut or made optional. Note if either remains as required: "Say how the task needs this item, or cut it or make it optional."
- Every change has a reason that mentions the task or the plan. Note if a reason is missing: "Give a one-sentence reason for this change that refers to the task."

A pass shows: "You kept what the plan needs, moved or cut what it does not, and gave a reason tied to the task each time."

**Bridge.** The programme is now lean, and the next lesson decides what the manager will see when someone has finished it.

#### Lesson 5: What the manager will see

**Id:** `what-the-manager-will-see`

**Where it sits.** The programme has a skill, a task, a check, and only the content it needs. This lesson designs the view the person's manager receives.

**Core idea.** A manager is the person most likely to notice whether learning has changed the work, and the person most likely to create or remove the chance to use it. The manager's view of a redesigned programme should show the task the person did, whether the check has been passed or not yet, and the work itself where appropriate, together with one question the manager can ask in their next one-to-one. It should not be a completion percentage or a score out of a hundred, because a figure like that hides which part of the check was not passed and gives the manager nothing to talk about. It should not show the person's failed attempts, because those are part of learning and are for the programme team to use when improving the design. A good manager view is short, is written in the words of the task, and prompts a conversation about the work.

**Worked example.** A team leader receives this view after a new adviser completes the induction. "Task: handle three real complaints using the complaints policy. Check: passed on all three replies. The replies are attached. Question for your next one-to-one: which of the three was hardest to reply to, and what would you do differently now?" Reading it, the team leader knows what was done, can read the work, and has a question that brings the task back into the job. A view that said "Induction 100% complete" would have given the team leader none of that.

**Practice.** The learner writes the manager view for their own programme, using three fields: the task, what the manager sees about the check, and one question for a one-to-one. The page shows the worked view beside their draft.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "A new starter has finished a redesigned onboarding programme. Choose the view you would send to their manager."

Controls: "View A" and "View B".

View A: "Onboarding: 92% complete. Average quiz score 8 out of 10. Time spent: 3 hours 40 minutes."

View B: "Task: write a first-month plan using the onboarding checklist. Check: passed, reviewed with the HR business partner on Tuesday. The plan is attached. Question for your next one-to-one: which part of the plan has already changed, and why?"

Correct: View B. Feedback if correct: "Right. View B names the task, says the check was passed, attaches the work, and gives the manager a question about it." Feedback if View A is chosen: "View A gives a percentage, a score, and a time, and none of them tells the manager what the person can do or which part of the check is missing. It gives them nothing to talk about."

A pass shows: "You chose the view that shows the task, the check, and the work, with a question for the manager."

**Bridge.** The final lesson brings all of this together on one page for the programme you chose.

#### Lesson 6: Redesign one programme

**Id:** `redesign-one-programme`

**Where it sits.** This is the last lesson. The learner writes the redesign sheet for their own programme, and that sheet appears on their record.

**Core idea.** The redesign sheet is one page that a colleague, a sponsor, or the programme's owner could read and act on. It names the programme as it is today in one sentence. It states the skill as observable work, the task the learner will do, and the check with its standard. It lists what was cut, moved, or made optional, each with a one-sentence reason tied to the task. It describes the manager view, including the question for the one-to-one. It ends with the first thing the learner will change and when. A redesign sheet is not a full course design with every screen written. It is the decision about what the programme is for, written so that someone else can build it and so that the owner can hold the design to it.

**Worked example.** A redesign sheet for a finance team's expenses programme reads, in outline: the current programme is a forty-minute policy walkthrough with a quiz; the skill is to approve or reject an expense claim correctly under the policy and give the reason; the task is to review ten real claims from last quarter with names removed; the check is that at least nine decisions match the finance manager's and every rejection cites the clause; the walkthrough is cut and the policy is linked from the task, because the task uses the policy directly; the quiz is cut, because the check tests the task; the manager sees the ten decisions and the question "Which claim would you still ask about?"; the first change is to replace the quiz with the ten-claim task before the next intake. Reading it, the sponsor can see what the programme is for, what it asks people to do, and how anyone will know if it worked.

**Practice.** The learner checks their skill, task, and check from lesson two against the standard from lesson three, and revises them if needed, before writing the sheet.

**Check.** Kind: Write the artefact.

Question: "Write the redesign sheet for your own programme. Someone who was not in this course should be able to build from it."

Fields: "The programme today", "Skill", "Task", "Check and its standard", "What I cut, moved, or made optional, with a reason for each", "What the manager will see", "The question for the one-to-one", "The first change and when", and "Your name as it should appear on the record".

**Bridge.** There is no next lesson. The learner signs the record and can open it from My courses.

### Artefact and record

The learner produces a one-page redesign sheet for one programme they own or influence. The signed record names the learner, the course title, and the date, and shows the redesign sheet as written. It states that it is a record of work completed on an Experrt self-serve course.

### How learning is validated

Every check must pass before the artefact opens. For the artefact, a pass requires all of the following:

- The skill begins with an action verb and names a setting or a piece of work. A skill that is a single noun, such as "communication" or "leadership", or that uses "understand", "know", "be aware of", or "be confident" as its main verb, produces the note: "Write the skill as something a person does that someone else could see."
- The task describes work the learner does, and it does not begin with "watch", "read", "attend", or "browse". If it does, the note is: "This task describes content to consume. Describe the work the learner will do."
- The check names who or what judges the result and what they look for. A check that is a quiz, a survey, a rating, or a completion produces the note: "This check tests recall or feeling. Describe how someone will see the task done to standard."
- At least one item is listed as cut, moved, or made optional, and every item has a reason. A reason that does not refer to the task, the check, or the work produces the note: "Tie this reason to the task."
- The manager view names the task and the state of the check, and does not rely on a percentage, a score, or time spent.
- The first change names an action and a time.
- The artefact contains no names of individuals other than the signature, and no email address or telephone number.

## 4. Hiring and Selection with AI

**Slug:** `hiring-and-selection-with-ai`

**Hours and price in the catalogue:** 2.5 hours, £129.

**Promise in the catalogue:** What a model may draft, what a person must decide, and a selection standard.

### Who it is for

This course is for recruiters, talent acquisition partners, HR advisers who run recruitment, and hiring managers who shortlist and interview. It suits people who use a general AI assistant for recruitment writing and people whose applicant tracking system has added AI features such as ranking or matching.

The learner must already run or take part in recruitment and know how their organisation writes a job description and person specification. They should know, in outline, that the Equality Act 2010 protects people from discrimination in recruitment. They do not need legal training, and the course does not replace advice on whether a particular tool or process is lawful.

### Outcome

When they finish, the learner can say, for any step in a recruitment process, whether a model may draft it or a person must decide it. They can edit an AI-drafted advert to remove wording that could exclude people for a reason unrelated to the job. They can describe what meaningful human review of a shortlist looks like and recognise when it is only a formality. They can write an audit trail entry that records a decision against the criteria, and they can ask a vendor the questions that matter about an AI feature. They leave with a one-page selection standard for one real vacancy or role family.

### Learning objectives

1. Classify each step of a recruitment process as one a model may draft or one a person decides.
2. Edit an AI-drafted job advert so that every requirement is tied to the job and no wording excludes people for an unrelated reason.
3. Distinguish meaningful human review of a shortlist from a review that only approves the tool's output.
4. Write an audit trail entry that records a decision against the published criteria.
5. Produce a selection standard that states what may be drafted, what people decide, what is recorded, and what is asked of any AI feature.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: What may be drafted

**Id:** `what-may-be-drafted`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. It sets the line the whole course depends on, between writing that supports recruitment and decisions about candidates.

**Core idea.** Recruitment produces a great deal of writing, and much of it may be drafted with a model: a job advert from an agreed person specification, a set of interview questions tied to each criterion, a template email inviting candidates to interview, a scheduling message, or a plain-English summary of the benefits policy. A step may be drafted when the output is text that a person will read and approve, when it is built from criteria and material the organisation has already agreed, and when it does not judge any individual candidate. A person decides every step that judges a candidate: sifting, scoring, ranking, shortlisting, rejecting, choosing whom to offer, and setting the terms of the offer. This lesson calls these two kinds of step May be drafted and A person decides. May be drafted does not mean the draft is automatically fair or accurate, because an advert can still contain excluding language, which the next lesson deals with. A person decides does not mean a tool can never be used near the decision; it means that the judgement about the candidate belongs to a person who has looked at the evidence and has the authority to decide differently from any tool.

**Worked example.** A recruiter lists the steps for a finance analyst vacancy. Draft the advert from the approved person specification. Write interview questions for each of the four criteria. Read the forty applications and decide which ten go to a telephone screen. Send a template rejection email to those not shortlisted. Score each interview against the criteria. Choose the preferred candidate.

Marked with the two labels, drafting the advert, drafting the questions, and drafting the template rejection email may be drafted, because each is text a person approves and none judges an individual. Deciding which ten go forward, scoring interviews, and choosing the preferred candidate are steps where a person decides. Sending the template rejection email is drafting, but the decision about who receives it has already been made by a person in the sift, and the email must not state a reason the person did not give.

**Practice.** The learner writes the steps of a recent recruitment they ran, one per field, and marks each "May be drafted" or "A person decides". A note under each field reminds them of the three tests: a person approves the text, it is built from agreed criteria, and it judges no individual.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "These steps come from a recruitment for a warehouse team leader. Mark each as a step that may be drafted or a step where a person decides."

Controls: "May be drafted" and "A person decides".

1. "Turn the approved person specification into a job advert." Correct: May be drafted. Feedback if correct: "Right. A person will approve the advert, it is built from agreed criteria, and it judges no one." Feedback if marked "A person decides": "The advert judges no candidate. It may be drafted, although you still read it for excluding wording."
2. "Use the ranking in the applicant tracking system to decide who is rejected at the first sift." Correct: A person decides. Feedback if correct: "Right. Rejection is a decision about each applicant, and it belongs to a person who has looked at the evidence." Feedback if marked "May be drafted": "Rejecting applicants judges each of them. A person decides, whatever the ranking says."
3. "Write two interview questions for the criterion 'plans a shift rota that covers peak demand'." Correct: May be drafted. Feedback if correct: "Right. Questions tied to an agreed criterion are text a person will approve." Feedback if marked "A person decides": "Questions do not judge any candidate. They may be drafted from the criterion."
4. "Summarise each candidate's interview and recommend who should get the job." Correct: A person decides. Feedback if correct: "Right. A recommendation about who gets the job is the decision itself." Feedback if marked "May be drafted": "A recommendation about a candidate is a judgement about them. A person decides."
5. "Draft the email inviting shortlisted candidates to an assessment day." Correct: May be drafted. Feedback if correct: "Right. The shortlist was decided by a person, and the invitation is text you will approve." Feedback if marked "A person decides": "The decision about who is invited has been made. The invitation itself may be drafted."

A pass shows: "You kept every judgement about a candidate with a person and let the model draft only text you will approve."

**Bridge.** The first thing a model usually drafts is the advert, and the next lesson shows how an advert can shut people out before anyone applies.

#### Lesson 2: The criteria come first

**Id:** `the-criteria-come-first`

**Where it sits.** The learner can now separate drafting from deciding. This lesson looks at the most common draft, the job advert, and at what can go wrong in it.

**Core idea.** A job advert is only as fair as the criteria behind it. When a model is asked to write an advert with little information, it draws on the patterns in the adverts it learned from, and those patterns can include wording that has nothing to do with the job, such as "young and energetic team", "digital native", "native English speaker", "recent graduate", or a requirement to drive when the job does not involve driving. Under the Equality Act 2010, wording like this can amount to direct or indirect discrimination, because it may put people with a protected characteristic, such as age, race, or disability, at a disadvantage without a justification linked to the job. The fix is to write the criteria first, from what the job actually involves, and to give the model the criteria as its only source for requirements. Every requirement in the finished advert should then be traceable to a criterion. This lesson is not a full course in equality law; it teaches one move, which is to read an advert and tie each requirement to the job or remove it.

**Worked example.** A recruiter asks a model: "Write an advert for a customer support adviser." The model returns an advert that includes: "We are a young, dynamic team looking for a digital native with native-level English and a full driving licence. Recent graduates preferred." The agreed person specification says the adviser must answer customer queries by phone and chat in clear English, use the support system after training, and work flexible shifts between 8am and 8pm.

Reading the draft against the criteria, "young" and "recent graduates preferred" point to age and are not in the criteria. "Digital native" is also associated with age. "Native-level English" may disadvantage people by national origin, while the criterion is clear spoken and written English. The driving licence is not linked to any part of the job and could disadvantage some disabled applicants. The corrected advert says: "You will answer customer queries by phone and chat in clear English, use our support system after full training, and work flexible shifts between 8am and 8pm."

**Practice.** The learner sees the draft advert with its sentences as tappable phrases. For each requirement, they choose the criterion it belongs to, or "Not in the criteria". The page then shows the corrected advert.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "This advert was drafted by a model. The criteria are below it. Edit the advert so every requirement is tied to a criterion and no wording excludes people for a reason unrelated to the job."

The document on screen: "Join our energetic young sales team! We need a recent graduate who can hit the ground running, with native English, a clean driving licence, and no family commitments that would get in the way of travel." Criteria below it: "Sells our software to small businesses by phone and video call. Writes clear proposals in English. Travels to client sites about twice a month, by any means."

The check looks for these parts:

- "Young" and "energetic young" are removed. Note if present: "Young points to age and is not a criterion. Remove it."
- "Recent graduate" is removed or replaced with a criterion. Note if present: "Recent graduate points to age. Describe the work instead."
- "Native English" is replaced with the criterion about clear written English. Note if present: "The criterion is clear proposals in English. Native English can exclude people by national origin."
- The driving licence requirement is removed, because the criterion says travel by any means. Note if present: "The criteria allow travel by any means. A licence is not needed for the job."
- The reference to family commitments is removed. Note if present: "This wording can put people with caring responsibilities, and in particular women, at a disadvantage. The criterion is occasional travel. Say that instead."
- The advert still states the three criteria in some form. Note if any is missing: "The advert no longer describes part of the job. Add back the criterion about phone and video sales, proposals, or travel."

A pass shows: "Every requirement in your advert now comes from the criteria, and the wording that pointed to age, origin, family, or a licence is gone."

**Bridge.** Once applications arrive, the decisions begin, and the next lesson describes what it means for a person to really make them.

#### Lesson 3: What a person decides

**Id:** `what-a-person-decides`

**Where it sits.** The advert is fair and the applications are in. This lesson teaches what meaningful human review of a sift or a shortlist looks like.

**Core idea.** Saying that a person decides is not enough if the person only approves what a tool has already decided. UK data protection law places limits on decisions about people that are based solely on automated processing and have legal or similarly significant effects, and a decision about whether someone gets a job can be one of them. The rules on automated decision-making were amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, so the learner should read the ICO's current guidance rather than rely on older summaries. Whatever the exact rules, meaningful human review has four features. The reviewer looks at the candidate's evidence, not only the tool's score. The reviewer judges the evidence against the published criteria. The reviewer has the authority and the time to reach a different conclusion from the tool, and sometimes does. The reviewer records the reason for the decision in the terms of the criteria. Review that lacks these features is what this lesson calls a formality: a person clicks approve on a ranked list they have not read. A formality is not a safeguard, even though a human took part.

**Worked example.** Two recruiters use the same ranking feature for a sift of two hundred applications. The first exports the top twenty by score, glances at the list, and sends the rest a rejection. The second reads every application the tool placed in the bottom half that meets the essential criteria on the face of the application, finds six strong candidates the tool ranked low because their job titles used unusual wording, adds them to the shortlist, and records why.

Reading it, the first recruiter's review is a formality. They could not have disagreed with the tool, because they never looked at the evidence. The second recruiter's review is meaningful, because they looked at the evidence, judged it against the criteria, reached a different conclusion from the tool in six cases, and recorded the reason.

**Practice.** The learner describes how the last sift they took part in was done, in three short answers: what the reviewer looked at, how they judged it, and whether anyone could have overturned the tool. The page then shows the four features of meaningful review beside their answers.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Two hiring managers describe their shortlisting. Choose the one whose review is meaningful rather than a formality."

Controls: "Manager A" and "Manager B".

Manager A: "The system gives each applicant a match score. I shortlist everyone over 80 and approve the list. It saves me hours, and I sign it off, so a person has made the decision."

Manager B: "I read each application against the four criteria and score it myself. I then compare my scores with the system's match score, and where we disagree by a lot I read the application again. I record my score and one line per criterion for each person I shortlist or reject."

Correct: Manager B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Manager B looks at the evidence, judges it against the criteria, can reach a different view from the tool, and records the reason." Feedback if Manager A is chosen: "Manager A approves a threshold on the tool's score without reading the applications. Signing a list is a formality, not a review of the evidence."

A pass shows: "You chose the review in which a person looks at the evidence, judges it against the criteria, and could disagree with the tool."

**Bridge.** A decision that is meaningful still needs to be written down, and the next lesson describes the record.

#### Lesson 4: The audit trail

**Id:** `the-audit-trail`

**Where it sits.** The learner can now tell a real decision from a formality. This lesson teaches them to record the decision so it can be explained later.

**Core idea.** An audit trail for selection is the record that lets the organisation explain, later, how a decision about a candidate was reached. For each decision point, it records the criteria in use, the evidence the person looked at, the decision, the reason written against the criteria, which tools were used and for what, who decided, and the date. The ICO's employment practices guidance and its work on AI in recruitment both point to the value of being able to explain decisions to candidates, and a candidate may ask for their personal data, including notes about them, through a subject access request. That is a good reason to write every note as if the candidate will read it. In this lesson a line of a trail entry either records the decision against the criteria, when it links evidence to a published criterion, or records an impression the criteria do not contain, when it describes a feeling, a personal characteristic, or a judgement the criteria never asked for, such as "not a good cultural fit" or "seemed nervous". An impression is not always wrong to notice, but it is not a reason, and it should not stand in the trail as one.

**Worked example.** A hiring manager writes notes after an interview for a project coordinator. "Criterion 1, plans work to deadlines: described a clear plan for a six-month office move, with milestones and a contingency. Meets. Criterion 2, communicates progress to stakeholders: gave one example, weekly email updates, but could not describe how they handled a disagreement. Partly meets. Seemed a bit old-fashioned. Would not fit the team's energy. Decision: not progressed, based on criterion 2."

Reading it, the first two lines record the decision against the criteria, because each links evidence to a published criterion. "Seemed a bit old-fashioned" and "would not fit the team's energy" record impressions the criteria do not contain, and the first may point to age. The decision line is sound only because criterion 2 is the reason given. The manager deletes the two impressions and adds what further evidence on criterion 2 would have been needed.

**Practice.** The learner rewrites one impression from the worked example as a line that records evidence against a criterion, or decides it should be deleted. The page shows two acceptable rewrites afterwards.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each line of this trail entry for a data analyst interview as recording the decision against the criteria or recording an impression the criteria do not contain."

Controls: "Records the decision against the criteria" and "Records an impression the criteria do not contain".

1. "Criterion 1, cleans and joins data from two sources: walked through joining sales and returns data, and named the check they ran for duplicates. Meets." Correct: Records the decision against the criteria. Feedback if correct: "Right. It links specific evidence to a published criterion." Feedback if marked "Records an impression the criteria do not contain": "This line names the criterion and the evidence the candidate gave. It records the decision against the criteria."
2. "Very quiet, might struggle in our lively office." Correct: Records an impression the criteria do not contain. Feedback if correct: "Right. Neither quietness nor the office atmosphere is a criterion, and this could disadvantage people for reasons unrelated to the job." Feedback if marked "Records the decision against the criteria": "Which criterion is this about? None of them mentions being lively. This is an impression."
3. "Criterion 2, explains findings to non-specialists: explained a chart clearly, and answered a follow-up question in plain terms. Meets." Correct: Records the decision against the criteria. Feedback if correct: "Right. It ties the evidence to the criterion." Feedback if marked "Records an impression the criteria do not contain": "This line names criterion 2 and the evidence. It records the decision against the criteria."
4. "The tool's match score was 62, so probably not strong enough." Correct: Records an impression the criteria do not contain. Feedback if correct: "Right. A match score is not one of the criteria, and 'probably' is not a reason." Feedback if marked "Records the decision against the criteria": "The criteria are about the work. A tool's score is not a criterion, and this line gives no evidence."
5. "Decision: progressed to final stage, meets criteria 1 and 2, criterion 3 to be tested at the final stage." Correct: Records the decision against the criteria. Feedback if correct: "Right. It states the decision and the criteria behind it." Feedback if marked "Records an impression the criteria do not contain": "This line gives the decision and ties it to the criteria. It records the decision against the criteria."

A pass shows: "You kept the lines that tie evidence to a criterion, and you set aside the impressions and the tool score."

**Bridge.** Many recruiters did not choose the AI features in their systems, and the next lesson gives them questions to ask about them.

#### Lesson 5: Questions for an AI feature

**Id:** `questions-for-an-ai-feature`

**Where it sits.** The learner can decide and record. This lesson helps them ask the right questions about an AI feature in a recruitment tool before relying on it.

**Core idea.** An AI feature in a recruitment tool, such as ranking, matching, or scoring of video interviews, is a decision aid that someone else designed. The organisation using it remains responsible for how it is used. The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology's guide on responsible AI in recruitment and the ICO's report on its audits of AI tools in recruitment both point to the same practical questions. What does the feature judge, and from what data about the candidate? Has it been tested for whether it produces different outcomes for people with different protected characteristics, and can the vendor show the results? Can we see why a particular candidate was scored as they were? Can we switch the feature off, or use the tool without it? Has a data protection impact assessment been done for our use, and who holds it? What are candidates told about the feature? A vendor's statement that the feature is "unbiased" or "compliant" is not an answer to any of these. If the organisation's recruitment reaches the European Union, the EU AI Act lists AI systems used for recruitment and selection as high-risk, and the learner should ask their legal team what that means for the organisation.

**Worked example.** A recruitment manager asks their applicant tracking system vendor about a new "smart ranking" feature. The vendor replies: "Smart ranking uses advanced AI to find your best candidates. It is fully compliant with GDPR and has been designed to be unbiased." The manager's reply lists the questions from the idea and adds one: "Which parts of a candidate's application does the ranking read, and does it read their name, address, or dates of education?" The vendor's second reply says that the ranking reads the whole CV text, has not been tested by protected characteristic on the manager's organisation's own data, and can be switched off by an administrator.

Reading it, the first reply answered none of the questions. The second reply shows that the feature reads information from which age and other characteristics can be inferred, and that no testing has been done on this organisation's applicants. The manager switches the ranking off for the next campaign, records why, and asks for the vendor's testing results before reconsidering.

**Practice.** The learner writes the questions they would send about an AI feature in a tool they use, or would use, with the six questions from the idea as prompts. The page shows the worked example's questions beside theirs.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "A vendor offers an AI feature that scores recorded video interviews. Choose the message you would send before agreeing to use it."

Controls: "Message A" and "Message B".

Message A: "Please confirm the feature is unbiased and GDPR compliant, and send us your certificate."

Message B: "Please tell us what the scoring judges and from which data, including whether it analyses facial expressions or tone of voice. Please share any testing of outcomes by protected characteristic, and say whether it has been tested on applicants like ours. Can we see why a candidate received their score? Can we use the tool with the scoring switched off? What do you recommend we tell candidates?"

Correct: Message B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Message B asks what the feature judges, whether its outcomes have been tested, whether scores can be explained, whether it can be switched off, and what candidates are told." Feedback if Message A is chosen: "Message A invites the vendor to say the words unbiased and compliant, which answer none of the questions in the lesson. It also misses whether the scoring reads faces or voices, which matters a great deal in this case."

A pass shows: "You chose the message that asks what the feature judges and how you would know if it treats people unfairly."

**Bridge.** The final lesson turns everything in the course into one selection standard for a real role.

#### Lesson 6: The selection standard

**Id:** `the-selection-standard`

**Where it sits.** This is the last lesson. The learner writes a selection standard for one real vacancy or role family, and that standard appears on their record.

**Core idea.** A selection standard is one page that anyone involved in a recruitment can follow. It names the vacancy or role family and lists the criteria. It says which steps may be drafted and with what source. It names, by role, who decides each judgement about candidates, and says what that person looks at. It lists the fields of the audit trail entry. It records the answers, or the open questions, about any AI feature in use, and what candidates are told about it. It ends with who owns the standard and when it will be reviewed. A standard is not a legal opinion, and it does not say the process is lawful; it says what the team will do. Its value is that a new hiring manager can pick it up and run a fair, explainable process without having taken this course.

**Worked example.** A standard for a role family of customer support advisers lists four criteria from the person specification. It allows the model to draft the advert from the criteria, the interview questions for each criterion, and the template emails. It says the sift is done by the recruiter, who reads every application against the essential criteria, and the interview decision by the hiring manager and one colleague, who each score against the criteria before comparing. The audit trail records the criteria, the evidence, the decision, the reason against the criteria, the tools used, the decider, and the date. The ranking feature in the applicant tracking system is switched off until the vendor shares testing results. Candidates are told that the organisation uses AI to help draft adverts and emails, and that people make every decision about applications. The standard is owned by the head of talent and reviewed each January.

**Practice.** The learner looks at their answers to the practices in lessons one to five and chooses which ones they will carry into the standard, with a prompt beside each section of the standard.

**Check.** Kind: Write the artefact.

Question: "Write the selection standard for one real vacancy or role family. A hiring manager who was not in this course should be able to run the process from it."

Fields: "The vacancy or role family", "Criteria", "What may be drafted, and from what source", "Who decides each judgement, and what they look at", "The audit trail fields", "AI features in use, and the questions answered or still open", "What candidates are told", "Owner and review date", and "Your name as it should appear on the record".

**Bridge.** There is no next lesson. The learner signs the record and can open it from My courses.

### How learning is validated

Every check must pass before the artefact opens. For the artefact, a pass requires all of the following:

- At least three criteria, each describing work the person will do. A criterion that names an age, a year of graduation, a nationality, native language, or "cultural fit" produces the note: "This criterion may exclude people for a reason unrelated to the job. Describe the work instead."
- The "may be drafted" section names at least one step and its source, and does not include sifting, scoring, ranking, shortlisting, rejecting, or choosing. If it does, the note is: "This step judges candidates. Move it to the section on who decides."

- An owner is named by role and a review date or trigger is given.
- The artefact contains no candidate names, no email address, and no telephone number, and it contains no claim that the process is lawful, compliant, unbiased, or certified.

## 5. Performance and Feedback with AI

**Slug:** `performance-and-feedback-with-ai`

**Hours and price in the catalogue:** 2 hours, £99.

**Promise in the catalogue:** A preparation sheet for one real conversation. The judgement stays with the manager.

### Who it is for

This course is for line managers who hold one-to-ones, give feedback, and write or contribute to performance reviews, and for HR business partners who coach managers through those conversations. It suits a manager who has been tempted to ask a model to "write my review of this person" and wants to know what it can properly help with.

The learner must manage at least one person, or support managers who do, and must have a real conversation coming up that they can prepare for during the course. They should know their organisation's performance review process in outline. They do not need to have used AI tools extensively.

### Outcome

When they finish, the learner can prepare for one real feedback or performance conversation using a model only for the parts that are preparation, such as structure, the wording of an open question, and rehearsing an opening. They can separate what they have seen from what they have inferred, remove characterisations from a draft that the evidence does not support, and write an opening that states the purpose and the evidence and then asks for the other person's view. They leave with a signed preparation sheet for that conversation, in which the judgement is visibly their own.

### Learning objectives

1. Classify a task in preparing for a conversation as preparation a model may help with or a judgement the manager must make.
2. Distinguish a sentence that describes something the manager saw from one that describes something they inferred.
3. Edit an AI-drafted feedback note to remove characterisations the evidence does not support and replace them with observed behaviour and its effect.
4. Choose an opening that states the purpose and the evidence and invites the other person's view.
5. Produce a preparation sheet for one real conversation.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: What you may draft

**Id:** `what-you-may-draft`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. It draws the line between preparing for a conversation and judging the person, before any drafting starts.

**Core idea.** Preparing for a feedback or performance conversation involves two kinds of work, and this lesson names them. Preparation is the work of getting ready to have the conversation well: choosing a structure, wording an open question, thinking about how the person might respond, rehearsing an opening aloud, and making a note of the points you want to cover. Judgement is the work of deciding what you think about the person's performance: which rating they receive, whether they met an objective, whether a concern is serious enough to become formal, and what the evidence means. A model may help with preparation, provided you give it no personal information your organisation has not approved for the tool, because it is helping you say what you have already decided to say. A model must not make the judgement, because it has not seen the person work, it does not know the context, and its output would look like a considered assessment while being a guess. This line is not about whether the model's wording is good; a model can produce a fluent paragraph rating someone's performance, and that fluency is exactly what makes it dangerous.

**Worked example.** A manager has a mid-year review with a team member in two weeks and writes down what they plan to do. Ask the model for a structure for a thirty-minute mid-year review. Ask the model to decide whether the person's objectives are met, exceeded, or not met. Ask the model for three open questions about the person's development. Ask the model to write a paragraph summarising the person's performance for the review form, from the manager's bullet notes. Rehearse the opening with the model playing the team member.

Marked with the two labels, the structure, the open questions, and the rehearsal are preparation. Deciding whether objectives are met is judgement. Writing the summary paragraph for the review form is also judgement, even though it looks like drafting, because the paragraph is the manager's assessment of the person and a model working from bullet notes will fill the gaps with characterisations the manager did not make.

**Practice.** The learner lists what they plan to do before their own upcoming conversation, one item per field, and marks each "Preparation" or "Judgement". A note under each field asks: "Would this step decide what you think of the person?"

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "A manager lists what they will ask a model to do before a conversation about missed deadlines. Mark each as preparation or judgement."

Controls: "Preparation" and "Judgement".

1. "Suggest a structure for a twenty-minute conversation about workload and deadlines." Correct: Preparation. Feedback if correct: "Right. A structure helps you hold the conversation well and decides nothing about the person." Feedback if marked "Judgement": "A structure does not assess anyone. It is preparation."
2. "Tell me whether these missed deadlines are serious enough to start the formal capability process." Correct: Judgement. Feedback if correct: "Right. Whether to go formal is a decision about the person, and it needs you, your HR adviser, and your policy." Feedback if marked "Preparation": "Starting a formal process is one of the most serious judgements a manager makes. It is not preparation."
3. "Give me two open questions to ask about what got in the way." Correct: Preparation. Feedback if correct: "Right. Open questions help you listen, and you decide what to make of the answers." Feedback if marked "Judgement": "Wording a question does not judge anyone. It is preparation."
4. "Write a paragraph explaining why this person is underperforming." Correct: Judgement. Feedback if correct: "Right. The reason for underperformance is exactly what you do not know yet, and the model cannot know it either." Feedback if marked "Preparation": "This asks the model to explain the person's performance. That is a judgement, and one the model would have to invent."
5. "Play the team member so I can practise my opening." Correct: Preparation. Feedback if correct: "Right. Rehearsal helps you say it well, and you are not asking the model to assess the person." Feedback if marked "Judgement": "Practising your opening does not decide anything about the person. It is preparation."

A pass shows: "You used the model for structure, questions, and rehearsal, and you kept every judgement about the person with yourself."

**Bridge.** A judgement needs evidence, and the next lesson separates what you saw from what you only concluded.

#### Lesson 2: What you must have seen

**Id:** `what-you-must-have-seen`

**Where it sits.** The learner has kept the judgement with themselves. This lesson makes sure that judgement rests on evidence they can describe.

**Core idea.** Feedback that a person can act on describes something the manager actually saw or heard, when it happened, and what effect it had. The Center for Creative Leadership's Situation, Behaviour, Impact model is one well-known way to hold that shape. In this lesson a sentence describes Something I saw when it names a specific action or piece of work, a time or occasion, and, where possible, the effect it had, such as "In Tuesday's client call, you answered the pricing question before the client had finished asking it, and the client repeated the question twice." A sentence describes Something I inferred when it names a trait, a motive, or a pattern the manager has concluded from what they saw, such as "you are not a good listener" or "you do not seem committed". Inferences are not forbidden thoughts, and a manager will always form them, but an inference in a feedback conversation invites the person to defend their character instead of discussing the work. It is also the kind of sentence a model produces most readily when it is asked to "write feedback" from thin notes. The evidence must be yours, which is why this lesson comes before any drafting.

**Worked example.** A manager's notes about a team member read: "Late with the monthly report again. Doesn't take ownership. Missed the 5th in March and the 4th in April. The finance team chased twice in April. Bit of an attitude problem when I raised it." Sorted with the two labels, "missed the 5th in March and the 4th in April" is Something I saw, with dates. "The finance team chased twice in April" is also Something I saw, and it shows the effect. "Doesn't take ownership" and "a bit of an attitude problem" are Something I inferred, because each names a trait rather than an action. The manager rewrites the last one as something seen: "When I raised the April report, you said the deadline was unrealistic and then changed the subject." That sentence can now be discussed.

**Practice.** The learner writes four notes about the person they will speak with, without the person's name, and marks each "Something I saw" or "Something I inferred". For each inference, the page asks them to write what they saw that led to it, or to set it aside.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each sentence from a manager's notes as something the manager saw or something they inferred."

Controls: "Something I saw" and "Something I inferred".

1. "In the team meeting on the 12th, you presented the project update without the risks section we agreed." Correct: Something I saw. Feedback if correct: "Right. It names an occasion, a date, and a specific piece of work." Feedback if marked "Something I inferred": "This sentence names the meeting, the date, and the missing section. The manager saw it."
2. "You are not detail-minded." Correct: Something I inferred. Feedback if correct: "Right. It names a trait, not an action, and the person can only argue with it." Feedback if marked "Something I saw": "Nobody can see detail-mindedness. This is a conclusion about the person, so it is inferred."
3. "The client asked twice for the revised timeline before it was sent." Correct: Something I saw. Feedback if correct: "Right. It describes what happened and shows the effect." Feedback if marked "Something I inferred": "This describes two requests from the client, which the manager can point to. It was seen."
4. "You seem to have lost interest in the role." Correct: Something I inferred. Feedback if correct: "Right. Interest is a state of mind the manager has concluded from something else." Feedback if marked "Something I saw": "Nobody sees interest directly. Write what you saw that made you think this."
5. "Your last three expense claims were submitted after the month-end deadline." Correct: Something I saw. Feedback if correct: "Right. It is specific, it can be checked, and it describes an action." Feedback if marked "Something I inferred": "This is three dated submissions that can be checked. It was seen."

A pass shows: "You kept the sentences that name an action and an occasion, and you set aside the traits and states of mind."

**Bridge.** When those notes go into a model, the model can put the inferences back, and the next lesson shows how to catch and remove them.

#### Lesson 3: The draft that invents

**Id:** `the-draft-that-invents`

**Where it sits.** The learner can separate seen from inferred. This lesson applies that to a draft a model produced, which is where invented characterisations most often appear.

**Core idea.** When a manager gives a model a few notes and asks for a feedback message or a review paragraph, the model tends to smooth the notes into a finished-sounding narrative. It adds characterisations such as "has shown a pattern of disengagement" or "struggles with accountability", which read as considered but were never in the notes. It adds causes, such as "perhaps due to personal circumstances", which the manager does not know and should not guess. It adds outcomes, such as "further action may follow", which commit the manager to a process nobody has decided on. The repair is to read every sentence of the draft and keep only those that describe something the manager saw, or that state a purpose or a question the manager chose. A characterisation is replaced by the observation that led to it, or it is removed. A guessed cause is removed. An outcome is removed unless the manager has decided it with HR. The repaired draft is shorter, and that is a sign it is working, not a sign something is missing.

**Worked example.** The manager gives a model the dates of two missed reports and the finance team's two chasers, and asks for a short message to open the conversation. The model writes: "I wanted to talk about the monthly report, which has been late in March and April. This reflects a wider pattern of disengagement that I have noticed. I understand things may be difficult at home. If this continues, we may need to take further action." Reading it, the first sentence is what the manager saw. The second sentence invents a pattern and a trait. The third sentence guesses at a personal cause the manager never mentioned, and it could be hurtful and intrusive. The fourth sentence commits the manager to "further action" that nobody has decided. The repaired message reads: "I wanted to talk about the monthly report, which was sent after the deadline in March and April, and the finance team had to chase it twice in April. I would like to understand what gets in the way, and work out together what would help."

**Practice.** The learner sees the model's draft with each sentence tappable, and for each one chooses "Keep", "Replace with what I saw", or "Remove". The page then shows the repaired message.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "A manager's notes said only that a team member arrived after the 9am start on four days in the last two weeks and missed the start of two team briefings. Edit the model's draft so that it contains only what the manager saw, a purpose, and a question."

The document on screen: "I have noticed you arriving late on four days recently, and you missed the start of two briefings. You clearly do not see punctuality as important. I wonder whether you are looking for another job. This is now a disciplinary matter. Can we talk about it?"

The check looks for these parts in the edited draft:

- The observation about four late arrivals after 9am and two missed briefing starts is kept. Note if removed: "Keep what you saw. It is the part of the message the person can respond to."
- "You clearly do not see punctuality as important" is removed or replaced with an observation. Note if present: "This sentence names an attitude you inferred. Remove it, or replace it with something you saw."
- The guess about looking for another job is removed. Note if present: "This guesses at a motive that is not in your notes. Remove it."
- "This is now a disciplinary matter" is removed. Note if present: "Nobody has decided on a disciplinary process. Starting one follows your policy and the Acas Code, not a feedback message. Remove it."
- A purpose and an open question remain. Note if missing: "Say why you want to talk and ask for their view, so the conversation can begin."

A pass shows: "Your message now says what you saw, why you want to talk, and asks for their view, and the invented attitude, motive, and process are gone."

**Bridge.** The next lesson prepares the conversation itself, from the opening to the agreed next step.

#### Lesson 4: One conversation

**Id:** `one-conversation`

**Where it sits.** The learner has evidence and a clean opening. This lesson prepares one conversation from beginning to end.

**Core idea.** A prepared conversation has five parts. The purpose says in one sentence why you are meeting. The evidence is what you saw, from lesson two, usually no more than two or three examples. The question is the open question you will ask to hear the other person's view before you offer any solution. What you will listen for names the things that would change your view, such as a workload problem you did not know about, a health issue, or a process that makes the deadline impossible. The next step is what you hope to agree, written as an action and a date, with room for the other person to shape it. This preparation is for an informal conversation. If a concern may become formal, the Acas Code of Practice on disciplinary and grievance procedures, your organisation's policy, and your HR adviser govern what happens, and a model has no part in those decisions. Do not record the conversation or run an AI note-taker in it unless your organisation allows it and the other person has been told and agrees, and do not paste a transcript of the conversation into a tool afterwards.

**Worked example.** The manager prepares for the conversation about the monthly report. Purpose: "to understand what is getting in the way of the report reaching finance by the 5th, and to agree what would help". Evidence: the two late months, and the two chasers from finance in April. Question: "What happens in the week before the report is due?" What I will listen for: whether the data arrives late from another team, whether there is a clash with another deadline, and whether anything outside work is affecting them, in which case the manager will listen and point to support without asking for details. Next step: "agree one change and check in after the next report". Reading it, the preparation gives the manager a steady opening, keeps them listening before solving, and names the answers that would change their view. The model helped only to suggest the wording of the question.

**Practice.** The learner writes the five parts for their own conversation, with a line of help under each. The page shows the worked example beside theirs.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Two managers wrote the opening of the same conversation about missed briefings. Choose the one that follows the lesson."

Controls: "Opening A" and "Opening B".

Opening A: "I have noticed you are not very engaged at the moment, and I think we need to sort out your attitude to timekeeping before it becomes a bigger problem. Here is what I would like you to do."

Opening B: "I would like to talk about the start of the day. You arrived after nine on four days in the last two weeks and missed the start of two briefings. Before I say anything else, what has been happening in the mornings?"

Correct: Opening B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Opening B states the purpose and what the manager saw, then asks for the other person's view before any solution." Feedback if Opening A is chosen: "Opening A starts with inferences about engagement and attitude, gives no evidence, and moves to instructions before the person has spoken."

A pass shows: "You chose the opening with a purpose, the evidence, and a question asked before any solution."

**Bridge.** The final lesson puts all of this on one sheet, which is what appears on your record.

#### Lesson 5: The sheet

**Id:** `the-sheet`

**Where it sits.** This is the last lesson. The learner writes the preparation sheet for their own real conversation.

**Core idea.** The preparation sheet is the page the manager takes into the conversation. It holds the purpose, two or three pieces of evidence written as things the manager saw, the open question, what they will listen for, and the next step they hope to agree. It also has two lines that make the judgement visible. The first says what the model helped with, which should be preparation only, such as the structure or the wording of a question. The second says what the manager has decided and what they have not decided yet. The sheet does not name the other person, because the record will be seen by a verifier and because the learner should not need a name to show they can prepare well. It does not include anything the other person has disclosed about their health or private life. It is not a performance review and not a note for the person's file.

**Worked example.** A completed sheet reads, in outline: purpose, to understand what is getting in the way of the monthly report and agree what would help; evidence, the report was sent after the 5th in March and April, and finance chased twice in April; question, "What happens in the week before the report is due?"; listening for, late data from another team, a clashing deadline, or something outside work; next step, agree one change and check in after the next report; the model helped with the wording of the question and the order of the agenda; I have decided that this is an informal conversation, and I have not decided whether there is a performance concern until I have heard their view. Reading it, a verifier can see the manager prepared from evidence, used the model only for preparation, and kept the judgement open.

## 6. AI Adoption for Line Managers

**Slug:** `ai-adoption-for-line-managers`

**Hours and price in the catalogue:** 2.5 hours, £129.

**Promise in the catalogue:** A 1:1 standard for reviewing AI-touched work without doing it yourself.

### Who it is for

This course is for line managers whose team members have started using AI tools in their work, whether or not the organisation has a formal policy. It suits team leaders, supervisors, and middle managers in any function, and HR business partners who support them.

The learner must manage at least one person and hold regular one-to-ones. They should know which AI tools their team is allowed to use. They do not need to use AI tools heavily themselves, and the course does not assume they are an expert.

### Outcome

When they finish, the learner can hold a one-to-one about a piece of AI-touched work using a short standard: the person owns the work, can say what the tool did, has checked the facts against a source, and has kept within the organisation's limits. They can ask three questions that surface how the work was made, read a piece of work for claims that have not been checked, and return work with a question instead of rewriting it themselves. They can tell signs in the work that a habit has stuck from counts of tool activity. They leave with a one-page one-to-one standard card for their team.

### Learning objectives

1. Distinguish a review standard for AI-touched work from a rule about how much people should use the tool.
2. Ask three questions in a one-to-one that show what the tool did and what the person checked.
3. Identify sentences in AI-touched work that have been checked against a source and those that have not yet been checked.
4. Edit review comments so they return the work with a question instead of rewriting it.
5. Distinguish signs in the work that the standard has stuck from counts of activity, and produce a one-to-one standard card.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: The standard

**Id:** `the-standard`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. It sets out the standard the rest of the course uses, before any review happens.

**Core idea.** A one-to-one standard for AI-touched work is a short statement of what good looks like when a team member hands in work that a tool helped with. This course uses four parts. The work is owned, meaning the person who hands it in is responsible for every sentence and figure in it, whoever or whatever drafted it. The use is declared, meaning the person can say what they gave the tool and what the tool gave back. The facts are checked, meaning each claim, figure, date, or reference has been compared with a source the person can name. The work is within limits, meaning nothing went into the tool that the organisation does not allow, such as personal data about customers or colleagues. A standard is not a rule about how much people should use AI, such as "everyone must use the tool for three tasks a week", and it is not a ban. It is also not a way for the manager to take over the work: the manager's job is to hold the standard in the conversation, and the team member's job is to meet it.

**Worked example.** A manager drafts a team note. "From now on, please use the AI assistant for every first draft, and I will review everything that comes out of it before it goes anywhere." The manager then rewrites it. "When a tool helps with your work, you still own it. Be ready to tell me what you gave it and what it gave back. Check every figure, date, and reference against a source you can name. Keep customer and colleague details out of the tool. I will ask about this in our one-to-ones." Reading the two versions, the first is a usage rule and a promise that the manager will do all the reviewing, which will not last. The second is a standard with the four parts, and it puts the responsibility with the person doing the work.

**Practice.** The learner writes a first version of their own team's standard in four short fields, one for each part, with the worked example visible beside them. The page returns a note for any field that describes how much to use the tool rather than what good work looks like.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Two managers wrote a standard for their teams. Choose the one that follows the lesson."

Controls: "Standard A" and "Standard B".

Standard A: "Everyone should use the AI tool at least three times a week. Log each use in the tracker. I will review all AI outputs before they are sent."

Standard B: "You own anything you hand in, however it was drafted. Be ready to say what the tool did. Check every figure, name, and date against a source. Keep personal data out of the tool. We will talk about this in one-to-ones."

Correct: Standard B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Standard B says the work is owned, the use is declared, the facts are checked, and the work stays within limits." Feedback if Standard A is chosen: "Standard A sets a usage target and makes the manager review everything. It says nothing about ownership, checking, or limits, so it is not a standard for the work."

A pass shows: "You chose the standard with the four parts: owned, declared, checked, and within limits."

**Bridge.** A standard has to be held in conversation, and the next lesson gives you three questions to do that.

#### Lesson 2: Ask what the tool did

**Id:** `ask-what-the-tool-did`

**Where it sits.** The learner has a standard. This lesson gives them the questions that bring it into a one-to-one.

**Core idea.** The simplest way to hold the standard is to ask three questions about a piece of work. What did you give the tool? This shows the source and whether anything outside the limits went in. What did it give back that you changed? This shows whether the person read the output critically, because someone who changed nothing has usually not read closely. What did you check, and against what? This shows whether the facts were checked and against which source. The questions are asked with curiosity, not suspicion, and they apply equally to work that used no tool at all, because "what did you check?" is a good question for any work. They are not a test the person can fail in the moment; if the answer to the third question is "nothing yet", the next step is to agree what will be checked before the work goes out. The questions also teach the standard, because a team member who is asked them every fortnight starts preparing the answers before the one-to-one.

**Worked example.** In a one-to-one, a manager asks about a supplier comparison a team member wrote with help from a tool. Asked what they gave it, the team member says they pasted the three suppliers' public price lists and the team's requirements. Asked what they changed, they say the tool had described one supplier as offering next-day delivery, which was not in the price list, so they deleted it. Asked what they checked, they say they checked the prices against the price lists but not the contract terms, which the tool had summarised. The manager and the team member agree that the terms will be checked against the suppliers' documents before the comparison goes to the director. Reading it, the three questions found one invented claim that the person had already caught, and one unchecked section, without the manager reading the whole document.

**Practice.** The learner thinks of one piece of work a team member handed in recently and writes how they would ask the three questions in their own words, with the three questions from the idea visible as prompts.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Two managers asked about the same AI-assisted report in a one-to-one. Choose the exchange that follows the lesson."

Controls: "Exchange A" and "Exchange B".

Exchange A: "Manager: Did you use AI for this? Team member: Yes, a bit. Manager: Fine, as long as it is right. I will go through it tonight and fix anything."

Exchange B: "Manager: What did you give the tool for this report? Team member: Last quarter's sales figures and the template. Manager: What did it give back that you changed? Team member: It added a growth percentage I could not find in the figures, so I took it out. Manager: What did you check, and against what? Team member: The totals against the finance system. I have not checked the regional split yet. Manager: Let us agree you check that before it goes out on Thursday."

Correct: Exchange B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Exchange B asks the three questions, finds what was changed and what is still unchecked, and agrees a next step with the person." Feedback if Exchange A is chosen: "Exchange A asks only whether AI was used and then takes the review on to the manager. None of the three questions is asked, and the person keeps no responsibility for checking."

A pass shows: "You chose the exchange that asks what went in, what changed, and what was checked, and leaves the next step with the person."

**Bridge.** Sometimes you do need to read the work yourself, and the next lesson shows how to read it for what has not been checked.

#### Lesson 3: Review the work

**Id:** `review-the-work`

**Where it sits.** The learner can ask the three questions. This lesson teaches them to read a piece of AI-touched work quickly for the claims that still need a source.

**Core idea.** A manager does not need to verify every sentence of a team member's work. They need to find the sentences that would cause harm if they were wrong and see whether each has been checked. In this lesson a sentence is Checked against a source when it states a fact, figure, date, name, or reference and the person can point to where it came from, or has marked it with its source. A sentence is Not yet checked when it states such a fact and no source is given or known. Opinions, recommendations, and connecting sentences are neither; they are the person's judgement and are reviewed as judgement. The sentences most likely to be Not yet checked in AI-touched work are the ones that sound most finished: precise percentages, named studies, quoted policy clauses, and statements that something "has been agreed". Reading for these takes a few minutes. It is not proofreading and not rewriting, and the manager does not need to find the source themselves; they need to ask for it.

**Worked example.** A team member's briefing on a proposed change to the shift pattern says: "The current pattern has three shifts. Overtime costs rose by 18% last year. Research shows four-day weeks improve productivity by a quarter. The union has agreed in principle to a trial. I recommend a three-month trial on one site." The team member says the first figure came from the rota and the second from the finance report. Reading it, "three shifts" and "overtime costs rose by 18%" are Checked against a source. "Research shows four-day weeks improve productivity by a quarter" is Not yet checked, because no study is named and the figure sounds precise. "The union has agreed in principle" is Not yet checked, because agreement is a fact that someone must confirm. The recommendation is the team member's judgement.

**Practice.** The learner reads the worked briefing with each sentence tappable and marks each factual sentence "Checked against a source" or "Not yet checked", then writes the question they would ask about each unchecked sentence.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "The team member says the budget figure came from the finance system and the headcount from the HR report. Mark each factual sentence of their note as checked against a source or not yet checked."

Controls: "Checked against a source" and "Not yet checked".

1. "The team budget for this year is £240,000." Correct: Checked against a source. Feedback if correct: "Right. The team member says this came from the finance system." Feedback if marked "Not yet checked": "The team member named the finance system as the source of the budget figure. It has been checked."
2. "We currently have eleven people in the team." Correct: Checked against a source. Feedback if correct: "Right. The headcount came from the HR report." Feedback if marked "Not yet checked": "The HR report is the source the team member named. This sentence has been checked."
3. "Similar teams in our sector spend about 30% less on agency staff." Correct: Not yet checked. Feedback if correct: "Right. No source is named for this comparison, and it sounds precise." Feedback if marked "Checked against a source": "Where does this comparison come from? No source was named. Ask for one before it goes out."
4. "Procurement has already approved the new agency contract." Correct: Not yet checked. Feedback if correct: "Right. An approval is a fact someone must confirm, and no confirmation is mentioned." Feedback if marked "Checked against a source": "Nothing shows who in procurement approved it or when. It has not been checked."
5. "The Working Time Regulations require a rest break of 45 minutes for any shift over five hours." Correct: Not yet checked. Feedback if correct: "Right. This quotes a legal rule with no source, and a quoted rule is exactly where invented detail hides. It should be checked against GOV.UK before anyone relies on it." Feedback if marked "Checked against a source": "No source is given for this legal rule. Ask the team member to check it against GOV.UK before the note goes out."

A pass shows: "You found the sentences with no source behind them, including the comparison, the approval, and the quoted rule."

**Bridge.** Finding an unchecked sentence is tempting to fix yourself, and the next lesson shows how to hand it back instead.

#### Lesson 4: Return it with a question

**Id:** `return-it-with-a-question`

**Where it sits.** The learner can find what has not been checked. This lesson teaches them to hand the work back so the team member does the fixing.

**Core idea.** When a manager finds a problem in a team member's work, the fastest response is to fix it. Doing that every time teaches the team that the manager is the checking step, which is the opposite of the standard. Returning the work with a question means naming the sentence, saying which part of the standard it does not yet meet, and asking the person to resolve it, for example: "Where does the 30% comparison come from? If you cannot find a source, take it out." A question is not a vague comment such as "check this" or "not sure about this", because the person needs to know which sentence and which part of the standard. It is also not a rewritten paragraph in the margin. There are times when the manager should fix something directly, such as when the work is going out in ten minutes or when the error is a typing slip, and the lesson does not pretend otherwise. But the default is the question, because the question builds the habit.

**Worked example.** A manager's first comments on the shift briefing read: "Changed this to 'Research suggests shorter weeks may help some teams'. Deleted the union line. Rewrote your recommendation to be more cautious." The manager revises them: "The productivity figure has no study named. Can you find the source, or remove the figure? The union line states an agreement. Who confirmed it, and when? If nobody has, take it out. The recommendation is your call. Tell me what would make you more or less confident about it." Reading the two sets, the first rewrote the work and left the team member with nothing to learn. The second names each sentence, the part of the standard it misses, and a question, and leaves the recommendation as the team member's judgement.

**Practice.** The learner rewrites one comment from the first set as a question, using the three elements from the idea: the sentence, the part of the standard, and the question.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "These are a manager's comments on a team member's AI-assisted customer update. Edit them so that each one returns the work with a question instead of fixing it."

The document on screen: "1. I have changed the delivery date to the 14th, which I think is right. 2. Check this. 3. Rewrote the apology paragraph because it was too long. 4. The discount mentioned in paragraph three was never agreed, so I deleted it."

The check looks for these parts:

- Comment 1 asks where the delivery date came from, or asks the person to check it against a named source, instead of stating the manager's change. Note if missing: "Comment 1 still fixes the date. Ask where the date came from and what it was checked against."
- Comment 2 names a sentence or a part of the update. Note if missing: "Comment 2 does not say what to check. Name the sentence and the part of the standard it misses."
- Comment 3 returns the paragraph to the person with a question or a request, or is removed as a matter of style. Note if missing: "Comment 3 still rewrites the work. Ask the person to shorten it, or leave it if it is a matter of taste."
- Comment 4 names the discount sentence and asks the person to remove it or confirm who agreed it. Note if missing: "Comment 4 still deletes the sentence for them. Name the discount and ask who agreed it."
- No comment contains rewritten text of the update. Note if present: "One comment still contains your rewrite of their text. Replace it with a question."

A pass shows: "Each comment now names the sentence and asks a question, and the work stays with the person who owns it."

**Bridge.** After a few weeks of this, you need to know whether the standard has taken hold, and the next lesson says what to look for.

#### Lesson 5: Signs it stuck

**Id:** `signs-it-stuck`

**Where it sits.** The learner can hold the standard in one-to-ones. This lesson helps them tell whether it has become a habit.

**Core idea.** A habit shows itself in the work, not in the tool's statistics. In this lesson a Sign in the work is something a manager can see in what the team hands in or says, such as a team member naming the source of a figure without being asked, a note that says which parts were drafted with a tool and which were checked, a factual error caught before the work reaches the manager, or a question from a team member about whether something can go into the tool. An Activity count is a number about the tool itself, such as logins, prompts sent, licences used, or hours saved as estimated by the tool. Activity counts can be useful for knowing whether people have access, but they cannot show whether the work is owned, declared, checked, and within limits. A high number of prompts can even be a warning, if the work that comes out still has unchecked figures. Signs in the work take a few weeks to appear, so the manager should look for them after at least two or three one-to-ones.

**Worked example.** After six weeks, a manager's notes on the team include: "Usage dashboard shows 340 prompts this month, up from 120." "One team member's supplier note came with a line saying the prices were checked against the price lists on Monday." "Two people asked whether they could paste a customer email into the tool." "The tool reports 22 hours saved." "No unchecked figures in the last three reports." Reading them with the two labels, the prompt count and the hours saved are Activity counts. The line about checked prices, the questions about pasting customer email, and the absence of unchecked figures are Signs in the work. The manager uses the signs to judge whether the standard has stuck, and treats the counts as background.

**Practice.** The learner writes three things they could look for in their own team's work in the coming weeks, and the page asks them to confirm that each could be seen in the work rather than on a dashboard.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each item from a manager's six-week review as a sign in the work or an activity count."

Controls: "Sign in the work" and "Activity count".

1. "The tool dashboard shows the team sent 500 prompts last month." Correct: Activity count. Feedback if correct: "Right. It counts use of the tool and says nothing about whether the work was checked." Feedback if marked "Sign in the work": "Prompts sent is a number about the tool. It is an activity count."
2. "A team member's briefing marked which figures came from the finance system." Correct: Sign in the work. Feedback if correct: "Right. The person declared their sources without being asked, which is the standard in the work." Feedback if marked "Activity count": "This is something the manager saw in the work itself. 3. "Eight of nine licences were used at least once." Correct: Activity count. Feedback if correct: "Right. Licence use shows access, not whether the work met the standard." Feedback if marked "Sign in the work": "A licence being used is about the tool. It is an activity count."
4. "A team member caught an invented date in a draft before sending it to the manager." Correct: Sign in the work. Feedback if correct: "Right. The facts were checked by the person who owns the work." Feedback if marked "Activity count": "This describes checking in the work. It is a sign in the work."
5. "A new starter asked whether a candidate's CV could go into the tool." Correct: Sign in the work. Feedback if correct: "Right. Asking about limits before pasting shows the fourth part of the standard in action." Feedback if marked "Activity count": "This is a behaviour, not a number from the tool. It is a sign in the work."

A pass shows: "You looked for the standard in the work people handed in, not in the tool's numbers."

## 7. Building a Workforce Skills Plan

**Slug:** `building-a-workforce-skills-plan`

**Promise in the catalogue:** The few skills the next two quarters need, by role, on one page.

### Who it is for

This course is for HR business partners, L&D leads, workforce planners, and heads of function who have been asked for a skills plan, often in response to new technology, and who want one that leads to action rather than a long list of competencies.

The learner must know the roles in the part of the organisation they plan for, and must be able to find out what changes are already decided for the next six months, such as a system going live, a process changing, or a service being added. They do not need workforce analytics software or a skills taxonomy.

### Outcome

When they finish, the learner can build a skills plan for the next two quarters that starts from changes to the work that have actually been decided, names for each affected role no more than three skills written as observable work, and says what the organisation will not train and what it will do instead. They can defend each line of the plan by pointing to the change it responds to. They leave with a one-page plan for their area.

### Learning objectives

1. Distinguish a change to the work that can be pointed to from a trend that is being guessed at.
2. Write a skill for a role as observable work tied to a specific change.
3. Decide whether a skills gap should be closed by training or solved another way, and give the reason.
4. Edit a long list of skills down to the few that matter, using stated criteria.
5. Produce a one-page skills plan for two quarters.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: The work that is changing

**Id:** `the-work-that-is-changing`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. It sets the starting point of the plan, which is the work, not a list of skills.

**Core idea.** A skills plan for two quarters should start from changes to the work that are already decided or under way. In this lesson, A change we can point to is one with evidence inside the organisation: a system with a go-live date, a process redesign that has been signed off, a new service with a launch plan, a regulation with a known date that applies to the organisation, or a role being created or removed. A trend we are guessing at is a general claim about the future of work, such as "AI will transform every job" or "everyone will need data skills", which may be true in some form but does not tell you which role changes, how, or when. Reports such as the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report are useful background for thinking about direction over several years, but a plan for the next two quarters that is built on them alone will be vague and hard to act on. Starting from changes you can point to keeps the plan short, keeps it honest, and makes each line defensible. It is not a refusal to think ahead; it is a decision to plan for what is known and to review the plan as more becomes known.

**Worked example.** An HR business partner for a claims department collects six statements for the plan. "The new claims system goes live on 1 March." "Generative AI is reshaping knowledge work." "The fraud referral process changes in April, with a new checklist signed off by the risk committee." "Customers increasingly expect digital service." "Two team leader roles are being merged into one from May." "Everyone will need to be data literate." Marked with the two labels, the new system, the fraud referral change, and the merged team leader role are changes we can point to, each with a date and an owner. The statements about generative AI, digital expectations, and data literacy are trends we are guessing at. They may prompt useful questions, but none tells the partner which role must do what by when.

**Practice.** The learner lists five changes that people in their area have mentioned for the coming six months and marks each "A change we can point to" or "A trend we are guessing at". For each change they can point to, they write the evidence and the date.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "These statements were collected for a skills plan in a hospital's outpatient administration team. Mark each as a change we can point to or a trend we are guessing at."

Controls: "A change we can point to" and "A trend we are guessing at".

1. "The new appointment booking system replaces the current one on 3 June, according to the approved project plan." Correct: A change we can point to. Feedback if correct: "Right. It has a date and an approved plan behind it." Feedback if marked "A trend we are guessing at": "This names a system, a date, and an approved plan. It is a change we can point to."
2. "Automation will remove most administrative work within a few years." Correct: A trend we are guessing at. Feedback if correct: "Right. It names no system, no role, and no date inside the organisation." Feedback if marked "A change we can point to": "What would you point to? There is no system, date, or decision here. It is a trend we are guessing at."
3. "From September, patient letters will be sent by text message first, as agreed by the operations board." Correct: A change we can point to. Feedback if correct: "Right. It has a date and a decision by a named board." Feedback if marked "A trend we are guessing at": "The operations board agreed this change with a date. It is a change we can point to."
4. "Patients will expect more self-service in future." Correct: A trend we are guessing at. Feedback if correct: "Right. It may be true, but it does not say what changes for which role, or when." Feedback if marked "A change we can point to": "Nothing here has been decided inside the organisation. It is a trend we are guessing at."
5. "The team is taking on referral triage for two more clinics from July, under the signed service agreement." Correct: A change we can point to. Feedback if correct: "Right. It names the new work, the date, and the agreement." Feedback if marked "A trend we are guessing at": "A signed agreement with a date is evidence. It is a change we can point to."

A pass shows: "You built the plan on changes with a date and a decision behind them, and set the general trends aside."

**Bridge.** Each change affects particular roles, and the next lesson writes the skills those roles need.

#### Lesson 2: Skills by role

**Id:** `skills-by-role`

**Where it sits.** The learner has the changes. This lesson turns each change into the skills a specific role will need.

**Core idea.** A skill in a skills plan is something a person in a named role will need to do at work that someone could see, written with an action verb and tied to the change that creates the need. For example, "book, move, and cancel an appointment in the new system, including for a patient with two linked referrals" is a skill for booking clerks tied to the system change. A skill is not a topic such as "digital skills", not a trait such as "adaptability", and not a course title such as "Introduction to the new system", because none of these says what the person must be able to do. Writing skills this way has two benefits. It makes it clear which roles are affected and which are not, so the plan does not ask everyone to learn everything. It also makes it possible to check later whether the skill is there, which the course on measuring training covers. The CIPD Profession Map and many competency frameworks describe capabilities at a higher level, and they are useful for career development, but a two-quarter plan needs the level of the work.

**Worked example.** For the change "patient letters sent by text message first from September", the HR partner writes two rows. "Role: outpatient administrators. Skill: check a patient's contact preferences and mobile number before sending, and switch to a posted letter when the patient has asked for one. Change: text-first letters from September." "Role: team leaders. Skill: run the weekly report of failed text messages and assign each to an administrator for follow-up. Change: text-first letters from September." Reading it, each row names a role, a skill that can be seen, and the change behind it. The clinicians are not in the rows, because this change does not alter their work, and saying so is part of the plan.

**Practice.** The learner takes one change from their list in lesson one and writes the affected roles and one skill for each, with the three tests visible: a named role, an action someone could see, and the change it comes from.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Two rows were written for the change 'the new claims system goes live on 1 March'. Choose the row that follows the lesson."

Controls: "Row A" and "Row B".

Row A: "Role: all staff. Skill: digital confidence and adaptability. Change: digital transformation."

Row B: "Role: claims handlers. Skill: register a new claim in the new system and attach the customer's evidence, including when documents arrive after registration. Change: new claims system live from 1 March."

Correct: Row B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Row B names the role, a skill someone could see, and the dated change it comes from." Feedback if Row A is chosen: "Row A names everyone, describes traits no one can observe, and points to a general trend instead of the system change."

A pass shows: "You chose the row with a named role, a skill that can be seen, and the change that creates the need."

**Bridge.** Not every skill in the list should be met by training, and the next lesson decides which should not.

#### Lesson 3: What you will not train

**Id:** `what-you-will-not-train`

**Where it sits.** The learner has skills by role. This lesson asks, for each one, whether training is the right answer.

**Core idea.** Training is one way to close a gap between what a role needs and what people can do, and it is often not the cheapest or the quickest. In this lesson a gap is marked Train when people in the role will need to do the work themselves, often enough that they must be able to do it without help, and when a short, practical programme can build the skill before the change arrives. A gap is marked Solve another way when a different response fits better. The work can be redesigned, so the difficult step is removed or handled by the system. A job aid or checklist can be enough for a task that is done rarely. The skill can be hired in or bought as a service when it is specialist and needed by only one or two people. Or the organisation can accept the gap for now, when the change is not yet certain. Saying what you will not train is not a sign that the plan is thin. It is what keeps the plan to one page and keeps training budgets for the gaps that need them. Each "Solve another way" line should say which other way and who owns it.

**Worked example.** For the new claims system, the HR partner lists four gaps. Claims handlers must register claims and attach evidence every day. Team leaders must run a monthly audit report. One systems analyst must configure new claim types. Everyone must know how to reset their password. Marked with the two labels, registering claims is Train, because handlers do it daily and need to do it without help. The monthly audit report is Solve another way, with a one-page job aid, because it is done once a month by a few people. Configuring claim types is Solve another way, by asking the vendor to provide that service for the first six months, because it is specialist and needed by one person. Password resets are Solve another way, through the self-service guide the IT team already provides.

**Practice.** The learner takes the skills they wrote in lesson two and marks each "Train" or "Solve another way", writing the reason and, for each "Solve another way", the alternative and its owner.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "These gaps come from the outpatient administration plan. Mark each as a gap to train or a gap to solve another way."

Controls: "Train" and "Solve another way".

1. "Administrators will book, move, and cancel appointments in the new system many times a day from June." Correct: Train. Feedback if correct: "Right. The work is frequent and must be done without help, so a short practical programme before June is the right response." Feedback if marked "Solve another way": "This is the core daily work of the role from June. People must be able to do it themselves, so train."
2. "Once a quarter, one team leader exports a waiting list report for the operations board." Correct: Solve another way. Feedback if correct: "Right. A job aid for a quarterly task done by one person is quicker and lasts longer than training." Feedback if marked "Train": "This is a quarterly task for one person. A job aid, owned by the team leader, will do the job."
3. "The new system needs someone to set up clinic templates, which requires the vendor's specialist configuration training." Correct: Solve another way. Feedback if correct: "Right. This is specialist and may be best bought from the vendor for the first months, or given to one person as part of their role." Feedback if marked "Train": "This is a specialist task needed by very few people. Solve it another way, and name the owner."
4. "The text-first process sends a failed-message report that team leaders must act on each week." Correct: Train. Feedback if correct: "Right. Team leaders act on it every week, and acting well needs practice with real reports." Feedback if marked "Solve another way": "This weekly task is part of the team leader's job from September, and they must do it without help. Train."
5. "Administrators must know where to find the new system's help pages." Correct: Solve another way. Feedback if correct: "Right. A link in the system and a line in the team briefing will do this without a programme." Feedback if marked "Train": "Finding help pages does not need a programme. A link and a mention in the briefing will solve it another way."

A pass shows: "You kept training for the frequent work people must do themselves, and solved the rare, specialist, and simple gaps another way."

**Bridge.** Even the gaps to train can be too many for one page, and the next lesson chooses the few that matter most.

#### Lesson 4: The few that matter

**Id:** `the-few-that-matter`

**Where it sits.** The learner has decided what to train. This lesson cuts the list to the few skills per role that the plan can actually deliver.

**Core idea.** A skills plan that lists nine skills for a role will usually deliver none of them well. This course asks for no more than three skills per role in a two-quarter plan, chosen with four questions. What happens if the skill is missing on the day of the change: does work stop, do customers or patients suffer, or is it only slower? How many people in the role need it? How long does it take someone to become competent, and does that fit before the change? How certain is the change? A skill that scores high on consequence and certainty, and that many people need, goes on the plan. A skill that matters less, or whose change is uncertain, goes to a "later" list with a review date. Cutting is not the same as deciding a skill is unimportant; it is deciding what this plan will deliver first. Each cut should have a one-sentence reason drawn from the four questions, so the decision can be explained to the managers who asked for it.

**Worked example.** A team leader asks for nine skills for claims handlers before the new system goes live. The HR partner applies the four questions. Registering a claim and attaching evidence stops work if missing and is needed by every handler, so it stays. Searching for an existing claim is needed daily by everyone, so it stays. Recording a fraud referral with the new checklist has a serious consequence if missing and a signed-off date, so it stays. Creating custom reports is needed by few, so it goes to the later list. Advanced keyboard shortcuts only make work faster, so they are cut. A general "customer empathy" skill is not tied to this change, so it is cut from this plan with a note that it belongs in the service standards work. The remaining three skills are cut or moved with one sentence each.

**Practice.** The learner takes the "Train" skills for one role from lesson three and answers the four questions for each in short fields, then chooses up to three.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "This draft plan lists seven skills for booking clerks before the new appointment system goes live on 3 June. Edit it to keep no more than three, and give a one-sentence reason for each skill you cut or move, drawn from the four questions."

The document on screen lists seven skills: "1. Book, move, and cancel an appointment in the new system. 2. Customise the system's colour scheme. 3. Find a patient's existing appointments and linked referrals. 4. Run the monthly capacity report. 5. Send a text reminder and switch to a letter when the patient has asked. 6. Understand the history of the booking system project. 7. Be resilient during change."

The check looks for these parts:

- Skills 1 and 3 are kept. Note if either is cut: "This is daily work that stops if the skill is missing on 3 June. Keep it."
- No more than three skills are kept. Note if more remain: "Keep no more than three skills for this role, and move the rest to the later list with a reason."
- Skill 2 is cut, with a reason about consequence. Note if kept or if the reason is missing: "Nothing stops if the colour scheme is unchanged. Cut it and say so."
- Skill 6 and skill 7 are cut, with a reason saying they are not observable skills tied to the change. Note if kept or if the reason is missing: "This is not something a clerk does in the new system. Cut it and give the reason."
- Skill 4 is cut or moved to the later list, with a reason about how many people need it or how often it is done. Note if the reason is missing: "Say why this report can wait, using how many people need it or how often it is run."
- Every cut or moved skill has a reason. Note if any is missing: "Add a one-sentence reason for this change, drawn from the four questions."

A pass shows: "Your plan keeps the skills that stop work if missing and that every clerk needs, and each cut has a reason."

**Bridge.** The final lesson puts the changes, the skills, what you will not train, and the owners on one page.

#### Lesson 5: The plan

**Id:** `the-plan`

**Where it sits.** This is the last lesson. The learner writes the skills plan for their area, and that plan appears on their record.

**Core idea.** The plan is one page covering the next two quarters. It lists the changes the plan responds to, each with its evidence and date. For each affected role, it lists up to three skills, written as observable work, with the change each comes from, the measure that will build it, who owns it, and when it will be done relative to the change. It lists what the organisation will not train, with the other way it will solve the gap and who owns that. It names a later list of skills that were considered and deferred. It ends with a review date, which should fall before the second quarter begins, and with the name of the role that owns the plan. The plan is not a strategy document, not a competency framework, and not a training catalogue. It is a set of decisions a manager can read in two minutes and hold the organisation to.

**Worked example.** A plan for the outpatient administration team lists three changes: the booking system on 3 June, text-first letters in September, and referral triage for two more clinics in July. Booking clerks have two skills, both tied to the booking system, built through practice sessions in the training environment in May and owned by the service manager. Team leaders have one skill, handling the failed-message report, built through two supervised weeks in September and owned by the operations lead. Triage administrators have two skills tied to the new clinics, built by shadowing in June. Not trained: the quarterly waiting list report, solved with a job aid owned by the team leader, and template configuration, bought from the vendor for six months. Later list: custom reporting, reviewed in October. The review date is 1 September, and the owner is the HR business partner.

**Practice.** The learner gathers their answers from lessons one to four and checks that every skill still points to a change from lesson one.

**Check.** Kind: Write the artefact.

Question: "Write the skills plan for your area for the next two quarters. A manager should be able to read it in two minutes and know what will happen, for whom, and when."

Fields: "Changes this plan responds to, with evidence and date", "Skills by role", with for each role up to three skills, each with the change, the measure, the owner, and when. "What we will not train, how we will solve it instead, and who owns that". "Later list, with a review date". "Plan owner and review date". "Your name as it should appear on the record".

**Bridge.** There is no next lesson. The learner signs the record and can open it from My courses.

### Artefact and record

The learner produces a one-page skills plan for two quarters. The signed record names the learner, the course title, and the date, and shows the plan as written. It states that it is a record of work completed on an Experrt self-serve course.

### How learning is validated

Every check must pass before the artefact opens. For the artefact, a pass requires all of the following:

- At least one change, and every change has evidence and a date. A change without a date produces the note: "Give the date, or move this to the later list until one is known."
- At least two roles, and no role has more than three skills.
- Every skill begins with an action verb, names work in the role, and points to a listed change. A skill that is a single noun or a trait, such as "adaptability", "resilience", or "digital skills", produces the note: "Write this as something a person in the role will do that someone could see."
- Every skill has a measure, an owner by role, and a time.
- At least one gap is listed under "What we will not train", with the other way and its owner.
- The plan has an owner and a review date.
- The plan contains no names of individuals other than the signature, no email address, no telephone number, and no pay figures.

## 8. HR Operations with AI

**Slug:** `hr-operations-with-ai`

**Hours and price in the catalogue:** 2 hours, £99.

**Promise in the catalogue:** One HR operation with a checked AI step, written down.

### Who it is for

This course is for HR administrators, people operations staff, HR shared service teams, and HR advisers who handle repeating operational work such as offer letters, contract changes, onboarding, policy queries, and leaver processes. It suits anyone who has been asked to "use AI to speed up the admin" and wants to do it without an error reaching an employee.

The learner must own or regularly carry out at least one repeating HR operation and know its steps. They must know which AI tool their organisation has approved and what it may be used for. They do not need automation or technical skills.

### Outcome

When they finish, the learner can choose one repeating HR operation, map its steps, and decide which single step a model may take and which steps a person keeps. They can add a check step that names who checks what against which source, and they can write a failure note that says how an error would be caught and who is told. They leave with a written workflow for that operation, with a checked AI step, that a colleague could follow.

### Learning objectives

1. Choose a repeating HR operation that has defined inputs, a defined output, and a known source of truth.
2. Classify each step of the operation as one a model may take or one a person keeps.
3. Edit a workflow so that the step after the model's step is a check that names the checker, what is checked, and the source.
4. Distinguish a failure note that says how an error is caught from one that only hopes it will not happen.
5. Produce a written workflow for one operation with a checked AI step.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: One operation

**Id:** `one-operation`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. It chooses the operation the whole course will work on.

**Core idea.** An HR operation is a piece of work that repeats, follows steps, and produces a defined output, such as a contract variation letter after a change of hours, a reply to a common policy query, an onboarding pack for a new starter, or a leaver checklist. A good operation for a first AI step has three features. Its inputs are defined, so you know exactly what information arrives each time, such as the approved change form. Its output is defined, so you know what a correct result looks like, such as the letter template. It has a source of truth, meaning a place where the correct facts can be checked, such as the HR system, the policy, or the signed form. An operation is not a whole process area, such as "recruitment" or "employee relations", and it is not a one-off project. It is also not a case, such as a grievance or an investigation, where each instance turns on judgement about particular people; those do not make a good first AI step, whatever their volume.

**Worked example.** An HR administrator considers three candidates. The first is "handle employee relations queries", which is a whole area with no defined output. The second is "produce the contract variation letter when a manager's approved change form arrives", which has a form as its input, a template as its output, and the HR system and the form as its source of truth. The third is "decide whether flexible working requests should be approved", which is a judgement about particular people. Reading them against the three features, only the second is a good first operation. It happens every week, and when the letter is wrong, the error can be found by comparing the letter with the form.

**Practice.** The learner writes the name of one operation they carry out, then answers three prompts: what arrives each time, what a correct output looks like, and where the correct facts can be checked. The page returns a note for any prompt they cannot answer.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Two HR advisers describe the operation they want to use for a first AI step. Choose the one that follows the lesson."

Controls: "Operation A" and "Operation B".

Operation A: "Improving how we manage absence across the business."

Operation B: "Sending the right-to-work document request to each new starter. The input is the new starter record in the HR system. The output is the standard request email with the list of acceptable documents. The source of truth is the Home Office employer guide and our starter record."

Correct: Operation B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Operation B has a defined input, a defined output, and a source of truth to check against." Feedback if Operation A is chosen: "Operation A is a whole area of work with no defined input, output, or source. Choose one repeating piece of work inside it."

A pass shows: "You chose the operation with a defined input, a defined output, and a source of truth."

**Bridge.** With the operation chosen, the next lesson decides which of its steps a model may take.

#### Lesson 2: The step a model may take

**Id:** `the-step-a-model-may-take`

**Where it sits.** The learner has one operation. This lesson looks at its steps one by one and decides where a model may help.

**Core idea.** In this lesson a step is marked A model may take it when three conditions hold: the input to the step is information your organisation allows in the approved tool, the output is text that a person will read before it goes anywhere, and an error in the output could be caught by comparing it with the source of truth. A step is marked A person keeps it when any of these fails, and also when the step is a decision, a change to a record, or an action with a legal deadline or a legal effect. In most HR operations, the steps a person keeps include approving a change, updating the HR or payroll system, confirming right to work, and sending anything that changes a person's terms. The step a model may take is usually drafting: turning the approved facts into the words of a letter, summarising a long policy into a reply, or filling a template. This is not a statement that a model is unreliable at everything else. It is a line drawn so that every error a model could make lands in front of a person before it lands on an employee.

**Worked example.** The contract variation operation has six steps. The manager's change form arrives. An HR administrator checks the form has been approved by the budget holder. The administrator drafts the variation letter. The administrator updates the hours and pay in the HR system. The letter is sent to the employee for signature. The signed letter is filed. Marked with the two labels, drafting the letter is the step a model may take, provided the form's details are allowed in the approved tool: its output is a draft that will be read, and any error can be caught by comparing the letter with the form. Checking approval is a person keeps it, because it is a decision about whether the change may go ahead. Updating the HR system is a person keeps it, because it changes a record that drives pay. Sending and filing are a person keeps it, because sending changes the person's terms.

**Practice.** The learner lists the steps of their own operation, one per field, and marks each "A model may take it" or "A person keeps it", with the three conditions visible above the fields.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "These are the steps of a leaver process. Mark each as a step a model may take or a step a person keeps."

Controls: "A model may take it" and "A person keeps it".

1. "Draft the acknowledgement of resignation letter from the template, using the leaving date on the manager's form." Correct: A model may take it. Feedback if correct: "Right. The output is a draft that will be read, and the date can be checked against the form." Feedback if marked "A person keeps it": "This is drafting from a template with a date that can be checked against the form. A model may take it."
2. "Calculate the final holiday pay owed." Correct: A person keeps it. Feedback if correct: "Right. Final pay has a legal effect and must come from the payroll system and the policy." Feedback if marked "A model may take it": "A holiday pay calculation changes what the person is paid. A person keeps it, using the payroll system."
3. "End the employee's system access on their last day." Correct: A person keeps it. Feedback if correct: "Right. Changing access is an action on a system, not a draft." Feedback if marked "A model may take it": "This is an action on a record, not a piece of text to read. A person keeps it."
4. "Summarise the leaver policy into a short list of what the leaver needs to return." Correct: A model may take it. Feedback if correct: "Right. The output is text a person reads, and every item can be checked against the policy." Feedback if marked "A person keeps it": "This is a summary of policy text that you will check against the policy. A model may take it."
5. "Decide whether to pay the leaver in lieu of notice." Correct: A person keeps it. Feedback if correct: "Right. It is a decision with a contractual effect." Feedback if marked "A model may take it": "Paying in lieu of notice is a decision about the person's contract. A person keeps it."

A pass shows: "You gave the model the drafting and the summary, and kept the pay, the access, and the decision with a person."

**Bridge.** A model's step is safe only if the next step checks it, and the next lesson writes that check.

#### Lesson 3: The step a person keeps

**Id:** `the-step-a-person-keeps`

**Where it sits.** The learner knows which step a model may take. This lesson adds the check that comes straight after it.

**Core idea.** Every step a model takes in an HR operation is followed by a check step that a person keeps. A check step has three parts. The checker is named by role, such as "the HR administrator who prepared the form". What is checked is named precisely, such as "every date, number of hours, rate of pay, and job title in the letter". The source is named, such as "the approved change form and the HR system record". A check is not "review the letter", because that does not say what to look at or against what. It is also not "check for errors", because the errors a model makes in HR documents are often plausible, such as a notice period taken from a different contract, a start date one day off, or a clause the template never contained. The check exists to catch exactly these, so it must name them. A good check also says what happens when a mismatch is found: the letter is corrected from the source, not from memory, and the correction is noted.

**Worked example.** A draft workflow reads: "Step 3: the model drafts the variation letter from the change form. Step 4: the letter is sent." The administrator runs it on a test case, and the model's letter states a notice period of three months, which is not in the template and not in the form; the employee's contract says one month. Reading it, the workflow had no check step, so the invented notice period would have reached the employee. The revised workflow adds: "Step 4: the HR administrator compares every date, number of hours, rate of pay, job title, and notice period in the letter with the approved form and the HR system record. Any mismatch is corrected from the form or the system, and noted on the case. Step 5: the letter is sent."

**Practice.** The learner writes the check step for the model's step in their own operation, in three fields: the checker, what is checked, and the source. The page shows the worked example's check beside theirs.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "This workflow for new starter offer letters has a model step but no proper check. Edit it so that the step after the model's step names the checker, what is checked, and the source."

The document on screen: "Step 1: the hiring manager's approved offer form arrives. Step 2: the model drafts the offer letter from the form and the template. Step 3: someone has a look at it. Step 4: the letter is sent to the candidate."

The check looks for these parts in step 3:

- A checker named by role. Note if missing: "Name the role that checks the letter, such as the HR administrator."
- What is checked, including at least salary, start date, job title, and hours. Note if missing: "Name the facts the check compares, such as the salary, start date, job title, and hours."
- The source, naming the approved offer form or the template. Note if missing: "Say what the letter is checked against, such as the approved offer form."
- What happens when a mismatch is found. Note if missing: "Say what the checker does if a detail does not match, such as correcting it from the form and noting it."
- The phrase "someone has a look at it" is gone. Note if present: "Having a look does not say who checks what against which source. Replace it."

A pass shows: "Your check names who checks, what they compare, the source, and what happens if something does not match."

**Bridge.** Checks can still miss things, and the next lesson writes down what happens when an error gets through.

#### Lesson 4: When it goes wrong

**Id:** `when-it-goes-wrong`

**Where it sits.** The workflow now has a checked AI step. This lesson prepares for the day an error gets past the check.

**Core idea.** A failure note is a short part of the workflow that says what an error from the model's step would look like, how it would be caught if the check missed it, who would be told, and what would be done. In this lesson a sentence Says how the error is caught when it names a specific error, a way of noticing it, or an action and a person responsible. A sentence Hopes the error will not happen when it expresses confidence, such as "the tool is very accurate" or "errors are unlikely", or relies on care without saying what care means. Hoping is not a plan, and a failure note made of hopes will not help the colleague who finds a wrong letter on a Friday afternoon. A good failure note also says who to tell if personal data went into the tool by mistake, because that is a possible data breach and your organisation's data protection lead must assess it; under UK data protection law, a reportable breach must be notified to the ICO within 72 hours of the organisation becoming aware of it.

**Worked example.** A failure note for the contract variation workflow reads: "The model may put a notice period, a pay rate, or a date in the letter that does not match the form. The check in step 4 compares each of these with the form and the HR system. If a wrong letter is sent, the employee's signed copy will not match the HR system at filing, and the administrator who files it raises this with the HR manager on the same day. The HR manager writes to the employee to correct it. If anyone pastes more than the approved fields into the tool, they tell the data protection lead the same day." Reading it, every sentence names an error, a way of catching it, or a person and an action.

**Practice.** The learner writes the first sentence of a failure note for their own operation, naming the most likely error from the model's step, with the worked note visible.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each sentence of this failure note for an offer letter workflow as one that says how the error is caught or one that hopes the error will not happen."

Controls: "Says how the error is caught" and "Hopes the error will not happen".

1. "The model may put a salary in the letter that differs from the approved offer form." Correct: Says how the error is caught. Feedback if correct: "Right. It names the specific error to look for." ### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: What counts

**Id:** `what-counts`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. Before anyone writes a red-list, they need to know what counts as personal data, which is broader than most people expect.

**Core idea.** Under UK data protection law, personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable living person. A person is identifiable if they can be identified directly, for example by name, or indirectly, by combining information such as a job title, a location, a date, and a description of an event. In this lesson information is Personal data when it relates to a person who is identified or who could reasonably be identified from it, alone or with other information someone is likely to have. Information is Not personal data when it cannot reasonably be linked to any individual, such as policy text, a headcount for a large team, or a theme that has been drawn from many responses with nothing specific left in it. Removing a name does not make information anonymous, particularly in a small team, where "the only part-time analyst in the Leeds office" is as clear as a name. Pseudonymised information, where names are replaced with codes but someone could still re-identify the person, is still personal data. The ICO's guidance on what personal data is, and its guidance on anonymisation, both make these points, and they are the reason this lesson comes first.

**Worked example.** A manager writes: "One of my team in the Bristol office has been off a lot lately. She is our only payroll specialist, and she told me it is to do with a family situation. How should I handle the next conversation?" The manager believes they have removed the personal data because there is no name. Reading it against the idea, the person is identifiable: the office, the role, and the fact that she is the only payroll specialist point to one person. The absence and the family situation are therefore personal data about her, and the absence may relate to her health or her family's. The question "how should I handle the next conversation" is not personal data, and the manager can get help with it without any of the rest.

**Practice.** The learner writes three pieces of information they have seen in a prompt, a message, or a request in their own work, with any names already removed, and marks each "Personal data" or "Not personal data". For each one they mark as not personal data, the page asks: "Could someone in your organisation work out who this is about?"

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each piece of information as personal data or not personal data. None of them contains a name."

Controls: "Personal data" and "Not personal data".

1. "Our sickness absence policy triggers a review after five days in a rolling twelve months." Correct: Not personal data. Feedback if correct: "Right. Policy text relates to no individual." Feedback if marked "Personal data": "This is a sentence from a policy. It relates to no one, so it is not personal data."
2. "The newest member of the three-person legal team asked for a later start time because of her childcare." Correct: Personal data. Feedback if correct: "Right. In a team of three, 'the newest member' identifies one person, and the reason is about her private life." Feedback if marked "Not personal data": "There is no name, but in a team of three the newest member is one person. This is personal data."
3. "Across the 400 people who answered the survey, workload was the most common theme." Correct: Not personal data. Feedback if correct: "Right. A theme from four hundred responses cannot reasonably be linked to anyone." Feedback if marked "Personal data": "A theme drawn from four hundred responses does not point to any person. It is not personal data."
4. "Employee 4471 has two live warnings." Correct: Personal data. Feedback if correct: "Right. A staff number is a code, and the HR system links it straight back to a person." Feedback if marked "Not personal data": "Replacing a name with a staff number is pseudonymisation. Someone can re-identify the person, so it is personal data."
5. "The warehouse night shift supervisor was taken to hospital after an accident on Tuesday." Correct: Personal data. Feedback if correct: "Right. A role, a shift, and a date identify one person, and the information is about their health." A pass shows: "You recognised personal data even without a name, and let the policy and the survey theme stand as not personal data."

**Bridge.** Some personal data is more harmful than the rest, and the next lesson writes the red-list for it.

#### Lesson 2: The red-list

**Id:** `the-red-list`

**Where it sits.** The learner can recognise personal data. This lesson separates the people data that never goes into an AI tool from the data that may go into an approved tool for an approved purpose.

**Core idea.** A red-list is the list of kinds of people data that never go into an AI tool unless the organisation has set up a specific route for that data and purpose, with a data protection impact assessment and the data protection lead's agreement. It starts with special category data under the UK GDPR: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data used for identification, health, sex life, and sexual orientation. It adds criminal offence data. It then adds kinds of data that are not special category in law but cause serious harm in HR if exposed: grievance, disciplinary, and investigation files; individual pay, bank details, and National Insurance numbers; identity documents and immigration status; information about children; and anything connected with legal advice or a live tribunal claim. Information that is not on the red-list Can go in the approved tool when the tool is one your organisation has approved, the purpose is one it has approved, and only the information the task needs goes in. Can go in the approved tool is not a general permission, and a public consumer tool that your organisation has not approved is not the approved tool, however convenient it is. The red-list does not replace your organisation's own policy; where your organisation's list is stricter, it wins.

**Worked example.** An HR adviser receives four requests from managers in a week. One wants to paste a list of their team's job titles and objectives into the approved assistant to draft a development plan template. One wants to paste a staff member's occupational health report to get a summary. One wants to paste a spreadsheet of salaries to ask which roles are paid below the median. One wants to paste the anonymised themes from a team survey to draft a briefing. Read against the idea, the job titles and objectives can go in the approved tool for drafting a template, as long as only what the template needs goes in. The occupational health report is red-list, because it is health data. The salary spreadsheet is red-list, because it is individual pay, and pay analysis goes through the reward team's approved route. The anonymised survey themes can go in the approved tool.

**Practice.** The learner writes the first draft of their red-list, one kind of data per line, with a reason. The three groups from the idea stay visible, and the page notes if the learner's list has no line from one of them.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each item that a manager wants to put into the organisation's approved AI assistant as red-list or as able to go in the approved tool."

Controls: "Red-list" and "Can go in the approved tool".

1. "The approved job description for a team leader role, to draft interview questions." Correct: Can go in the approved tool. Feedback if correct: "Right. It is approved text, it identifies no one, and the purpose is drafting." Feedback if marked "Red-list": "A job description is not about any person. It can go in the approved tool for drafting."
2. "A team member's fit note from their GP, to help plan adjustments." Correct: Red-list. Feedback if correct: "Right. A fit note is health data about a named person." Feedback if marked "Can go in the approved tool": "A fit note is health data, which is special category data. It is on the red-list."
3. "The notes from a disciplinary investigation meeting, to summarise them." Correct: Red-list. Feedback if correct: "Right. Investigation notes are on the red-list whatever the tool." Feedback if marked "Can go in the approved tool": "Disciplinary and investigation files are red-list in this lesson, even in the approved tool."
4. "A list of the team's bank holiday cover dates, with first names, to turn into a rota table." Correct: Can go in the approved tool. Feedback if correct: "Right. Names and working dates are personal data, but they are not on the red-list, and a rota is an approved purpose if your organisation allows it. Put in only what the rota needs." A pass shows: "You kept health, investigation, and identity data on the red-list, and let approved text and routine working information go into the approved tool."

**Bridge.** Often the task can still be done once the identifying detail is gone, and the next lesson shows how to remove it properly.

#### Lesson 3: Anonymised is harder than it looks

**Id:** `anonymised-is-harder-than-it-looks`

**Where it sits.** The learner has a red-list. This lesson teaches the move that most often lets a manager get help without breaking it, which is to remove what identifies a person.

**Core idea.** Anonymising a prompt means changing it so that no one could reasonably work out who it is about, using the information in it and the information people in the organisation are likely to have. That usually takes more than removing the name. You remove or generalise the role when the role is held by one or two people. You remove the location, the team size, and the dates when they narrow it down. You remove the distinctive detail of the event, keeping only the type of situation the question is about. You remove any red-list detail that the question does not need. The test is whether a colleague in the same organisation, reading the prompt, could say "I know who that is". If they could, it is not anonymous. The ICO's guidance on anonymisation describes this in terms of whether a person is identifiable by anyone reasonably likely to try, which is a stricter test than whether the name is present. Anonymising is not the same as making the question vague: the aim is to keep everything the question needs and nothing it does not.

**Worked example.** The manager's original prompt read: "One of my team in the Bristol office has been off a lot lately. She is our only payroll specialist, and she told me it is to do with a family situation. How should I handle the next conversation?" The anonymised prompt reads: "A team member has had several short absences recently and has mentioned a personal situation. I want to have a supportive conversation about attendance without asking for details they have not offered. What structure and questions would help?" Reading it, the office, the role, the gender, and the family situation have gone, and the question is sharper than before, because it says what the manager wants from the conversation.

**Practice.** The learner takes a prompt from their own work, or the example prompt on screen, and taps each phrase that could identify someone, choosing "Remove" or "Generalise" for each, then reads the result with the colleague test in mind.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "Edit this prompt so that no colleague could work out who it is about, while keeping the question the manager needs answered."

The document on screen: "I manage the Glasgow finance team, which has four people. Our longest-serving accountant, who is in her sixties, told me last week that she has been diagnosed with arthritis and is finding the keyboard painful. What adjustments could I offer?"

The check looks for these parts:

- The location and team size are removed. Note if present: "Glasgow and a team of four together point to one team. Remove them."
- "Longest-serving accountant" is removed or generalised to "a team member". Note if present: "The longest-serving accountant in a team of four is one person. Use 'a team member'."
- The age is removed. Note if present: "Age is not needed for the question and helps identify her. Remove it."
- The specific diagnosis is removed or generalised, while keeping that the person finds keyboard work painful. Note if the diagnosis remains: "The diagnosis is health data and is not needed to suggest adjustments. Keep the effect, painful keyboard use, and remove the condition." Note if the effect is also removed: "The question needs to say what the person finds difficult. Keep that keyboard work is painful."
- "Last week" is removed. Note if present: "The date helps narrow it down. Remove it."
- The question about adjustments remains. Note if missing: "Keep the question the manager needs answered."

A pass shows: "Your prompt keeps the question and the difficulty the person has, and no colleague could say who it is about."

**Bridge.** Knowing the limit is one thing, and saying it to a busy manager is another, which the next lesson covers.

#### Lesson 4: The answer

**Id:** `the-answer`

**Where it sits.** The learner can apply the red-list and anonymise a prompt. This lesson prepares the answer they give when a manager asks to paste something they should not.

**Core idea.** A good answer has three parts, in this order. It acknowledges what the manager is trying to do, because they usually have a sound aim, such as preparing well for a difficult conversation or saving time on a long document. It states the limit in one sentence with the reason, such as "health information about a colleague cannot go into the tool, because it is special category data and we have not set up a safe route for it". It then offers a route that works, such as removing what identifies the person and asking the general question, asking HR or occupational health directly, using the organisation's approved route for that data if one exists, or doing that part of the task without a tool. An answer is not a lecture on data protection law, and it is not a flat no, because a flat no teaches the manager to stop asking and start pasting. It is also not a yes with a warning, because once red-list data is in a tool, a warning cannot take it back.

**Worked example.** A manager writes to the HR adviser: "Can I paste the grievance letter I received into the AI assistant? I just want a summary so I can prepare for the meeting." The adviser replies: "It makes sense to want a clear summary before the meeting. Grievance letters cannot go into the assistant, because they are on our red-list and contain detail about the people involved that we have to protect. I can go through the letter with you tomorrow morning, and if it would help, the assistant can give you a general structure for a grievance meeting without any of the letter's details." Reading it, the reply acknowledges the aim, states the limit and the reason in one sentence, and offers two routes that work.

## 10. Measuring Whether Training Stuck

They can write signs in the work that someone other than the learner could see two weeks after the programme, ask a manager questions that bring out evidence rather than opinion, and tell a skill that is missing from a work environment that gave no chance to use it. ### Learning objectives

2. Write a sign in the work that is observable, appears in normal work, can be seen by someone other than the learner, and has a before state.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1: What you stop counting

**Id:** `what-you-stop-counting`

**Where it sits.** This is the first lesson. It sets aside the measures that cannot answer the question, before choosing the ones that can.

**Core idea.** Most training reports count things that are easy to collect: how many people enrolled, how many completed, how long they spent, how they rated the session, and how they scored on an end-of-module quiz. In this lesson these are measures that Count activity, because they describe what happened in the programme. A measure Shows the work changed when it describes something in the person's normal work after the programme that is different from before, such as the proportion of customer replies that state the next step, the number of expense claims returned for missing receipts, or whether a new manager now runs a weekly one-to-one. Models of evaluation such as Kirkpatrick's four levels and Thalheimer's Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model both separate reaction and knowledge from behaviour in the work, and both place the work higher. Activity counts are not useless: completions tell you who has had the chance to learn, and a low rating can warn you of a problem in the design. But they cannot tell a sponsor whether the programme did what it was for, and reporting them as if they could is the habit this course asks you to stop.

**Worked example.** A report on a customer service programme reads: "412 staff enrolled and 389 completed. Average satisfaction was 4.6 out of 5. The average quiz score was 86%. Two weeks later, team leaders found that 7 of the 10 replies they sampled from each adviser stated the next step, compared with 4 of 10 before the programme." Read with the two labels, the enrolment, completion, satisfaction, and quiz figures count activity. The sample of replies shows the work changed, because it compares normal work before and after, and someone other than the adviser collected it. The report has one line of evidence about the work and four about the programme.

**Practice.** The learner lists the measures in the last report they wrote or received about a programme, and marks each "Counts activity" or "Shows the work changed". The page then asks which one line they would keep if the report could hold only one.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "Mark each line from a report on a new manager programme as counting activity or showing the work changed."

Controls: "Counts activity" and "Shows the work changed".

1. "Ninety-four per cent of participants completed all four modules." Correct: Counts activity. Feedback if correct: "Right. Completion describes what happened in the programme." Feedback if marked "Shows the work changed": "Completing modules says nothing about what managers now do at work. It counts activity."
2. "Participants rated the programme 4.7 out of 5." Correct: Counts activity. Feedback if correct: "Right. A rating is a reaction to the programme." Feedback if marked "Shows the work changed": "A rating tells you how people felt about the programme, not what changed in their work. It counts activity."
3. "Six weeks on, 11 of 14 new managers were holding a fortnightly one-to-one with each team member, compared with 3 of 14 before, according to their calendars and their teams." Correct: Shows the work changed. Feedback if correct: "Right. It compares normal work before and after, and someone other than the manager can confirm it." Feedback if marked "Counts activity": "This describes what managers now do at work, compared with before. It shows the work changed."
4. "The average end-of-programme quiz score was 82%." Correct: Counts activity. Feedback if correct: "Right. A quiz score measures what people remembered on the day." Feedback if marked "Shows the work changed": "A quiz is taken inside the programme. It counts activity, not change in the work."
5. "Team members of new managers who took part reported fewer unanswered requests for leave after the programme than before, in the monthly pulse survey." Correct: Shows the work changed. Feedback if correct: "Right. It describes a change in the work, reported by people other than the managers, with a before and an after." Feedback if marked "Counts activity": "This comes from the team, about the manager's work, before and after. It shows the work changed."

A pass shows: "You separated what happened in the programme from what changed in the work."

**Bridge.** To show the work changed, you need to know what to look for, and the next lesson writes the signs.

#### Lesson 2: Signs in the work

**Id:** `signs-in-the-work`

**Where it sits.** The learner has set aside activity counts. This lesson writes the signs that will show whether the work changed.

**Core idea.** A sign in the work is a specific thing someone could see in the person's normal work after a programme, which would be different if the programme had worked. A good sign has four features. It is observable, meaning it describes an action or a piece of work rather than a feeling or an attitude. It appears in normal work, not in an exercise set up for the evaluation. Someone other than the learner can see it, such as a manager, a colleague, a customer, or a system record. It has a before state, so you know what the work looked like before the programme and can tell whether it changed. Signs are best collected two to six weeks after the programme, which is long enough for the person to have had the chance to use the skill and short enough to link any change to the programme. A sign is not a target, and it does not have to be a number; "the new manager now opens one-to-ones by asking about workload" is a sign if someone can see it. Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method uses a similar idea, looking for specific evidence of use in the work among the people who used the learning most and least.

**Worked example.** For a programme on writing customer delay updates, an L&D lead drafts two signs. The first is "advisers feel more confident about delay updates". The second is "in a sample of ten delay updates per adviser, taken from the case system two weeks after the programme, the update states the new date, the reason, and the next step; before the programme, the team leader's sample found this in about four of ten". Reading them against the four features, the first is a feeling, it is not in normal work, and only the adviser can report it. The second is observable, is in normal work, is collected by the team leader from the case system, and has a before state.

**Practice.** The learner writes one sign for their own programme, using four short fields for the four features, and the page shows the worked sign beside theirs.

**Check.** Kind: Choose.

Question: "Two signs were written for a programme on safe handling of personal data in HR. Choose the sign that follows the lesson."

Controls: "Sign A" and "Sign B".

Sign A: "Participants say they understand data protection better and feel more careful."

Sign B: "In the four weeks after the programme, the number of HR emails sent with unencrypted spreadsheets of staff data attached, as flagged by the email security system, compared with the four weeks before."

Correct: Sign B. Feedback if correct: "Right. Sign B is observable, comes from normal work, is recorded by a system rather than the learners, and has a before state." Feedback if Sign A is chosen: "Sign A is a feeling, reported only by the learners, with no before state. It could not show whether the work changed."

A pass shows: "You chose the sign that someone else could see in normal work, with a before and an after."

**Bridge.** Many signs can only be seen by the person's manager, and the next lesson prepares that conversation.

#### Lesson 3: The manager conversation

**Id:** `the-manager-conversation`

**Where it sits.** The learner has signs. This lesson prepares the questions that get evidence of those signs from managers.

**Core idea.** A manager is often the best source of evidence about whether a programme stuck, but the way the question is asked decides what comes back. A question that asks for opinion, such as "Did the training help?", "Are you happy with the programme?", or "Has their confidence improved?", invites a polite yes and tells you nothing about the work. A question that asks for evidence names the sign and asks what the manager has seen, such as "In the last two weeks, have you seen them open a one-to-one by asking about workload?", or asks them to show a piece of work, such as "Could you send me one delay update they wrote this week?". A good set of manager questions has three kinds. It asks what the manager has seen the person do, in terms of the sign. It asks for one example of the work. It asks what got in the way, because a missing sign may have a cause in the work rather than in the learning. The questions should be short enough to ask in ten minutes, and they should not ask the manager for a rating of the person.

**Worked example.** An L&D lead's first draft of questions for the managers of new managers reads: "Did you find the programme useful? Has their management improved? Would you recommend it?" The revised set reads: "In the last two weeks, how often has the new manager held a one-to-one with each team member? Could you share one agenda they used? Has anything made it harder for them to hold the one-to-ones, such as workload or rota changes?" Reading the two, the first set asks for opinions and a recommendation. The second asks what the manager has seen, asks for a piece of work, and asks what got in the way.

**Practice.** The learner writes one question of each kind for a manager of someone who took their programme, with the three kinds as labelled fields.

**Check.** Kind: Edit.

Question: "Edit these questions for the managers of people who took a programme on handling customer complaints, so that they ask for evidence and not opinion. The sign is that complaint replies now offer a specific next step and a date."

The document on screen: "1. Did the complaints training help your team? 2. Are they more confident with complaints now? 3. How would you rate the programme out of ten? 4. Anything else?"

The check looks for these parts:

- A question that asks what the manager has seen in terms of the sign, naming a next step and a date in complaint replies. Note if missing: "Ask what the manager has seen, in terms of the sign: replies that offer a next step and a date."
- A question that asks for an example of the work, such as one complaint reply. Note if missing: "Ask for one piece of work, such as a complaint reply from this week."
- A question that asks what got in the way. Note if missing: "Ask what has made it harder to use the skill, so a missing sign can be explained."
- Questions 1, 2, and 3 are removed or rewritten. Note for each that remains: "This question asks for an opinion or a rating. Rewrite it to ask what the manager has seen, or remove it."
- No question asks the manager to rate a person. Note if present: "Do not ask the manager to rate the person. Ask about the work."

A pass shows: "Your questions now ask what the manager has seen, ask for one piece of work, and ask what got in the way."

**Bridge.** Once the answers come in, some signs will be missing, and the next lesson reads what that means.

#### Lesson 4: Reading what you found

**Id:** `reading-what-you-found`

**Where it sits.** The learner has collected evidence. This lesson teaches them to read a missing sign correctly before deciding what to change.

**Core idea.** When a sign is missing two weeks after a programme, there are two quite different explanations, and they call for different responses. In this lesson a finding is The skill is missing when the person had the chance to use the skill in their work and did not use it, or used it poorly, which points to the design of the programme or the need for more practice. A finding is The work gave no chance to use it when the person has not had the occasion, time, tools, or permission to use the skill, such as a manager who learned to run one-to-ones but whose team was on leave, or an adviser who learned the new reply structure but whose system template still forces the old one. Research on the transfer of training has long found that the work environment, including support from managers and the opportunity to use new skills, affects whether learning is used. Treating the second kind of finding as the first leads to retraining people who already have the skill, and it leaves the real obstacle in place. Reading the findings correctly is not about excusing anyone; it is about fixing the right thing.

**Worked example.** Two weeks after a programme on writing delay updates, the L&D lead has three findings. In one team, sampled updates state the date and the next step but not the reason, even though the adviser had many delays to write about. In a second team, no delay updates were sent because the team was moved to handle a product launch. In a third team, advisers say they cannot state the new date because the case system does not show it until the next day. Read with the two labels, the first finding is The skill is missing, because the chance was there and the reason was left out, so the programme needs more practice on stating reasons. The second and third are The work gave no chance to use it: one because there was no occasion, and one because the system hid the information. The response to the third is to ask the systems team to show the date sooner, not to retrain the advisers.

**Practice.** The learner writes one finding from their own programme, or one they expect, and marks it "The skill is missing" or "The work gave no chance to use it", writing what they would change in response.

**Check.** Kind: Mark.

Question: "These findings come from a programme teaching new managers to hold fortnightly one-to-ones. Mark each as the skill is missing or the work gave no chance to use it."

Controls: "The skill is missing" and "The work gave no chance to use it".

1. "A manager held one-to-ones every fortnight, but the agendas show they used the time only to allocate tasks, without asking the team member anything." Correct: The skill is missing. Feedback if correct: "Right. The chance was there and was used, but not in the way the programme taught." Feedback if marked "The work gave no chance to use it": "The manager held the meetings, so the chance was there. What is missing is the skill of asking the team member for their view."
2. "A manager's team of four was on a three-week site shutdown, so no one-to-ones were possible." Correct: The work gave no chance to use it. Feedback if correct: "Right. There was no occasion to use the skill." Feedback if marked "The skill is missing": "Nobody could hold a one-to-one while the team was away. The work gave no chance to use it."
3. "A manager was told by their own director to cancel one-to-ones to meet a deadline." Correct: The work gave no chance to use it. Feedback if correct: "Right. The manager lost permission to use the skill, which is an obstacle in the work." Feedback if marked "The skill is missing": "The manager was told to stop. Retraining them would not help. The work gave no chance to use it."
4. "A manager with a full team and no deadline pressure held one one-to-one in four weeks." Correct: The skill is missing. Feedback if correct: "Right. The chance was there and the skill was not used, so look at whether the programme built the habit." Feedback if marked "The work gave no chance to use it": "Nothing in the work stopped this manager. The chance was there, so treat it as the skill missing for now, and ask the manager what happened."
5. "A manager could not book a private room, because the office has none, and the team works on an open floor." Correct: The work gave no chance to use it. Feedback if correct: "Right. The work environment lacks what the skill needs, and the fix is a room or a different arrangement." Feedback if marked "The skill is missing": "The obstacle here is the office, not the manager. The work gave no chance to use it."

A pass shows: "You told the findings that need a change to the programme from the findings that need a change to the work."

**Bridge.** The final lesson puts the signs, the questions, and the way you will read the findings on one sheet.

#### Lesson 5: The sheet

**Id:** `the-sheet`

**Where it sits.** This is the last lesson. The learner writes the measurement sheet for their programme, and that sheet appears on their record.

**Core idea.** The measurement sheet is one page that says how the learner will know whether one programme stuck. It names the programme and the skill it was meant to build. It lists no more than three signs in the work, each with the four features from lesson two: what someone would see, where in normal work, who sees it, and the before state. It says when the signs will be collected, usually between two and six weeks after the programme. It gives the three manager questions from lesson three. It says how the learner will read a missing sign, using the two findings from lesson four, and what they will change in each case. It lists the activity counts that will still be reported, if any, and says what they are for, so no one mistakes them for evidence that the work changed. It names who owns the sheet. It is not an evaluation report; it is the plan that makes a useful report possible.

**Worked example.** A measurement sheet for the delay update programme names the skill as writing a delay update that states the new date, the reason, and the next step. It has two signs: the proportion of sampled updates with all three parts, collected by team leaders from the case system two weeks after the programme, with a before sample of about four in ten; and the number of follow-up contacts from customers asking when their order will arrive, from the contact log, compared with the month before. It gives three manager questions, says that a missing sign where delays were written will be treated as the skill missing and lead to a practice session, and that a missing sign where the system hides the date will go to the systems team. It reports completion only to show who has had the chance to learn. The owner is the L&D lead for customer service.

**Practice.** The learner reviews their signs, questions, and findings from lessons two to four, and removes any sign that fails one of the four features.

**Check.** Kind: Write the artefact.

Question: "Write the measurement sheet for your programme. A sponsor should be able to see how you will know whether it stuck."

Fields: "The programme and the skill it builds", "Signs in the work", with up to three signs, each with what is seen, where, who sees it, and the before state. "When the signs will be collected". "The three manager questions". "How I will read a missing sign, and what I will change". "Activity counts I will still report, and what they are for". "Owner". "Your name as it should appear on the record".

**Bridge.** There is no next lesson. The learner signs the record and can open it from My courses.

### Artefact and record

The learner produces a one-page measurement sheet for one programme. The signed record names the learner, the course title, and the date, and shows the sheet as written. It states that it is a record of work completed on an Experrt self-serve course.

### How learning is validated

Every check must pass before the artefact opens. For the artefact, a pass requires all of the following:

- The skill is written with an action verb and names work, not a topic or a feeling.
- There are one to three signs, and each has all four features. A sign that is a completion, a rating, a quiz score, or a feeling, such as "more confident" or "more aware", produces the note: "This counts activity or describes a feeling. Name something someone else could see in normal work."
- Each sign names who sees it, and that person or system is not the learner alone.
- Each sign has a before state, even if it is an estimate from a sample.
- The collection time falls after the programme, within a stated number of weeks.
- There are three manager questions: one about what the manager has seen, one asking for an example of the work, and one about what got in the way. A question that asks for a rating or an opinion of the programme produces the note: "Ask what the manager has seen, not what they think of the programme."
- The section on missing signs names both findings and a different response for each.
- Any activity count listed has a stated purpose that is not described as evidence that the work changed.
- The sheet contains no names of individuals other than the signature, no email address, and no telephone number.
