# Technology track: course plans

These are the plans for the ten technology courses in the self-serve catalogue. Each plan is written to the standard in `../SKILL.md`. The modules in `lib/self-serve/catalog.ts` are the spine of each course, and each module has been expanded into one or more lessons. Every lesson follows the same shape: where it sits, the idea, one worked example, one practice, one check, and a bridge to the next lesson.

A few rules apply across the whole track. The words on every button are words that the lesson has already defined in its core idea. Checks use only the moves the skill allows: marking sentences with labels the lesson defined, choosing between two real pieces of work, editing a document, and writing the artefact. Ordering appears only in the rollout course, where the sequence is the skill being taught. No record says that the learner or their organisation is compliant with any regulation, standard or scheme. No lesson promises that pay will change.

## 1. Getting Value from the Technology You Already Pay For

The slug is `getting-value-from-the-technology-you-already-pay-for`. The course runs for two hours and costs £99. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: The inventory, Three jobs, Why people opt out, and The 30-day plan.

### Who it is for

This course is for team leaders, operations managers and office managers who hold or influence a software budget and suspect that their team uses only a small part of what it pays for. You should know which tools your team logs in to, and you should be able to see, or ask for, the list of licences your organisation pays for. You do not need administrator rights or any technical training.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can set the licences your team pays for beside the jobs your team repeats each week, point to three jobs that a tool you already own could carry, explain in plain words why people have not used it for those jobs so far, and hand your manager a 30-day plan that names the job, the feature, the owner, the first action, what stops, and the sign in the work that will show the change has taken hold.

### Learning objectives

1. Distinguish, for any tool, between a licence that is in use for a job and a licence that is paid for with no job named.
2. Produce an inventory of your team's licences that records, for each tool, the job it does and the nearby job still done somewhere else.
3. Select three repeating jobs that a tool you already pay for could carry, and give a reason for each one you reject.
4. Diagnose why colleagues have not moved a job into a tool, using the three reasons the course defines.
5. Write a 30-day plan for the three jobs that a colleague or manager could follow without asking you what you meant.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. A licence is not a job

- The id is `a-licence-is-not-a-job`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson sets out the difference between owning software and getting work out of it. Every later lesson in the course depends on that difference.
- **Core idea.** A licence is the right to use a piece of software, and it is paid for whether anyone uses it or not. The value arrives only when a job the team does anyway is done inside the tool, faster, more reliably or with less rework than before. A licence is not the same as a feature being switched on, and a feature being switched on is not the same as the team using it for a job. Nor is a daily login proof of value, because a person can open a tool every morning and still keep the real work in email and a personal spreadsheet. In this course, a tool is "In use for a job" when you can name the job, the person who does it, and the last week it happened inside the tool. A tool is "Paid for, no job named" when you cannot.
- **Worked example.** The input is an office manager's note about a twelve-person office on Microsoft 365: "We have Teams, SharePoint, Planner, Forms and Bookings. Everyone uses Teams daily. Client appointments are booked by phone and written into a shared Excel file. Planner was set up last year and has one board with no cards moved since March." The output is the manager's own rewrite: "Teams is in use for a job: daily internal chat and the weekly team call. Bookings is paid for, no job named, while client appointments are still booked by phone into Excel. Planner is paid for, no job named, with one board untouched since March." The reading is that the first note described what the office owned, while the rewrite describes where the work happens. Bookings stands out because a real job, booking appointments, is being done outside a tool the office already pays for. That kind of gap is exactly what this course is looking for.
- **Practice.** You type the names of three tools your team has access to. For each one, you write a single sentence that either names the job it is used for and who does it, or says "Paid for, no job named". The worked example stays visible above the boxes.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A team lead wrote these four lines about their tools. Mark each line with the label it deserves." The two controls read "In use for a job" and "Paid for, no job named".
  - "Slack: every client escalation is posted in the escalations channel, and the on-call person replies there." The correct label is "In use for a job". The feedback for a right answer is: "This line names the job, client escalations, and the person who handles it inside the tool, so it is in use for a job." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look again at this line. It names a job and the person who does it inside Slack, which is what in use for a job means."
  - "Zoom: everyone has an account." The correct label is "Paid for, no job named". The feedback for a right answer is: "An account is the licence, not the job, and nothing in the line says what work happens in Zoom." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Having an account is the licence itself. The lesson asked for the work that happens inside the tool, and this line names none."
  - "Google Forms: we have it and it is really useful." The correct label is "Paid for, no job named". The feedback for a right answer is: "Useful is an opinion rather than a job, so no job has been named." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This line gives an opinion, not a job. It does not say what form is used, by whom, or when it was last used."
  - "SharePoint: the signed contracts library, where finance files every contract on the day it is signed." The correct label is "In use for a job". The feedback for a right answer is: "The line names the job, filing signed contracts, the team that does it, and when, so it is in use for a job." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Read the line again. It names finance, the job of filing signed contracts, and when it happens, which is everything the lesson asked for."
- **Bridge.** Now that you can tell a licence from a job, the next lesson turns that distinction into an inventory of everything your team pays for.

#### Lesson 2. Take the inventory

- The id is `take-the-inventory`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the first module of the catalogue outline. It produces the table that the rest of the course draws on.
- **Core idea.** An inventory is a table with one row for each licensed tool and four columns: the tool, who holds a licence, the job it is in use for, and the nearby job that is still done somewhere else. It is not the finance list of subscriptions, although that list is the right place to start, because finance records cost and not work. It is also not the vendor's feature list, which tells you what is possible rather than what is happening. The fourth column, the nearby job, is the one that matters most: for each tool, you write the repeating job closest to what the tool can already do that the team still does by hand, by email or in a personal spreadsheet. If you need the list of assigned licences, the person who administers your accounts can usually export it, and both the Microsoft 365 admin centre and the Google Workspace Admin console provide licence and usage reports.
- **Worked example.** The input is the finance list for a small agency: "Microsoft 365 Business Standard for twelve people, Adobe Acrobat Pro for four people, Mailchimp Standard, Xero, and DocuSign for two people." The output includes this row: "Acrobat Pro, four licences held by the account managers, in use for editing PDF brochures. Nearby job still done elsewhere: client contracts are printed, signed and scanned, although both Acrobat Pro and DocuSign can send a document for electronic signature." The reading is that the row has found two things at once. It has found a capability the agency already pays for, twice, and a repeating job that is still done on paper. Whether to keep both signature tools is a question for the renewal date, not for this lesson, but the inventory has put it on the table.
- **Practice.** You fill in two rows of your own inventory with all four columns. A hint beside the fourth column reads: "Name a job that repeats, not a feature you have heard of."
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two colleagues each wrote an inventory row for the same tool. Choose the row you could act on." The two controls read "Row A" and "Row B". Row A reads: "Mailchimp: marketing uses it. Good tool, we should use it more." Row B reads: "Mailchimp Standard, one licence used by the marketing team, in use for the monthly newsletter. Nearby job still done elsewhere: event invitations are sent from Outlook with a pasted list of two hundred addresses." The correct answer is Row B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Row B names who holds the licence, the job it is in use for, and the nearby job still done elsewhere, so you can act on it." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Row A says who uses the tool but not for what job, and it leaves the fourth column, the nearby job still done elsewhere, empty. Look again at what that column asks for."
- **Bridge.** An inventory usually shows more nearby jobs than a team can move at once, so the next lesson shows you how to choose three.

#### Lesson 3. Three jobs worth moving

- The id is `three-jobs-worth-moving`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It narrows the inventory down to the three jobs your plan will cover.
- **Core idea.** A job is "Worth moving" when four things are true: it repeats at least weekly, it involves more than one person or a handoff between people, a tool already in your inventory can do it, and a mistake during the change would be cheap to put right. A job is "Not now" when any of those fails. This is not a search for the team's hardest problem, because hard problems usually need a project, a budget or a new purchase, and this course is about the licences you already hold. It is also not a search for the most impressive feature. Three jobs is deliberate: it is enough for the team to feel a difference in a normal week, and few enough that you can support each one properly for a month.
- **Worked example.** The input is five nearby jobs from the agency's inventory: contracts printed and scanned, event invitations sent from Outlook, the quarterly board pack assembled by hand, holiday requests sent by email to one manager, and a one-off migration of old files from a retired server. The output marks contracts, invitations and holiday requests as worth moving, with the reasons that each repeats weekly, involves a handoff, and uses Acrobat Pro, Mailchimp and Microsoft Forms respectively. It marks the board pack as not now because it happens quarterly, and the file migration as not now because it happens once. The reading is that the two rejected jobs are real and may well matter, but neither will build a weekly habit, and a plan built on them would stall after the first week.
- **Practice.** You copy five nearby jobs from your own inventory and mark each one "Worth moving" or "Not now", writing one sentence of reason for each "Not now".
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A finance team lead listed four candidate jobs. Mark each one with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Worth moving" and "Not now".
  - "Supplier invoices arrive in three people's inboxes and are forwarded to the approver; the team already has a shared mailbox it does not use." The correct label is "Worth moving". The feedback for a right answer is: "This repeats weekly, involves a handoff, and uses a tool the team already has, so it is worth moving." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check the four tests again. Invoices repeat every week, pass between people, and the shared mailbox is already paid for."
  - "Rebuild the annual budget model in a new planning system." The correct label is "Not now". The feedback for a right answer is: "It happens once a year and needs a new purchase, so it fails two of the four tests." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This job needs a system the team does not own and happens once a year. The lesson is about weekly jobs in tools you already pay for."
  - "The month-end checklist lives in one person's Word document and is emailed round; Planner is licensed and unused." The correct label is "Worth moving". The feedback for a right answer is: "It repeats every month for several people, and Planner is already paid for, so a slip is cheap to fix." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at the handoff. Several people work from this checklist every month, and the tool that could carry it is already licensed."
  - "Answer the auditor's questions about last year's accounts." The correct label is "Not now". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is a one-off, so it will not build a habit." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This job does not repeat. The first test in the lesson is that the job comes round at least weekly."
- **Bridge.** Choosing the job is the easy part, and the next lesson explains why a good job still stays outside the tool.

#### Lesson 4. Why people opt out

- The id is `why-people-opt-out`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It gives you a way to diagnose the reason behind each job you chose, so the plan fixes the right thing.
- **Core idea.** When a team ignores a tool, the cause is rarely stubbornness, and it is not fixed by an announcement. This course uses three reasons that you can hear in what people say. The first is "Quicker the old way": the new route costs time the first few times, and the benefit arrives later, so a busy person reasonably returns to what they know. The second is "Nobody showed them": any training they had was a tour of features, not ten minutes on their own piece of work. The third is "Waiting on someone else": the job is a handoff, and one person using the new route while the others stay on email is worse than everyone staying on email. Each reason calls for a different remedy: a ready-made template for the first, a short session on their own case for the second, and moving the whole handoff on the same day for the third.
- **Worked example.** The input is three short quotes the office manager collected about Bookings. The first is: "I tried it once, but it took twenty minutes to set my hours, and the phone is two seconds." The second is: "I didn't know it could send the reminder text for me." The third is: "There's no point me using it if reception still writes appointments in the spreadsheet." The output labels the first quote "Quicker the old way", the second "Nobody showed them" and the third "Waiting on someone else". The reading is that three people have three different reasons, so one reminder email would have fixed none of them. The plan now needs pre-set hours for each person, a ten-minute session using a real appointment, and a single date on which reception stops using the spreadsheet.
- **Practice.** You write down one thing a colleague has said about a tool on your list of three jobs, and you mark it with one of the three reasons, adding the remedy the lesson pairs with it.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A team lead collected these three comments about a shared task board. Mark each comment with the reason behind it." The three controls read "Quicker the old way", "Nobody showed them" and "Waiting on someone else".
  - "I'll move my tasks over when the others do; right now I'd just be updating it for myself." The correct label is "Waiting on someone else". The feedback for a right answer is: "The speaker depends on colleagues who have not moved, so the remedy is to move the whole handoff at once." The feedback for "Quicker the old way" is: "The speaker has not said the board is slow. They said it is pointless while others stay behind, which is waiting on someone else." The feedback for "Nobody showed them" is: "The speaker knows how to use the board. What stops them is that colleagues are not on it, which is waiting on someone else."
  - "Typing it into the board takes me longer than sending a quick message." The correct label is "Quicker the old way". The feedback for a right answer is: "The complaint is about time on the first attempts, so a template that removes the set-up will help." The feedback for "Nobody showed them" is: "The speaker can use the board and finds it slower. That is the cost of the first few times, which the lesson called quicker the old way." The feedback for "Waiting on someone else" is: "Nothing here mentions colleagues. The speaker compares their own time on the two routes, which is quicker the old way."
  - "I didn't realise I could get a reminder when something is due." The correct label is "Nobody showed them". The feedback for a right answer is: "The speaker was never shown the part that would help with their own work, so ten minutes on their own case is the remedy." The feedback for "Quicker the old way" is: "The speaker has not compared speeds. They did not know the feature existed, which means nobody showed them." The feedback for "Waiting on someone else" is: "No colleague is mentioned. The gap is knowledge of a feature, which means nobody showed them."
- **Bridge.** You now have three jobs and the reason behind each, and the next lesson shows you what a plan must contain by repairing a weak one.

#### Lesson 5. Repair a 30-day plan

- The id is `repair-a-30-day-plan`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson prepares you for the final module. You practise on someone else's plan before you write your own.
- **Core idea.** A 30-day plan is one row for each job, with seven parts: the job, the tool and the feature, the owner, the reason people opted out and its remedy, the first action with a date in week one, the old route that stops and when, and the sign in the work you expect to see in week four. It is not a wish list or a training schedule. Two parts are most often missing. The first is what stops, because if the spreadsheet stays open the team will keep using it. The second is the sign in the work, which must be something you could see in the work itself, such as appointments appearing in Bookings instead of the spreadsheet, rather than a count of logins or a sense that people like the tool.
- **Worked example.** The input is a weak row: "Holiday requests: use Forms. Tell the team at the next meeting. Should be easier for everyone." The output is the repaired row: "Job: holiday requests, currently emailed to Priya. Tool and feature: Microsoft Forms with a response spreadsheet. Owner: Priya. Reason and remedy: nobody showed them, so a ten-minute walkthrough at the Monday meeting using a real request. First action: Priya builds the form by Friday of week one. What stops: from the first of next month, emailed requests are sent back with the form link. Sign in the work: in week four, every request for the month is in the response sheet and none is in Priya's inbox." The reading is that the weak row had a tool and a hope, while the repaired row has a person, a date, an end to the old route, and a way to tell whether it worked.
- **Practice.** You are shown a plan row with the owner and the first action filled in and the other parts blank, and you fill in what stops and the sign in the work.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this plan row so that a manager could follow it without asking you anything. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "Job: the weekly stock order. Tool: the shared Excel file in SharePoint. Everyone will try to use it. Hopefully fewer mistakes." A correct edit names an owner, a first action with a date in week one, what stops and when, and a sign in the work in week four that is not a count of logins or a feeling. The feedback for a correct edit is: "The row now names who owns it, what happens first and when, which old route stops, and what you will see in the work in week four." The feedback when the owner is missing is: "The row still does not say who owns it. 'Everyone' is not an owner; name one person or role." The feedback when the first action or its date is missing is: "The row does not yet say what happens first, or by which day in week one." The feedback when what stops is missing is: "The row does not say which old route stops. If the emailed order still works, people will keep using it." The feedback when the sign is a feeling or a count is: "'Fewer mistakes' or 'more people using it' is not something you can see in the work. Say what you expect to find in the stock file in week four."
- **Bridge.** You have repaired someone else's plan, and in the last lesson you write your own for the three jobs you chose.

#### Lesson 6. Your 30-day plan

- The id is `your-30-day-plan`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the final module of the outline, and the plan you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** The plan is the artefact of the course. It has three rows, one for each job from Lesson 3, and each row has the seven parts from Lesson 5. Write it for the person who will actually carry it out, who may not be you, and who was not in this course. It is not a proposal to buy anything, and it should not mention a tool that is missing from your inventory. When you have written it, you read it once as your manager would, and ask whether any row could be followed on Monday without a question.
- **Worked example.** The input is the office manager's three chosen jobs: client appointments, holiday requests, and the month-end checklist. The output is a three-row plan in which the appointments row reads: "Job: client appointment booking, now by phone into Excel. Tool and feature: Microsoft Bookings with email confirmation. Owner: reception lead. Reason and remedy: waiting on someone else, so reception and all four advisers switch on the same Monday. First action: the reception lead sets each adviser's hours by Wednesday of week one. What stops: the appointments sheet is made read-only on the switch-over Monday. Sign in the work: in week four, every appointment for that week appears in Bookings and none appears in the old sheet." The reading is that each part of the row can be checked by someone other than the author, which is what makes it a plan rather than an intention.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. The worked example stays open in a panel you can expand while you write.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write your 30-day plan for your three jobs. Each row must have all seven parts." There are three rows, each with seven fields labelled "Job", "Tool and feature", "Owner", "Reason and remedy", "First action", "What stops" and "Sign in the work". The plan passes when every row meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Each row names a repeating job, a tool from your inventory, an owner, a remedy that matches the reason, a first action with a date, the route that stops, and a sign you could see in the work." When a row fails, the feedback names the row and the part. For example: "Row two does not say what stops, so the old route will stay open." Another example is: "The sign in row three is a count of logins. Say what you would see in the work itself." After a pass, you type your name to sign the plan.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed plan is on your record and suggests you share it with the owners you named.

### The artefact and the signed record

The learner produces a 30-day plan with three rows, one for each job, and each row has the seven parts taught in Lesson 5. The signed record shows the learner's signed name, the course title, the date of signing, and the three plan rows exactly as written. It does not show the inventory, the practice answers, or any failed attempts.

### How learning is validated

A plan passes only when every row has all seven parts and each part does its job. The job must repeat at least weekly and must not be a one-off. The tool must appear in the inventory the learner wrote in Lesson 2. The owner must be a named person or role, and "everyone" or "the team" is rejected. The remedy must match the reason, using the pairings in Lesson 4. The first action must carry a day in week one. What stops must name the old route. The sign in the work must describe something observable in the work in week four, and it is rejected when it is a count of logins, a measure of usage alone, or a feeling such as "people like it". Each check in Lessons 1 to 5 must also have been passed.

### Sources and reading

- Microsoft Learn, the documentation for Microsoft 365 reports in the admin centre, which explains how to see assigned licences and activity by product.
- Google Workspace Admin Help, the documentation on reports and licence management in the Admin console.
- Microsoft Adoption (adoption.microsoft.com), Microsoft's public guidance and resources on driving adoption of Microsoft 365 tools.
- Chip Heath and Dan Heath, *Switch: How to Change Things When Change Is Hard* (2010), on why small, specific behaviours move more readily than general appeals.

## 2. Choosing Technology for Your Team

The slug is `choosing-technology-for-your-team`. The course runs for two hours and costs £129. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: The job, Five questions, The switching cost, and The choice record.

### Who it is for

This course is for team leaders and managers who have been asked to choose a tool for their team, or to recommend one to someone who holds the budget. You should be able to describe the work your team does in a normal week, and you should have at least one tool in mind, or one that a colleague or vendor has suggested. You do not need to know how software is built or hosted.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can describe the job a tool must do in terms of your team's real work, put the same five questions to every option including doing nothing, estimate what it would cost to switch in and to switch out, and write a one-page choice record that a colleague could read a year later and understand what was chosen, what was rejected, and why.

### Learning objectives

1. Describe the job a tool must do as a piece of your team's work, not as a list of features.
2. Apply the course's five questions to each option, including the option of changing nothing.
3. Test an option on your own work rather than on a vendor's demonstration.
4. Estimate the switching cost of an option, in both directions, in people's time as well as money.
5. Write a one-page choice record that states the decision, the reasons, and the date on which it will be reviewed.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. Start with the job

- The id is `start-with-the-job`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson is the first module of the outline. Everything else in the course is measured against the job you write here.
- **Core idea.** A job statement describes the work the tool must carry: who does it, what goes in, what comes out, how often, and what goes wrong today. It is written before you look at any product, because a product page will quietly rewrite your needs around its own features. A job statement is not a requirement such as "must have a mobile app" or "must use AI", because those are features, and a feature is only worth having if it serves the job. It is also not a complaint, although today's failure belongs in it. In this course, a sentence that describes the work is "Describes the job", and a sentence that names a capability without saying what work it serves is "Names a feature".
- **Worked example.** The input is a first draft from a facilities manager: "We need a helpdesk tool with a mobile app, AI triage and good dashboards." The output is the rewritten job statement: "Staff in three buildings report faults such as broken lights and heating problems, about forty a week, by email or by stopping a caretaker in the corridor. The two caretakers need to see every open fault, who reported it, and where it is, and staff need to know it has been logged. Today, faults reported in the corridor are often forgotten, and staff chase by email." The reading is that the first draft contained three features and no work. The rewrite shows who is involved, the volume, and the failure that matters, which is forgotten corridor reports. A mobile app might help with that failure, but now you can ask whether it does, rather than assume it.
- **Practice.** You write three sentences about the job your own team needs a tool for: who does the work, how often, and what goes wrong today.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A sales manager wrote these four sentences about the tool they want. Mark each sentence with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Describes the job" and "Names a feature".
  - "Our six account managers each keep their own list of prospects in a spreadsheet." The correct label is "Describes the job". The feedback for a right answer is: "This sentence says who does the work and how they do it today." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "No product capability is named here. The sentence describes who does the work and where it lives today."
  - "It must integrate with LinkedIn." The correct label is "Names a feature". The feedback for a right answer is: "An integration is a capability, and the sentence does not say what work it would serve." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This sentence names what the tool must have, not the work. Ask what job the integration would do."
  - "When someone is off sick, nobody else can see which prospects are due a call." The correct label is "Describes the job". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is today's failure, stated as work, which is exactly what a job statement needs." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This sentence names a failure in the work, not a feature. Failures belong in the job statement."
  - "It needs a modern dashboard." The correct label is "Names a feature". The feedback for a right answer is: "A dashboard is a feature, and 'modern' says nothing about the work." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look for the work in this sentence. There is none, only a feature and an adjective."
- **Bridge.** With the job written down, the next lesson gives you five questions to put to every option against it.

#### Lesson 2. Five questions for every option

- The id is `five-questions-for-every-option`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It gives you one set of questions to use on every option, so the comparison is fair.
- **Core idea.** The five questions are these. First, does it do the job in the way we do it, or would we have to change the work to fit the tool? Second, who will run it day to day, including adding and removing people, and do they have the time? Third, where does our information go, who can see it, and can we get all of it back out in a usable form? Fourth, what does it need to connect to, and does that connection already exist? Fifth, what will it cost over three years, counting people's time as well as the licence? The questions are not a scoring sheet, and adding up points hides the one answer that should stop the choice. They apply to the option of changing nothing as well, because doing nothing also has a cost, a person who runs it, and a place where the data lives. A sentence in a vendor document or a colleague's summary either "Answers a question" with something you could check, or "Leaves it open".
- **Worked example.** The input is a vendor's reply to the facilities manager: "Our platform is trusted by leading organisations. Data is hosted in the UK on Microsoft Azure. Admins can add users in seconds. Pricing is per user per month. We integrate with hundreds of apps." The output marks the hosting sentence as answering the third question in part, because it says where data is held, while noting that it does not say how to get data out. It marks "admins can add users in seconds" as leaving the second question open, because it does not say who the administrator will be. It marks "hundreds of apps" as leaving the fourth question open, because it does not say whether it connects to the email system the staff actually use. The reading is that a confident document can answer very little. Of five questions, one is partly answered, and the rest need to be asked directly.
- **Practice.** You take one option you are considering and write one line under each of the five questions, writing "Not yet known" where you do not have an answer.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four sentences come from a colleague's summary of a scheduling tool. Mark each sentence with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Answers a question" and "Leaves it open".
  - "Everything can be exported as a CSV file from the settings page, including past bookings." The correct label is "Answers a question". The feedback for a right answer is: "This answers the third question about getting your data back out, and you could test it." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This sentence says how to get all the data out and in what form. That is a checkable answer to the third question."
  - "It's really intuitive." The correct label is "Leaves it open". The feedback for a right answer is: "Intuitive is an impression, and it answers none of the five questions." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check which of the five questions this sentence answers. It is an impression, so it leaves them all open."
  - "Joe in operations will add and remove staff, which he estimates at an hour a month." The correct label is "Answers a question". The feedback for a right answer is: "This names who runs the tool and how much of their time it takes, which answers the second question." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This sentence names the person who will run it and the time involved. That is the second question answered."
  - "It connects to lots of calendars." The correct label is "Leaves it open". The feedback for a right answer is: "It does not say whether it connects to the calendar your team uses, so the fourth question is still open." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "'Lots of calendars' does not tell you whether it connects to yours. The fourth question is about the connection you need."
- **Bridge.** Some of the five questions can only be answered by trying the tool, and the next lesson shows you how to try it on your own work rather than on a demonstration.

#### Lesson 3. Test it on your own work

- The id is `test-it-on-your-own-work`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson expands the second module. It turns the first of the five questions into a small trial you can run in an afternoon.
- **Core idea.** A vendor demonstration shows the tool doing the vendor's best case, with clean data and a practised presenter. A trial on your own work means taking three real cases from last week, including one awkward one, and asking the person who normally does the job to put them through the tool themselves during a free trial or a sandbox account. It is not a pilot, which is a longer period of real use, and it does not need anyone's approval beyond the trial terms. The awkward case matters most, because a tool that handles the typical case well may make the exception much harder than it is today. Write down what happened to each case in one sentence.
- **Worked example.** The input is the facilities manager's three cases: a broken light reported by email, a heating fault reported in the corridor to a caretaker, and a leak that turned out to be the landlord's responsibility. The output is the trial note: "The email report was logged automatically. The corridor report took a caretaker three minutes on the phone app, standing in the corridor. The landlord's leak could not be closed without assigning it to one of our own caretakers, so it would sit open forever or be recorded falsely." The reading is that the demonstration had covered only the email case. The trial found that the tool helps with the failure named in the job statement, corridor reports, and also found an exception that the vendor now needs to answer before a choice is made.
- **Practice.** You write down three real cases from last week for your own job statement, and you mark which one is the awkward case.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two team members tested the same expenses tool. Choose the trial note you would rely on." The two controls read "Note A" and "Note B". Note A reads: "We watched the vendor's demonstration and it handled receipts, mileage and approvals smoothly. It looked much faster than our spreadsheet." Note B reads: "Sam put three of last week's claims through the free trial: a taxi receipt, a mileage claim, and a hotel bill split between two projects. The first two took under a minute each. The split bill could only be charged to one project, so Sam had to enter it twice." The correct answer is Note B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Note B uses real cases, the person who does the job, and an awkward case, and it records what happened to each." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Note A describes the vendor's demonstration, not your own work. It has no awkward case, so it cannot tell you where the tool makes the job harder."
- **Bridge.** Once a tool passes the trial, the next lesson asks what it will cost to move into it and, one day, out of it.

#### Lesson 4. The switching cost

- The id is `the-switching-cost`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It adds the cost that is usually left off the comparison.
- **Core idea.** The switching cost is everything it takes to move the work from where it is now into the new tool, and everything it would take to move it out again. Moving in includes moving existing data, setting the tool up, teaching people, running old and new side by side for a period, and the slower weeks while people learn. Moving out includes getting your data back in a form another tool can read, notice periods in the contract, and the same learning cost again for the next tool. It is not the licence price, which is usually the easiest number to find and often the smallest part of the cost. In this course, a line in a comparison is either "Switching cost" or "Licence cost", and a comparison that has only the second kind is not finished.
- **Worked example.** The input is a comparison line from the sales manager: "Option B: £25 per user per month, six users, £1,800 a year." The output adds switching cost lines: "Moving in: six spreadsheets of prospects to be cleaned and imported, estimated at two days of one account manager's time. Set-up: fields and stages configured by the sales manager, about one day. Teaching: one hour for each account manager. Side by side: two weeks in which the spreadsheets stay open as a back-up. Moving out: the contract is annual with ninety days' notice, and contacts export as CSV but call notes do not." The reading is that the licence line was correct and incomplete. The call notes that cannot be exported are the most important finding, because they are the thing the team will build up over years and could not take with them.
- **Practice.** You write two switching cost lines for your own option, one for moving in and one for moving out.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four lines come from a comparison of two project tools. Mark each line with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Switching cost" and "Licence cost".
  - "Tool X costs £8 per user per month on the annual plan." The correct label is "Licence cost". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is the price of the licence and nothing else." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This line is only the price per user. Switching cost is the time and effort of moving in or out."
  - "Our 140 open projects would need to be re-entered by hand, as there is no import from our current tool." The correct label is "Switching cost". The feedback for a right answer is: "Re-entering existing work is part of the cost of moving in." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This line is about the effort of moving existing work into the tool. That is switching cost, not the licence."
  - "Attachments can only be downloaded one at a time if we leave." The correct label is "Switching cost". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is a cost of moving out, which the lesson counts as switching cost." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Think about the day you leave. Downloading files one by one is the cost of moving out."
  - "Tool Y is free for up to ten users." The correct label is "Licence cost". The feedback for a right answer is: "A free tier is still a statement about the licence price." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This line says what the licence costs, even though the answer is nothing. It says nothing about moving in or out."
- **Bridge.** You now have the job, the five answers, a trial and the switching cost, and the next lesson shows how these fit on one page by repairing a weak choice record.

#### Lesson 5. Repair a choice record

- The id is `repair-a-choice-record`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson prepares you for the final module. You practise the shape of the record on someone else's work first.
- **Core idea.** A choice record is a single page with six parts: the job statement, the options considered including changing nothing, the answers to the five questions for each option, the result of the trial on your own work, the switching cost in both directions, and the decision with a named review date. It is written for someone who was not in the room, such as your successor, an auditor, or you in a year's time. It is not a sales case for the winning tool, and it should say plainly what the chosen option does worse than the others. The two parts most often missing are the option of changing nothing and the review date, and without the review date the choice is never revisited.
- **Worked example.** The input is a weak record: "We chose Tool B because it was the best fit and the team liked the demo." The output is a repaired record whose decision section reads: "We chose Tool B over Tool A and over keeping the spreadsheets. Tool B handled the split-project claim in the trial and Tool A did not. Tool B's weakness is that receipts cannot be bulk-exported, so we will download the receipt archive each quarter. We will review the choice on 1 March, when the annual contract can still be cancelled with notice." The reading is that the weak record gave a feeling and a demonstration, while the repaired one gives the reason, the weakness, what the team will do about it, and a date on which someone will look again.
- **Practice.** You are shown a record that is complete except for the options section, and you add the option of changing nothing with one sentence on what it would cost.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this choice record so a colleague could understand the decision in a year's time. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "Job: the team needs a shared rota. Options: RotaApp and ShiftBoard. Decision: ShiftBoard, because it is cheaper." A correct edit adds the option of changing nothing, at least one answer to the five questions for each option, a trial result on real cases, a switching cost line, what the chosen option does worse, and a review date. The feedback for a correct edit is: "The record now shows every option including changing nothing, the evidence from your own work, the cost of switching, the weakness you accepted, and when it will be looked at again." The feedback when changing nothing is missing is: "The record still does not consider keeping the current rota. Say what that would cost." The feedback when the trial is missing is: "The record does not say what happened when real shifts were put through either tool." The feedback when switching cost is missing is: "'Cheaper' refers only to the licence. Add what it would take to move in and out." The feedback when the weakness is missing is: "Say what ShiftBoard does worse than the other options, so a reader knows what was accepted." The feedback when the review date is missing is: "Add the date on which someone will look at this choice again."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you write the choice record for your own team's decision.

#### Lesson 6. Your choice record

- The id is `your-choice-record`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the final module of the outline, and the record you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** Your choice record uses the six parts from Lesson 5 and draws on the job statement, the five answers, the trial and the switching cost you wrote earlier in the course. If you have not yet made the decision, you may write "Recommended" instead of "Chosen", but you must still say which option you recommend and why. A record that recommends changing nothing is a valid record, and sometimes the right one. The test is whether a colleague who joins next year can read the page and know what was weighed and what was accepted.
- **Worked example.** The input is the facilities manager's notes from the course. The output is a one-page record that recommends the helpdesk tool, lists changing nothing as an option costing roughly two hours a week of caretaker time spent chasing corridor reports, records the landlord-leak exception and the vendor's written answer that a "closed, external" status can be added, sets out two days of set-up and ninety days' notice on exit, and names a review on the contract anniversary. The reading is that the record admits what the tool does not do well and names the date the choice will be revisited, which is why a reader can trust it.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. The repaired record from Lesson 5 stays open in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the choice record for your own team's decision. Include all six parts." The fields are labelled "The job", "Options considered", "Five questions", "Trial on our work", "Switching cost" and "Decision and review date". The record passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your record states the job, weighs every option including changing nothing, shows evidence from your own work, counts the cost of switching both ways, admits the weakness you accepted, and names a review date." When a part fails, the feedback names it, for example: "The options do not include changing nothing" or "The switching cost covers moving in but not moving out." After a pass, you type your name to sign the record.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed choice record is on your record.

### The artefact and the signed record

The learner produces a one-page choice record with six parts. The signed record shows the learner's signed name, the course title, the date of signing, and the choice record exactly as written.

### How learning is validated

The record passes only when every part does its job. The job statement must describe work, with at least who does it and what goes wrong today, and must not be a list of features. The options must include changing nothing. Each option must have an answer or an explicit "Not yet known" under each of the five questions. The trial must name at least two real cases, one of them awkward, and what happened to each. The switching cost must include at least one line for moving in and one for moving out, and a licence price alone is rejected. The decision must name the option, one thing it does worse, and a review date. Each check in Lessons 1 to 5 must also have been passed.

## 3. No-Code Automation for Everyday Work

The slug is `no-code-automation-for-everyday-work`. The course runs for two and a half hours and costs £99. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: The task, A no-code path, Run it, and When it breaks.

### Who it is for

This course is for administrators, coordinators and team leads who spend part of every week on a task that follows the same steps each time, such as copying form responses into a list or sending a standard email when a file arrives. You should use at least one tool your organisation already licenses that offers simple automation, such as Microsoft Power Automate, Zapier, Make, or the rules built into your email client. You do not need to write code, and you should have permission from whoever manages your systems before you connect anything.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can pick a repeating task that is safe to automate, describe its trigger, steps and conditions in plain words, build one small automation in a tool you already have, test it on real cases including the awkward ones, and write a failure note that says how you will know it has stopped working, who will notice, what people do by hand in the meantime, and how to switch it off.

### Learning objectives

1. Distinguish the parts of a task that follow a rule a tool can apply from the parts that need a person's judgement.
2. Describe a task as a trigger, a set of steps and a set of conditions that a colleague could build from.
3. Build one automation in a tool you already have, running under an account and permissions you can justify.
4. Test the automation on normal, incomplete, duplicate and unusual cases, and record what happened to each.
5. Write a failure note that a colleague could use when the automation stops working and you are away.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. Rule or judgement

- The id is `rule-or-judgement`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson is the first module of the outline. It gives you the test for whether a task, or part of one, can be handed to a tool.
- **Core idea.** An automation follows rules: when this happens, do that, unless this other thing is true. A step is a "Rule a tool can follow" when you could write it down so precisely that a new colleague on their first day would do it the same way every time. A step is "Judgement a person makes" when doing it well depends on reading the situation, such as deciding whether a customer's tone means they are about to leave. Automation is not a way to make judgement faster, and a tool that is given a judgement step will apply a rule anyway, just a bad one. Most real tasks mix the two, and the useful move is to automate the rule steps around the judgement, so the person gets the case prepared and makes the decision.
- **Worked example.** The input is a coordinator's description of handling training requests: "A manager fills in the request form. I copy the details into the training tracker. I check whether the course is suitable for the person's role. I email the manager to confirm, and I add the date to the shared calendar." The output marks copying into the tracker, emailing a confirmation and adding the date as rules a tool can follow, and marks checking suitability as judgement a person makes. The reading is that four of the five steps can be automated, but only if the automation stops and waits for the coordinator's suitability decision before it sends the confirmation. If the automation sent the confirmation straight away, it would be applying a rule, "every request is suitable", that nobody chose.
- **Practice.** You write down the steps of one task you repeat every week, one step per line, and mark each one with either "Rule a tool can follow" or "Judgement a person makes".
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "An office manager listed these four steps for handling new starters. Mark each step with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Rule a tool can follow" and "Judgement a person makes".
  - "When HR adds a new starter to the list, create a folder named with their full name in the Starters library." The correct label is "Rule a tool can follow". The feedback for a right answer is: "Anyone would do this the same way every time, so a tool can follow it." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "There is no decision in this step. The trigger and the action are both exact, so it is a rule a tool can follow."
  - "Decide which desk they should sit at, given who they will work with most." The correct label is "Judgement a person makes". The feedback for a right answer is: "Choosing a desk depends on reading the team, which is judgement." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Two sensible people could choose different desks here. That means it is judgement a person makes."
  - "Send the standard welcome email three days before the start date." The correct label is "Rule a tool can follow". The feedback for a right answer is: "The timing and the content are fixed, so this is a rule." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Nothing here needs a decision. The email is standard and the timing is exact."
  - "Check whether their manager has planned enough for their first week." The correct label is "Judgement a person makes". The feedback for a right answer is: "'Enough' needs someone to read the plan and decide, which is judgement." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at the word 'enough'. Deciding what is enough is judgement a person makes."
- **Bridge.** Once you know which steps are rules, the next lesson shows you how to describe them so precisely that they can be built.

#### Lesson 2. Trigger, steps and conditions

- The id is `trigger-steps-and-conditions`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson expands the first module. It turns the rule steps into a description that you, or a colleague, can build from.
- **Core idea.** Every automation has three parts. The trigger is the single event that starts it, such as a new form response, a new file in a folder, or an email arriving from a particular address. The steps are what happens next, in order, each naming the exact place information comes from and goes to. The conditions are the rules that change what happens, such as "only if the amount is over £500" or "stop if the email field is empty". A description is not a wish such as "handle new requests automatically", and it is not a list of apps. In this course, a description is "Ready to build" when every part names a place, a field or an event, and "Needs more detail" when someone would have to ask you what you meant.
- **Worked example.** The input is the coordinator's first description: "When someone requests training, put it in the tracker and let them know." The output is the rewritten description: "Trigger: a new response to the Training Request form in Microsoft Forms. Step one: add a row to the Requests table in the Training Tracker workbook, copying the requester's name, email, the course and the preferred month. Step two: send the coordinator an approval request in Teams with the course and the person's role. Condition: if the coordinator approves, send the manager the confirmation email from the shared mailbox; if the coordinator rejects, send the manager the standard 'not this time' email with the coordinator's comment. Condition: if the email field is empty, stop and email the coordinator." The reading is that the first description hid four decisions: which form, which tracker, who says yes, and what happens with an incomplete response. The rewrite makes each of them visible.
- **Practice.** You rewrite the rule steps from your Lesson 1 practice as a trigger, steps and at least one condition.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two colleagues described the same invoice task. Choose the description that is ready to build." The two controls read "Description A" and "Description B". Description A reads: "Automatically save supplier invoices and tell finance." Description B reads: "Trigger: a new email with a PDF attachment arrives in the invoices@ shared mailbox. Step one: save the attachment to the Incoming folder in the Finance SharePoint library, named with the date and the sender. Step two: post a message in the Finance Teams channel with a link to the file. Condition: if the email has no attachment, move it to the 'Check' folder in the mailbox and do not post." The correct answer is Description B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Description B names the trigger, the exact places for each step, and what happens when there is no attachment, so it is ready to build." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Description A does not say which mailbox, which folder, how finance will be told, or what happens when there is no invoice. It needs more detail."
- **Bridge.** With a description ready to build, the next lesson helps you choose where to build it and whose account it will run under.

#### Lesson 3. A no-code path

- The id is `a-no-code-path`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It covers the choice of tool and the question of access that people usually skip.
- **Core idea.** Start with the automation tools your organisation already licenses and allows, because a tool your IT team has not approved may break policy and will be switched off when someone notices. The simplest path is often built into a tool you already use, such as a rule in your email client or a flow in Microsoft Power Automate, before you reach for a separate connector service. Every automation runs as some account, and it can see and change everything that account can. That means an automation built under your own login can read your whole mailbox, and it stops working when you leave or change your password. In this course, a permission is "Access it needs" when a step in your description uses it, and "Access it does not need" when no step does. The aim is to grant the first and refuse the second.
- **Worked example.** The input is the invoice description from Lesson 2 and the connector's request for permissions when the office manager starts building in Power Automate: "Read and write all mail in your mailbox. Read and write all files you can access. Post messages in Teams as you." The output is a note the office manager sends to the IT administrator: "This flow only needs to read the invoices@ shared mailbox, write to the Finance Incoming folder, and post in one Teams channel. Can we run it under a service account with only those permissions, rather than under my login?" The reading is that the default would have given the automation access to everything the office manager can see, and would have tied the flow to one person's account. Asking the question before building is cheaper than unpicking the permissions later.
- **Practice.** You list every place your own description reads from or writes to, and next to each one you write which account you intend the automation to run under.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "An automation copies new rows from the bookings form into the events calendar. Mark each permission it asked for with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Access it needs" and "Access it does not need".
  - "Read responses to the Bookings form." The correct label is "Access it needs". The feedback for a right answer is: "The trigger reads the form, so this permission is needed." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The automation starts from the form. Without this access it cannot run."
  - "Create events in the Events calendar." The correct label is "Access it needs". The feedback for a right answer is: "The step writes to this calendar, so it needs this access." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The only step writes to the Events calendar, so it needs to be able to create events there."
  - "Read and send email as you." The correct label is "Access it does not need". The feedback for a right answer is: "No step sends email, so this access should be refused." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at the description again. No step reads or sends email."
  - "Read all files in your OneDrive." The correct label is "Access it does not need". The feedback for a right answer is: "No step uses your files, so this access should be refused." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The automation moves a form response into a calendar. Your files play no part in it."
- **Bridge.** Once it is built, the next lesson shows you how to run it on real cases before anyone relies on it.

#### Lesson 4. Run it on real cases

- The id is `run-it-on-real-cases`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It is the difference between an automation that works in a demonstration and one that works on a Monday morning.
- **Core idea.** Before you switch an automation on for real, you run it on a small set of cases taken from actual recent work, and you check what happened at every step. The set must include four kinds of case: a normal one, one with a missing or empty field, a duplicate of something already handled, and an unusual one, such as a very long name, an attachment in the wrong format, or two items in one message. A test is not the automation running without an error message, because many automations fail quietly by doing the wrong thing successfully. For each case, you record "Handled correctly" when every step did what the description said, and "Handled wrongly" when any step did not, even if nothing reported an error.
- **Worked example.** The input is four emails the office manager sent to the invoices@ mailbox: a normal invoice with one PDF, an email with no attachment, the same invoice sent twice, and an email with two PDFs, an invoice and a delivery note. The output is the test log: "Normal: saved and posted, handled correctly. No attachment: moved to Check, handled correctly. Duplicate: saved twice with the same name, the second overwrote the first, and two messages were posted, handled wrongly. Two PDFs: both saved, and the delivery note was posted as if it were an invoice, handled wrongly." The reading is that the automation reported success on all four, and two were wrong. The duplicate case could lose an invoice silently, and the second case would send finance a delivery note to pay. Both need a condition added before it goes live.
- **Practice.** You write down four real cases for your own automation, one of each kind, and predict what should happen at each step.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "An automation should add each new form response to the tracker and email the manager. Here is what happened in four tests. Mark each result with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Handled correctly" and "Handled wrongly".
  - "A normal response was added as one row and the manager received one email." The correct label is "Handled correctly". The feedback for a right answer is: "Every step did what the description said." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check each step. One row was added and one email was sent, which is what the description asked for."
  - "A response with no manager email was added to the tracker, and the flow reported success." The correct label is "Handled wrongly". The feedback for a right answer is: "The flow reported success but nobody was told, so this is a quiet failure." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Success in the log is not the test. The manager was never emailed, so a step did not do what it should."
  - "A response submitted twice by accident created two rows." The correct label is "Handled wrongly". The feedback for a right answer is: "A duplicate should not become a second row, so the automation needs a condition for it." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Two rows for one request will be counted twice. That is handled wrongly even though nothing broke."
  - "A response with a very long course name was added in full and the email showed the whole name." The correct label is "Handled correctly". The feedback for a right answer is: "The unusual case passed through every step intact." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at the result. The long name survived both steps, so the case was handled correctly."
- **Bridge.** Every automation eventually breaks, and the next lesson shows you how to write down what happens when yours does.

#### Lesson 5. When it breaks

- The id is `when-it-breaks`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the fourth module of the outline. It prepares the failure note, which is the part that protects the work when you are not there.
- **Core idea.** An automation breaks for ordinary reasons: a password changes, a form gains a new question, a folder is renamed, a licence lapses, or the person who built it leaves. A failure note has five parts: what the automation does in one sentence, how someone will know it has stopped, who is told and who owns it, what people do by hand until it is fixed, and how to switch it off. It is not a technical manual, and it should be readable by the colleague who covers for you on holiday. The part most often missing is how someone will know, because a quiet failure can run for weeks before anyone notices that the tracker has stopped growing.
- **Worked example.** The input is the office manager's first note: "If it breaks, let me know." The output is the repaired failure note: "What it does: saves invoices from invoices@ into Finance Incoming and posts a link in the Finance channel. How we know it stopped: Power Automate emails the owner when a run fails, and the finance assistant checks on Friday that the week's invoices in the mailbox match the files in Incoming. Owner: the office manager, and the finance lead when she is away. By hand in the meantime: the finance assistant saves attachments from the mailbox into Incoming each morning. Switch it off: open the flow in Power Automate and choose Turn off; nothing else depends on it." The reading is that the first note relied on the automation's author noticing, which does not work when the author is on leave. The repaired note gives a second person a weekly check and a way to keep the work moving.
- **Practice.** You write the "How we know it stopped" part for your own automation, naming a check a person can do without opening the automation tool.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this failure note so that a colleague could use it while you are on holiday. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "This flow sends the weekly rota email to all staff. It usually works. Speak to Dan if there is a problem." A correct edit includes what it does, how someone will know it has stopped, an owner and a deputy, what people do by hand in the meantime, and how to switch it off. The feedback for a correct edit is: "The note now says what the flow does, how someone will notice when it stops, who owns it and who covers, what to do by hand, and how to switch it off." The feedback when how they will know is missing is: "The note still does not say how anyone will notice that the rota email did not go out." The feedback when the deputy is missing is: "Dan is named, but nobody covers for him. Add who to speak to when Dan is away." The feedback when the manual step is missing is: "Say what someone does to send the rota by hand until it is fixed." The feedback when switching off is missing is: "Add how to switch the flow off, so a broken version cannot keep sending."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you put your description, your test log and your failure note together into the automation note that goes on your record.

#### Lesson 6. Your automation note

- The id is `your-automation-note`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the final lesson of the course, and the note you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** The automation note is one page that describes a real automation you have built and tested. It has four sections: the description, with trigger, steps and conditions; the access, listing the account it runs under and what it can reach; the test log, with four cases and whether each was handled correctly; and the failure note with all five parts. It is not a screenshot of the flow, because a screenshot does not tell a colleague why it was built that way or what to do when it fails. If your test found a case that was handled wrongly, the note must say what you changed and show the case passing afterwards.
- **Worked example.** The input is the coordinator's training request automation. The output is a note whose test log reads: "Normal: handled correctly. Missing email: stopped and emailed the coordinator, handled correctly. Duplicate: first run created two rows; I added a condition that checks for an existing row with the same email and course in the same month, and the rerun created one row, handled correctly. Unusual, a request for two courses in one response: handled wrongly, only the first course was recorded; the form now allows one course per response and says so." The reading is that the note records two failures honestly and what was done about each, which gives a colleague far more confidence than a log of four passes would.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. The repaired failure note from Lesson 5 stays open in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the automation note for the automation you built. Include all four sections." The fields are labelled "Description", "Access", "Test log" and "Failure note". The note passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your note describes the trigger, steps and conditions, names the account and its access, records four real cases, and gives a colleague what they need when it breaks." When a section fails, the feedback names it, for example: "The test log has no duplicate case" or "The failure note does not say how anyone will know it has stopped." After a pass, you type your name to sign the note.

## 4. From Spreadsheets to Simple Systems

The slug is `from-spreadsheets-to-simple-systems`. The course runs for two and a half hours and costs £99. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: What the file is doing, The dangerous parts, A simpler shape, and What you move.

### Who it is for

This course is for people whose team depends on a spreadsheet that has grown over years, such as a tracker, a pricing workbook, a rota or a monthly report, and who are worried about what would happen if it broke or its author left. You should be able to open the workbook, move between its sheets, and click into a cell to see its formula. You do not need to write macros or know any programming.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can describe what each part of a workbook is doing, trace one important number from the result back to where it was typed in, point to the parts that could quietly produce a wrong answer, sketch a simpler shape for the data, and decide which parts should stay in the spreadsheet, which should move to a list or a system, and which should be retired. You leave with a one-page map of the workbook that a colleague could use.

### Learning objectives

1. Classify each sheet in a workbook by the role it plays: holding the records, doing the sums, or showing the result.
2. Trace one number in a report back to every cell and input it depends on.
3. Identify the parts of a workbook that could produce a wrong answer without anyone noticing.
4. Propose a simpler shape for the data, with one table per kind of thing and one row per record.
5. Decide, with a reason, what stays in the workbook, what moves to a system, and what is retired.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. What the file is doing

- The id is `what-the-file-is-doing`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson is the first module of the outline. It gives you three words for the jobs a workbook does, which the rest of the course uses.
- **Core idea.** Most long-lived workbooks do three different jobs at once, often on the same sheet. A sheet "Holds the records" when it is the place where facts are typed in and kept, such as one row for each order or each member of staff. A sheet "Does the sums" when it takes those records and works something out, such as prices, totals or dates. A sheet "Shows the result" when it lays out the answer for someone to read, such as a summary for the monthly meeting. A workbook is not dangerous because it is a spreadsheet; it becomes dangerous when these jobs are tangled, so that a person typing in a record can overwrite a formula, or a person reading the result cannot tell where it came from. Naming the job of each sheet is the first step towards untangling them.
- **Worked example.** The input is a list of sheets in a small charity's grants workbook: "Grants 2024", "Grants 2025", "Calc", "Board summary", "Lookup", and "Sheet7". The output is the manager's labelled list: "Grants 2024 and Grants 2025 hold the records, one row per grant. Calc does the sums, working out the amount remaining on each grant. Lookup holds the records, a short list of funders and their reporting dates. Board summary shows the result. Sheet7 does the sums as well, and turned out to hold a second calculation of the remaining amounts that the Board summary actually uses." The reading is that labelling each sheet found something nobody knew: the number the board sees comes from Sheet7, not from Calc. Two calculations of the same thing is a warning sign that the next lessons will come back to.
- **Practice.** You list the sheets in your own workbook and label each one with one of the three jobs, adding a note where a sheet does more than one.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four sheets come from a sales team's pricing workbook. Mark each sheet with the job it does." The three controls read "Holds the records", "Does the sums" and "Shows the result".
  - "Products: one row per product, with its code, description and list price, typed in by the product manager." The correct label is "Holds the records". The feedback for a right answer is: "Facts are typed in and kept here, so this sheet holds the records." The feedback for "Does the sums" is: "Nothing is worked out on this sheet. Prices are typed in, which means it holds the records." The feedback for "Shows the result" is: "This sheet is where the facts are entered, not where an answer is laid out for someone to read."
  - "Quote: the salesperson picks products from a list and the sheet shows the total with discount and VAT, ready to paste into an email." The correct label is "Shows the result". The feedback for a right answer is: "This sheet lays out an answer for someone to read and send." The feedback for "Holds the records" is: "Quotes are not kept here; each new quote replaces the last. The sheet shows a result." The feedback for "Does the sums" is: "There are sums behind it, but the job of this sheet is to present the quote. Look for where the discount rules actually live."
  - "Discounts: formulas that look up each customer's band and work out the discount rate." The correct label is "Does the sums". The feedback for a right answer is: "This sheet works something out from other records." The feedback for "Holds the records" is: "The rates here are calculated, not typed in, so this sheet does the sums." The feedback for "Shows the result" is: "Nobody reads this sheet directly. It works out the rate that the Quote sheet uses."
  - "Customers: one row per customer, with their name and discount band." The correct label is "Holds the records". The feedback for a right answer is: "Each customer is typed in once and kept here." The feedback for "Does the sums" is: "The band is typed in rather than worked out, so this sheet holds the records." The feedback for "Shows the result" is: "This sheet is a list of facts other sheets use, not a result for someone to read."
- **Bridge.** Knowing the job of each sheet lets you follow a single number through them, which is the next lesson.

#### Lesson 2. Trace one number

- The id is `trace-one-number`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson expands the first module. It shows you how a single result depends on the rest of the workbook.
- **Core idea.** To trace a number, you start at a result someone relies on, click into it, and follow each cell its formula refers to, back and back, until every path ends in a value someone typed in. Excel's Trace Precedents tool and Google Sheets' formula highlighting can help, but a written trace is what matters. A trace is not a check that the formula is correct; it is a map of what the number depends on, which tells you what could make it wrong. It ends with a short list of the typed-in values behind the result and who types each one. A good trace often finds a value that nobody remembers typing, such as a rate from three years ago sitting inside a formula.
- **Worked example.** The input is the charity's Board summary cell showing "£48,200 remaining across all grants". The output is the trace: "The cell adds column F on Sheet7. Each row of column F subtracts the amount spent, from Grants 2025 column E, from the award, from Grants 2025 column C. Column E is typed in by the finance officer each month from the accounting system. Column C is typed in when a grant is awarded. One row in Sheet7 subtracts a fixed £3,000 typed directly into the formula, with no note." The reading is that the trace found the one number the board relies on, the two typed-in columns it depends on, and a hidden £3,000 that nobody can explain. That hidden value is exactly the kind of dangerous part the next lesson is about.
- **Practice.** You choose one result in your own workbook and write the first two steps of its trace, naming the cells or columns it refers to.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two colleagues traced the monthly overtime total in the same rota workbook. Choose the trace you could rely on." The two controls read "Trace A" and "Trace B". Trace A reads: "The total comes from the Hours sheet. It looks right, and it matched last month roughly." Trace B reads: "The total on Summary adds column H on Hours. Column H multiplies overtime hours in column G by the rate in cell Rates!B2. Column G is typed in by each shift lead. Rates!B2 is typed in by HR and was last changed in April. Two rows in column H multiply by 15.5 typed into the formula instead of using Rates!B2." The correct answer is Trace B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Trace B follows every reference back to a typed-in value, names who types it, and finds the rows that ignore the rate cell." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Trace A names a sheet and a feeling. It does not follow the formula back to what was typed in, so it cannot tell you what might make the total wrong."
- **Bridge.** Your trace will have turned up at least one surprise, and the next lesson names the kinds of surprise that make a workbook dangerous.

#### Lesson 3. The dangerous parts

- The id is `the-dangerous-parts`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It gives you a short list of what to look for, so you can judge the risk of a workbook quickly.
- **Core idea.** A part of a workbook is a "Dangerous part" when it could produce a wrong answer without anyone noticing, or when the work would stop if one person were away. The common ones are numbers typed directly into formulas, figures pasted in by hand from another file each week, ranges in a formula that stop short of the newest rows, two calculations of the same thing that could disagree, macros or complex formulas that only one person understands, and personal data kept where too many people can see it. A part is "Safe as it is" when it does one job clearly and a mistake in it would be visible. Dangerous does not mean wrong today, and many dangerous parts give the right answer for years. It means that when they go wrong, nothing tells you.
- **Worked example.** The input is the rota workbook from the check in Lesson 2. The output is the list the HR adviser made: "Dangerous: two rows multiply by 15.5 typed into the formula, so a rate change will not reach them. Dangerous: the total adds H2 to H200, and the rota now has 214 rows. Dangerous: the Hours sheet includes staff home addresses in a hidden column, and the file is shared with all shift leads. Safe as it is: the Rates sheet has one clearly labelled cell per rate, changed only by HR." The reading is that none of these dangerous parts shows an error message. The total has been quietly leaving out fourteen people's overtime since the rota grew, and nobody would have found it without looking.
- **Practice.** You look at the trace from your own Lesson 2 practice and mark each step as a dangerous part or safe as it is, with one sentence of reason.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "An operations lead listed four features of their stock workbook. Mark each one with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Dangerous part" and "Safe as it is".
  - "The reorder quantity formula is =B4*1.15, where 1.15 is the safety margin agreed two years ago." The correct label is "Dangerous part". The feedback for a right answer is: "The margin is typed inside the formula, so if it changes, nobody will know which cells to update." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look inside the formula. A number typed there cannot be seen or changed in one place, which makes it a dangerous part."
  - "Every Monday, Lee copies the stock levels from the warehouse system export and pastes them into column C." The correct label is "Dangerous part". The feedback for a right answer is: "A weekly paste by one person can be missed, pasted into the wrong rows, or stop when Lee is away." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Think about the week Lee is on holiday, or pastes one row out of line. Nothing would warn you."
  - "Supplier names are chosen from a drop-down list linked to the Suppliers sheet." The correct label is "Safe as it is". The feedback for a right answer is: "The drop-down stops misspelt names, and a mistake would be visible." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "A drop-down protects the data rather than risking it. This part does one job clearly."
  - "There is a macro that rebuilds the summary, and only Dana knows what it does." The correct label is "Dangerous part". The feedback for a right answer is: "If Dana is away or leaves, the summary cannot be rebuilt or checked." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The lesson counts work that depends on one person as a dangerous part, and nobody else understands this macro."
- **Bridge.** Many dangerous parts come from the shape of the data itself, so the next lesson shows you a simpler shape.

#### Lesson 4. A simpler shape

- The id is `a-simpler-shape`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It gives you a shape for data that is safer inside a spreadsheet and much easier to move out of one.
- **Core idea.** A simpler shape has one table for each kind of thing, such as customers, orders or staff, with one row for each record and one column for each fact. It keeps the records separate from the sums and the results, so typing in a new row never touches a formula. It avoids merged cells, blank rows used for spacing, totals mixed in among the records, and a new sheet or a new column for each month. In Excel this usually means formatting each list as a Table, so formulas and totals grow with the data, and in Google Sheets it means keeping each list on its own sheet with a single header row. This shape is not about appearance, and a well-shaped table can look plain. It is the shape that a list tool, a database or almost any system can import without being rebuilt.
- **Worked example.** The input is the charity's two record sheets, "Grants 2024" and "Grants 2025", each with a coloured header, a blank row between funders, subtotals under each funder, and a new column for every month's spending. The output is one Grants table with the columns Grant reference, Funder, Award date, Award amount and End date, and one Spending table with the columns Grant reference, Month and Amount spent, with one row for each grant in each month. The Board summary now works out the remaining amount from these two tables. The reading is that the new shape needs no new sheet next year, removes the second calculation on Sheet7, and could be imported into a list tool such as Microsoft Lists or a finance system as it stands.
- **Practice.** You sketch the columns of one table from your own workbook in the simpler shape, writing the column names in a single line.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two colleagues redesigned the same training attendance sheet. Choose the layout with the simpler shape." The two controls read "Layout A" and "Layout B". Layout A reads: "One sheet per course. Each sheet has the course name in a merged cell at the top, then one column per session date with a tick for each person who attended, and a total row at the bottom." Layout B reads: "One Courses table with Course code, Title and Provider. One Attendance table with Course code, Session date, Staff ID and Attended (Yes or No), one row per person per session. Totals are worked out on a separate Summary sheet." The correct answer is Layout B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Layout B has one table per kind of thing, one row per record, and keeps the totals away from the records." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Layout A adds a sheet for every course and a column for every date, merges cells, and mixes a total into the records. Each of those makes it harder to keep right and to move."
- **Bridge.** Once the data has a simple shape, you can decide which parts should leave the workbook altogether, which is the next lesson.

#### Lesson 5. What you move

- The id is `what-you-move`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the fourth module of the outline. It gives you three decisions for each part of the workbook and a reason for each.
- **Core idea.** Each part of the workbook gets one of three decisions. A part "Moves to a system" when several people need to add records at the same time, when others depend on it as the official record, when you need a history of who changed what, or when it holds personal data that should be restricted. The system can be modest, such as a shared list in a tool you already pay for, and it does not have to be a new purchase. A part "Stays in the workbook" when one person or a small group uses it for their own analysis or planning, where the flexibility of a spreadsheet is the point. A part should "Retire it" when nobody uses its result any more, which is more common than people expect. Moving is not the goal in itself, and moving a badly shaped sheet simply moves the problem.
- **Worked example.** The input is the charity's reshaped workbook. The output is the decision list: "The Grants table moves to a system, a Microsoft List, because the fundraising team and finance both add to it and it is the official record of what was awarded. The Spending table stays in the workbook for now, because only the finance officer updates it, from the accounting system. The Board summary stays in the workbook, because it is one person's report. Sheet7 is retired, because its calculation now comes from the two tables. The Lookup of funder reporting dates moves to a system, into the same List, as columns on each grant." The reading is that the decision is made part by part, each with a reason from the lesson, and only two things move.
- **Practice.** You take three parts of your own workbook and give each one a decision with one sentence of reason.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "An HR team lead is deciding what to do with parts of their people workbook. Mark each part with the decision it deserves." The three controls read "Moves to a system", "Stays in the workbook" and "Retire it".
  - "The absence log, which six managers update, and which payroll uses each month." The correct label is "Moves to a system". The feedback for a right answer is: "Several people add to it and payroll depends on it as the record, so it should move." The feedback for "Stays in the workbook" is: "Six managers editing a shared file that payroll relies on is exactly the case the lesson said should move." The feedback for "Retire it" is: "Payroll uses this every month, so it is very much alive."
  - "The HR lead's own sheet for modelling next year's headcount under different budgets." The correct label is "Stays in the workbook". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is one person's analysis, where the flexibility of a spreadsheet is the point." The feedback for "Moves to a system" is: "Nobody else adds to this sheet, and it is not an official record. It is one person's modelling." The feedback for "Retire it" is: "This sheet is in active use for planning, so there is no reason to retire it."
  - "A 2019 tab of training costs that nothing refers to and nobody has opened since." The correct label is "Retire it". The feedback for a right answer is: "Nobody uses its result, so it can be retired." The feedback for "Moves to a system" is: "Moving an unused tab only moves clutter. Nobody uses its result." The feedback for "Stays in the workbook" is: "Keeping an unused tab leaves one more place for confusion. Nobody uses its result."
  - "The staff list with home addresses and emergency contacts, visible to everyone who opens the file." The correct label is "Moves to a system". The feedback for a right answer is: "Personal data that should be restricted belongs somewhere access can be controlled." The feedback for "Stays in the workbook" is: "Everyone who opens this file can see home addresses. The lesson said restricted personal data should move." The feedback for "Retire it" is: "The team needs emergency contacts. The problem is who can see them, which is a reason to move them."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you bring your labels, trace, dangerous parts, shape and decisions together into a one-page map of your workbook.

#### Lesson 6. Your workbook map

- The id is `your-workbook-map`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the final lesson of the course, and the map you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** The workbook map is one page about a real workbook your team depends on. It names the workbook and its owner, lists each sheet with the job it does, gives the trace of one important number, lists the dangerous parts you found, gives the simpler shape for at least one table, and records a decision for each part with its reason. It is not a request to replace the spreadsheet, and a map that concludes most of it should stay is a valid map. The test is whether a colleague could pick up the workbook tomorrow, using only your map, and know where the risks are and what is planned.
- **Worked example.** The input is the HR adviser's notes on the rota workbook. The output is a map that records the three sheets and their jobs, traces the overtime total to the shift leads' hours and the HR rate cell, lists the two hard-coded rates, the short range and the hidden addresses as dangerous parts, proposes a single Shifts table with Staff ID, Date, Hours and Overtime hours, and decides that the addresses move to the HR system, the Shifts table moves to a shared list, and the summary stays in the workbook. The reading is that the map is short, specific and honest about what it will not change, which makes it something a manager can approve.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. Your answers from the earlier practices are shown in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the map for one workbook your team depends on. Include every section." The fields are labelled "Workbook and owner", "Sheets and their jobs", "One number traced", "Dangerous parts", "A simpler shape" and "What stays, moves or retires". The map passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your map names the job of each sheet, traces a number to what was typed in, lists the dangerous parts, gives a simpler shape, and decides each part with a reason." When a section fails, the feedback names it, for example: "The trace does not reach a typed-in value" or "Two parts have a decision but no reason." After a pass, you type your name to sign the map.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed map is on your record and suggests you save a copy beside the workbook.

### The artefact and the signed record

## 5. Data Skills for People Who Are Not Analysts

The slug is `data-skills-for-people-who-are-not-analysts`. The course runs for two and a half hours and costs £99. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: The claim, Filters and missing rows, The question before you act, and Your checklist.

### Who it is for

This course is for managers, coordinators and specialists who are sent tables, dashboards and summary figures and are expected to act on them, but who are not analysts and do not build the numbers themselves. You should be comfortable opening a spreadsheet, sorting and filtering a column, and reading a simple chart. You do not need any statistics.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can state exactly what a number claims, find out where it came from, spot when rows have been filtered out or are missing, tell a fair comparison from an unfair one, and ask the person who owns the data the one question that matters before you act. You leave with a short checklist you have already used on a real table from your own work.

### Learning objectives

1. Restate any figure as a full claim: what was counted, of whom, over what period, and compared with what.
2. Record where a number came from, including its source, its definition, the date it was taken and who owns it.
3. Identify when a total or average has been affected by filters, blanks or missing rows.
4. Distinguish a fair comparison from an unfair one, and say what would make it fair.
5. Write the question you would send to the data owner before acting, and apply a checklist to a real table.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. A number is a claim

- The id is `a-number-is-a-claim`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson is the first module of the outline. It changes how you read every figure in the rest of the course.
- **Core idea.** A number on its own says very little; it becomes useful when you can state the full claim it makes. A full claim says what was counted, of whom or of what, over what period, and compared with what. "Complaints are up 30%" becomes "The number of complaints logged in the customer system in March was 30% higher than in February." A number is not wrong just because it is incomplete, but an incomplete claim lets each reader fill the gap with their own assumption, so two people can act on the same figure for opposite reasons. In this course, a sentence either "States the claim fully" or "Leaves the claim open".
- **Worked example.** The input is a line in a weekly operations email: "Late deliveries are down to 4%." The output is the manager's restatement after asking: "Of orders dispatched from the Leeds warehouse in the week ending 14 June, 4% arrived after the date promised to the customer, compared with 7% in the previous week. Orders from the Bristol warehouse are not included, because its data has not been loaded since May." The reading is that the short line was true, and it left out two things that change the decision: which warehouse it covered, and that the other warehouse, which had the complaints, was not in it at all.
- **Practice.** You take one figure you were sent this week and rewrite it as a full claim, writing "Not stated" for any part you do not know.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four sentences come from a monthly people report. Mark each sentence with the label it deserves." The two controls read "States the claim fully" and "Leaves the claim open".
  - "Staff turnover is 18%." The correct label is "Leaves the claim open". The feedback for a right answer is: "It does not say who is counted, over what period, or compared with what." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check the four parts. This sentence gives a figure but not who is counted, the period or the comparison."
  - "Of permanent staff in post on 1 April last year, 18% had left by 31 March this year, compared with 14% the year before." The correct label is "States the claim fully". The feedback for a right answer is: "It says who was counted, over what period, and compared with what." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look again. Who, what, when and the comparison are all in this sentence."
  - "Sickness absence is much higher in the north." The correct label is "Leaves the claim open". The feedback for a right answer is: "It gives no figure, no period and no definition of the north." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "'Much higher' and 'the north' leave the reader to supply the claim."
  - "In the three northern offices, staff took an average of 6.2 days of sickness absence each between January and June, compared with 4.1 days across the other offices." The correct label is "States the claim fully". The feedback for a right answer is: "The places, the measure, the period and the comparison are all stated." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This sentence names the offices, the measure, the period and what it is compared with."
- **Bridge.** Once you can state the claim, the next lesson asks where the number came from.

#### Lesson 2. Where the number came from

- The id is `where-the-number-came-from`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson expands the first module. It gives you four facts to find out about any figure before you rely on it.
- **Core idea.** Every number has a source, a definition, a date and an owner. The source is the system or survey it was taken from. The definition is exactly what counts, such as whether a "customer" means an account or a person, or whether a "late" delivery means late against the promised date or the requested one. The date is when the data was taken, which may be weeks before the report was sent. The owner is the person or team who can answer questions about it. This is not about distrusting colleagues; most errors in reports are honest ones, such as an old extract or a definition that changed. A note is "Good enough to use" when all four facts are there, and "Not yet known" when any is missing.
- **Worked example.** The input is a dashboard tile: "Active customers: 12,480." The output is the note the manager wrote after asking the business intelligence team: "Source: the billing system. Definition: an account with at least one paid invoice in the last ninety days; one company with three accounts counts three times. Date: extracted on the first of the month, so it is up to four weeks old. Owner: the BI team, through the data requests channel." The reading is that the number is fine for tracking a trend, but it is not the number of companies the sales team works with, and it would be the wrong figure for a plan that counted customers as companies.
- **Practice.** You write the four facts for the figure you restated in Lesson 1, marking any you do not know as not yet known.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two colleagues each noted where the same waiting-time figure came from. Choose the note that is good enough to use." The two controls read "Note A" and "Note B". Note A reads: "From the system, it's the official figure, and it was in last week's pack." Note B reads: "Source: the booking system. Definition: days from referral to first appointment, for referrals that have had a first appointment; referrals still waiting are not included. Date: extract taken on 3 June. Owner: the performance team." The correct answer is Note B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Note B gives the source, the definition, the date and the owner, and its definition reveals that people still waiting are left out." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Note A does not say which system, what counts, when it was taken or who owns it. All four facts are still not yet known."
- **Bridge.** The definition in Note B hinted at rows being left out, which is the subject of the next lesson.

#### Lesson 3. Filters and missing rows

- The id is `filters-and-missing-rows`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It covers the most common way a correct-looking total turns out to be wrong.
- **Core idea.** A total or average is only as complete as the rows behind it. Rows can go missing in several ordinary ways: a filter was left on in a spreadsheet, an export stopped at a fixed number of rows, blank cells were skipped by an average, a date range cut off the last few days, or records that failed a rule were quietly dropped. In Excel, a plain SUM adds hidden rows as well as visible ones, while SUBTOTAL and AGGREGATE can be set to ignore hidden rows, so two cells can show different totals for what looks like the same list. This is not a reason to distrust every table, but it is a reason to know how many rows there should be. In this course, a figure "Counts every row" when all the rows it should include are included, and "Rows are missing" when any are not.
- **Worked example.** The input is a sales table sent by a colleague, showing total orders of £182,000 for the quarter. The output is what the manager found in five minutes: "The table has 1,000 rows, exactly. The export tool has a limit of 1,000 rows, and the sales system shows 1,247 orders for the quarter. The Region column has a filter set to exclude 'Unknown'. The average order value skips 38 rows where the value is blank because the order was cancelled." The reading is that three separate things were changing the figures, and none of them showed an error. The round number of rows was the first clue.
- **Practice.** You open one table you were sent, check whether any filter is on, count the rows, and write one sentence on how many rows you expected.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A colleague describes how four figures in a report were produced. Mark each description with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Counts every row" and "Rows are missing".
  - "The total of overdue invoices was taken from the full ledger export, and the row count matches the number shown in the finance system." The correct label is "Counts every row". The feedback for a right answer is: "The row count was checked against the source, so the figure includes every row it should." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This description checks the rows against the system, which is exactly what the lesson asked for."
  - "The average response time was worked out after filtering the sheet to show only tickets marked 'Closed'." The correct label is "Rows are missing". The feedback for a right answer is: "Open tickets, which may be the slowest, are left out." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The filter removes every ticket that is not closed. Ask whether the slowest ones are among them."
  - "The export was capped at 5,000 rows, and the export returned exactly 5,000." The correct label is "Rows are missing". The feedback for a right answer is: "An export that stops exactly at the cap has almost certainly left rows behind." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "A round number equal to the limit is the warning sign from the worked example."
  - "Headcount was taken from the HR system on the last day of the month, including part-time and fixed-term staff, as the report's definition says." The correct label is "Counts every row". The feedback for a right answer is: "The figure uses the definition and includes the groups it names." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Nothing is filtered out here that the definition says should be included."
- **Bridge.** Even a complete figure can mislead when it is set next to the wrong comparison, which is the next lesson.

#### Lesson 4. A fair comparison

- The id is `a-fair-comparison`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson expands the second module. It deals with the comparison half of the claim, where most confident conclusions go wrong.
- **Core idea.** Most decisions rest on a comparison: this month against last month, this team against that one, before a change against after it. A comparison is fair when both sides are counted the same way, over periods of the same length, from groups big enough that a small change is not just chance, and when a percentage is shown with the numbers behind it. It is unfair when the definition changed between the two, when one period is a short month or includes a holiday, when one side is so small that a single case moves it a long way, or when a percentage rise hides a tiny starting number. Unfair does not mean dishonest; it usually means someone put the two numbers side by side without asking. In this course, a comparison is either a "Fair comparison" or an "Unfair comparison", and for an unfair one you say what would make it fair.
- **Worked example.** The input is a headline in a service report: "Complaints at the Harbour Street branch doubled after the new booking system went live." The output is what the manager found: "Harbour Street had three complaints in the month before and six in the month after. The month before was February and the month after was March, which has more trading days. Across all branches, complaints rose by a similar proportion in March." The reading is that the headline was arithmetically correct and still an unfair comparison. Three to six is a change that could easily be chance, the months are different lengths, and the other branches, which did not change systems, moved in the same way. A fair version would compare several months either side, with the other branches alongside.
- **Practice.** You find one comparison in a report you received and write one sentence on whether it is fair, using the tests from the lesson.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four comparisons come from a regional manager's slides. Mark each comparison with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Fair comparison" and "Unfair comparison".
  - "Sales in the four weeks after the price change were 12% lower than in the four weeks before, and the same four weeks last year show no such fall." The correct label is "Fair comparison". The feedback for a right answer is: "The periods are the same length and there is a check against the same weeks last year." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at the periods and the check against last year. Both sides are counted the same way."
  - "Staff satisfaction rose from 62% to 71%, although the survey question was reworded this year." The correct label is "Unfair comparison". The feedback for a right answer is: "A changed question means the two figures are not counted the same way." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "When the question changes, the two numbers measure different things."
  - "Our smallest team's error rate rose by 50%." The correct label is "Unfair comparison". The feedback for a right answer is: "A 50% rise in a small team may be one or two cases, and the numbers behind it are not shown." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Ask how many errors that is. Without the numbers behind the percentage, a small team can move a long way by chance."
  - "The two call centres handled 4,100 and 4,300 calls in June, using the same definition of a resolved call, and resolved 81% and 79% respectively." The correct label is "Fair comparison". The feedback for a right answer is: "Similar volumes, the same month and the same definition make this fair." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check the tests. The period, the definition and the size of each group all match."
- **Bridge.** Knowing a figure may be unfair is only useful if you ask the right question before you act, which is the next lesson.

#### Lesson 5. The question before you act

- The id is `the-question-before-you-act`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It turns your doubts into a single message the data owner can answer quickly.
- **Core idea.** Before you act on a number, ask what you are about to decide and whether a different number would change that decision. If it would not, you can act. If it would, you send the data owner one short message that says what you are deciding, states the claim as you understand it, names the one thing you are unsure of, and asks a question they can answer with a fact. It is not a request to redo the analysis, and it is not a list of every possible concern, because a long list of doubts is easy to put off. The best question is usually about the definition, the missing rows or the comparison.
- **Worked example.** The input is the manager's first draft message: "Hi, are these late-delivery numbers right? They seem off." The output is the edited message: "I'm deciding on Thursday whether to move two drivers from Leeds to Bristol. The report says 4% of orders were late in the week ending 14 June. Does that figure include Bristol, and if not, when will Bristol's data be loaded?" The reading is that the first message asked the owner to check everything and gave them no reason to hurry. The edited one gives the decision, the date, the claim and a question with a factual answer, which the owner can reply to in a minute.
- **Practice.** You write the first sentence of a message to a data owner, stating the decision you are about to make and when.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this message so the data owner can answer it quickly and your decision can go ahead. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "Can you double-check the training completion figures? Something looks wrong and I need to present them." A correct edit states the decision and its date, restates the claim with what was counted and over what period, names the one thing the sender is unsure of, and asks a question that can be answered with a fact. The feedback for a correct edit is: "The message now says what you are deciding and when, restates the claim, and asks one question the owner can answer with a fact." The feedback when the decision is missing is: "The message does not say what you are going to decide, so the owner cannot tell how much it matters." The feedback when the claim is missing is: "Restate the figure you were given, with what was counted and over what period." The feedback when the question is vague is: "'Double-check' asks the owner to redo the work. Ask about one thing: the definition, the missing rows or the comparison."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you collect the tests from this course into a checklist and use it on a real table.

#### Lesson 6. Your checklist

- The id is `your-checklist`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the fourth module of the outline and the final lesson. The checklist you write and apply here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** Your checklist is a short list of questions, in your own words, that you will ask of any table or figure before you act on it. It must cover the claim, where the number came from, the rows behind it, the comparison, and the question you would send. It is not a copy of this course; it is the version you will actually use, so it should be short enough to keep beside your desk. You then apply it to one real table from your own work, answering each question and ending with a decision: "Safe to use", "Safe to use with a caveat", or "Ask first", with the question you would send.
- **Worked example.** The input is the late-delivery table from Lesson 1. The output is the manager's checklist applied to it: "Claim: 4% of Leeds orders late against the promised date, week ending 14 June, against 7% the week before. Source: the dispatch system, extract on 15 June, owned by the logistics analyst. Rows: 2,311 orders, matching the dispatch system; Bristol not loaded. Comparison: fair for Leeds, same definition and period. Decision: ask first, because the driver decision depends on Bristol; the question has been sent." The reading is that the checklist took a few minutes and changed the decision from acting on the headline to waiting a day for the missing half of the picture.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. The worked example stays open in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write your checklist, then apply it to one real table from your work." The first part has five fields labelled "The claim", "Where it came from", "The rows", "The comparison" and "The question", each holding one question in your words. The second part repeats those five fields with your answers for the real table, followed by a field labelled "Decision" with the three options "Safe to use", "Safe to use with a caveat" and "Ask first". The work passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your checklist covers the claim, the source, the rows, the comparison and the question, and you have used it on a real table to reach a decision." When a part fails, the feedback names it, for example: "Your answer on the rows does not say how many there should be" or "You chose Ask first but did not write the question." After a pass, you type your name to sign the work.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed checklist is on your record.

### The artefact and the signed record

The learner produces a five-question checklist and one application of it to a real table, ending in a decision. The signed record shows the learner's signed name, the course title, the date of signing, the checklist, and the applied answers and decision as written. It does not show the table itself.

### How learning is validated

The work passes only when every part does its job. Each checklist question must be a question and must correspond to one of the five areas. In the application, the claim must state what was counted, over what period and compared with what, or mark a part "Not stated". The source answer must include all four facts from Lesson 2 or mark them "Not yet known". The rows answer must say how many rows there are and how many were expected, or how that was checked. The comparison answer must say whether it is fair and why. A decision of "Safe to use with a caveat" must state the caveat, and a decision of "Ask first" must include a question in the form taught in Lesson 5. The learner is reminded not to paste personal data from the table. Each check in Lessons 1 to 5 must also have been passed.

## 6. Security Decisions for Non-Technical Teams

The slug is `security-decisions-for-non-technical-teams`. The course runs for two hours and costs £99. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: What you actually share, What would hurt, Three changes, and The exposure list.

### Who it is for

This course is for team leaders and office managers in small and medium-sized organisations, or in teams within larger ones, who make everyday decisions about how people share accounts, files and devices, often without a security specialist to ask. You should know which systems your team logs in to and how you currently share access with new starters, temporary staff and suppliers. You do not need any technical security knowledge, and this course does not replace the advice of your IT provider or security team.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can list how your team really shares access, rather than how the policy says it should, judge which of those would cause serious harm if misused, recognise the common ways small teams are attacked, and choose three changes that your team can make this week, each with an owner and a date. You leave with an exposure list for your own team.

### Learning objectives

1. List the ways your team actually shares access to accounts, files and devices, including informal ones.
2. Judge, for each item, whether its misuse would cause serious harm or only a nuisance.
3. Recognise phishing, payment diversion, reused passwords and unrevoked access in realistic workplace examples.
4. Select three changes your team can make this week that reduce a serious harm, each with an owner and a date.
5. Write an exposure list that a colleague or IT provider could act on.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. What you actually share

- The id is `what-you-actually-share`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson is the first module of the outline. It asks you to describe how access really works in your team, which is the ground the rest of the course stands on.
- **Core idea.** Access is "Named access" when each person has their own login, their own permissions, and access that is removed when they leave. Access is "Shared access" when more than one person uses the same login, when a password is written on a sticky note or in a shared document, when a file is shared as "anyone with the link", or when a former member of staff or supplier can still get in. Shared access is common for good reasons, such as a single social media account or a supplier portal that only offers one login, and it is not a sign of carelessness. Its problem is that nobody can tell who did what, and nobody can remove one person without changing it for everyone. Listing it honestly is not an admission of fault; it is the only way to decide what to change.
- **Worked example.** The input is an office manager's first answer to "How does your team share access?": "Everyone has their own Microsoft 365 login." The output is the list she wrote after walking round the office: "Everyone has their own Microsoft 365 login, which is named access. The bank's online portal has one login used by the finance officer and me, which is shared access. The company Instagram password is in a pinned message in the team chat, which is shared access. The shared drive's 'Clients' folder is shared with anyone with the link, which is shared access. A former temp still appears in the accounting software user list, which is shared access in the sense that someone outside the team can still get in." The reading is that the first answer was true and described only the part that was already fine. The walk round found four items that the policy did not show.
- **Practice.** You list five ways your team gets into systems, files or devices, and label each one "Named access" or "Shared access".
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A team lead listed four ways their team gets into systems. Mark each one with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Named access" and "Shared access".
  - "Each caseworker logs in to the case system with their own account, and HR removes it on their last day." The correct label is "Named access". The feedback for a right answer is: "Each person has their own login and it is removed when they leave." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look again. Every caseworker has their own account and it is removed on leaving, which is named access."
  - "The courier booking website has one login, and the password is on a card by the printer." The correct label is "Shared access". The feedback for a right answer is: "One login used by several people, with a password on display, is shared access." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Several people use one login here, so nobody can tell who booked what."
  - "The price list is shared as 'anyone with the link can view'." The correct label is "Shared access". The feedback for a right answer is: "Anyone who is sent the link, or finds it, can open the file without logging in." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "'Anyone with the link' means access is not tied to a named person."
  - "A contractor who left in March can still log in to the project board." The correct label is "Shared access". The feedback for a right answer is: "Someone outside the team can still get in, which the lesson counts as shared access." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The contractor has gone, but their access has not. The lesson counts this as access that is not properly named."
- **Bridge.** Not every shared item matters equally, and the next lesson helps you decide which would hurt.

#### Lesson 2. What would hurt

- The id is `what-would-hurt`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It lets you put your list in order, so the changes you make go where they matter.
- **Core idea.** For each item on your list, ask what the worst realistic outcome would be if the wrong person had that access. The outcome is "Serious harm" when money could be moved or paid to the wrong place, when personal data about customers or staff could be seen or taken, when you could be locked out of a system you cannot work without, or when you could lose records you cannot rebuild. The outcome is a "Nuisance" when it would be embarrassing or cost some time but could be put right quickly and cheaply. This is not a formal risk assessment, and it does not ask you to calculate probabilities. It is a quick, honest sort that shows you which few items deserve your attention this week.
- **Worked example.** The input is the office manager's list from Lesson 1. The output is her sort: "The shared bank portal login is serious harm, because a payment could be made and we could not tell who made it. The 'Clients' folder shared by link is serious harm, because it contains client contact details and contracts. The former temp in the accounting software is serious harm, because they could see and change invoices. The Instagram password in the team chat is a nuisance, because the worst case is an embarrassing post that we could delete and recover the account from." The reading is that three of the four items are serious harm, and two of them involve personal data or money. The Instagram account, which people had worried about most, turned out to matter least.
- **Practice.** You take your five items from Lesson 1 and sort each shared item into serious harm or nuisance, with one sentence on the worst realistic outcome.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A practice manager is sorting items on their team's list. Mark each item with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Serious harm" and "Nuisance".
  - "The login for the payroll system is shared between two administrators." The correct label is "Serious harm". The feedback for a right answer is: "Payroll moves money and holds staff personal data." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Think about the worst realistic outcome. Payroll can pay money to the wrong account and exposes staff data."
  - "The password for the office radio streaming account is on the fridge." The correct label is "Nuisance". The feedback for a right answer is: "The worst case is someone changing the station, which is quick to put right." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "No money, personal data or essential system is involved here."
  - "Patient appointment lists are exported to a spreadsheet shared with anyone with the link." The correct label is "Serious harm". The feedback for a right answer is: "Patient information could be seen by anyone who has the link." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The file holds personal data about patients, which the lesson counts as serious harm."
  - "Only one person knows the password to the domain name account, and they are retiring next month." The correct label is "Serious harm". The feedback for a right answer is: "If that account is lost, the website and email could stop and be hard to recover." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Being locked out of the domain could take down email and the website, which you cannot work without."
- **Bridge.** Knowing what would hurt, the next lesson shows you how small teams are usually attacked, so you know where the harm tends to come from.

#### Lesson 3. How it usually goes wrong

- The id is `how-it-usually-goes-wrong`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson links the second and third modules. It connects your list to the attacks that the National Cyber Security Centre describes as common for small organisations.
- **Core idea.** Most harm to small teams does not come from sophisticated hacking. It comes from four ordinary routes. The first is phishing, where an email or message tricks someone into entering a password or opening a harmful file. The second is payment diversion, where a message that appears to come from a supplier or a senior colleague asks for a payment to go to a new bank account. The third is a reused or guessed password, where a password leaked from another website is tried on your email. The fourth is access that was never removed, such as a leaver's account or a link that was never switched off. None of these needs you to understand technology, and all of them are made much less likely by habits you can set, such as checking a bank change by phone on a known number. A response is "Checks by another route" when it confirms a request using a contact you already had, and "Trusts the message" when it relies on anything in the message itself.
- **Worked example.** The input is an email to the finance officer that appears to come from a regular supplier: "Please note our bank details have changed. Kindly update your records and pay the attached invoice to the new account today." The output is what the finance officer did: "I did not reply to the email or call the number in its signature. I rang our usual contact at the supplier on the number in our supplier file. They said they had not changed their bank. I forwarded the email to report@phishing.gov.uk and told the office manager." The reading is that the email looked genuine, and the right response did not depend on spotting that. It depended on a habit: bank changes are confirmed by a known route, never by the message that asks for them.
- **Practice.** You write down one message your team might receive that asks for a password, a payment, or a change of details, and how you would check it by another route.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "An office manager receives a text that appears to come from the managing director, asking them to buy gift cards for a client today and send the codes. Choose the response you would want in your team." The two controls read "Response A" and "Response B". Response A reads: "The office manager replies to the text to ask whether it is genuine, receives a reply saying yes, and buys the cards." Response B reads: "The office manager does not reply to the text, calls the managing director on the number saved in the staff directory, learns the text was not from them, and reports it to the team." The correct answer is Response B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Response B checks by another route, using a contact already held, instead of anything in the message." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Response A trusts the message. Replying to the sender only asks the person who may be the attacker whether they are genuine."
- **Bridge.** With the harms and the routes in view, the next lesson helps you choose the three changes to make this week.

#### Lesson 4. Three changes this week

- The id is `three-changes-this-week`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It turns your list into action you can finish in days rather than months.
- **Core idea.** A good change reduces a serious harm on your list, can be done this week by someone in your team or your IT provider, and has one named owner and a date. The changes that most often meet this test, and that the National Cyber Security Centre's guidance for small organisations recommends, include turning on two-step verification for email and any system that moves money, replacing shared passwords with a password manager that allows shared vaults, removing leavers and switching "anyone with the link" files to named people, confirming payment changes by a known route, and checking that important data is backed up somewhere separate. A change is "A change for this week" when it meets all three tests, and "A project for later" when it needs a budget, a supplier or a decision above your level. A project for later is not a bad idea, and it belongs on the list with a note of who needs to decide.
- **Worked example.** The input is the office manager's list of serious harms. The output is her three changes: "Remove the former temp from the accounting software, owner: finance officer, by Tuesday. Change the 'Clients' folder from anyone with the link to the four account managers by name, owner: me, by Wednesday. Give the finance officer and me our own logins to the bank portal and turn on two-step verification for both, owner: finance officer, by Friday, after checking with the bank that it offers separate users." The reading is that each change removes a serious harm from the list, each has one owner and a date this week, and none needs new money. Moving the whole team to a password manager was also on her list, and she recorded it as a project for later, because it needs a decision on which product to buy.
- **Practice.** You write one change for your own list, with its owner and date, and say which serious harm it reduces.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A team lead proposed four changes. Mark each change with the label it deserves." The two controls read "A change for this week" and "A project for later".
  - "Turn on two-step verification for all staff email accounts, owner: Aisha, by Thursday." The correct label is "A change for this week". The feedback for a right answer is: "It reduces a serious harm, has an owner and a date, and needs no budget." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check the three tests. It reduces a serious harm, has one owner and a date this week, and can be done now."
  - "Move all our systems to a single sign-on platform." The correct label is "A project for later". The feedback for a right answer is: "This needs a supplier, a budget and a decision above team level." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This cannot be done this week without a budget and a supplier, so it is a project for later."
  - "Remove the three leavers who still have access to the CRM, owner: Tom, by Monday." The correct label is "A change for this week". The feedback for a right answer is: "Removing leavers closes access that was never removed, with an owner and a date." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This is quick, reduces a serious harm and has an owner and a date."
  - "Everyone should be more careful with emails." The correct label is "A project for later". The feedback for a right answer is: "It has no owner, no date, and no specific action, so it is not a change for this week." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look for the owner and the date. Without them, this is an intention, not a change."
- **Bridge.** You have the parts of an exposure list, and the next lesson shows you how they fit together by repairing a weak one.

#### Lesson 5. Repair an exposure list

- The id is `repair-an-exposure-list`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson prepares you for the final module. You practise the shape of the list on someone else's work before you write your own.
- **Core idea.** An exposure list has one row for each item of shared access that would cause serious harm, and each row has five parts: what is shared, who can get in, the worst realistic outcome, the change, and the owner with a date. It also has a short note of nuisance items and projects for later, so that nothing you found is lost. It is not a policy document, and it does not certify anything. The parts most often missing are who can actually get in, which people tend to guess, and the owner, which people tend to write as "IT" or "everyone".
- **Worked example.** The input is a weak row: "Shared drive: some folders open. Should fix." The output is the repaired row: "What is shared: the 'Clients' folder on the shared drive, set to anyone with the link. Who can get in: anyone who has been sent the link, including two former account managers and at least one client. Worst realistic outcome: client contact details and contracts seen by people outside the firm. Change: share the folder with the four current account managers by name and switch off link sharing. Owner and date: the office manager, by Wednesday." The reading is that the weak row admitted a problem, while the repaired row lets someone else check that it has been fixed.
- **Practice.** You are shown a row with every part except "Who can get in", and you fill that part in from the description.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this exposure list row so that someone else could check it has been fixed. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "Bank portal: shared. Risky. IT to look at." A correct edit states what is shared, who can get in, the worst realistic outcome, a change for this week, and one named owner with a date. The feedback for a correct edit is: "The row now names what is shared, who can get in, the worst realistic outcome, the change, and one owner with a date." The feedback when who can get in is missing is: "Say which people can use the bank portal login today." The feedback when the outcome is missing is: "'Risky' does not say what would happen. Name the worst realistic outcome." The feedback when the change is vague is: "'Look at' is not a change. Say what will be done." The feedback when the owner is not a person is: "'IT' is not one named owner. Name the person, and the date."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you write the exposure list for your own team.

#### Lesson 6. Your exposure list

- The id is `your-exposure-list`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the fourth module of the outline and the final lesson. The list you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** Your exposure list covers your own team's real access. It has a row with all five parts for every serious harm you found, at least three of which carry a change for this week, and a short note of nuisances and projects for later. It is written for your manager or your IT provider, who should be able to act on it without calling you. It does not say that your team is secure, and it is not evidence of Cyber Essentials or any other scheme; it is a record of what you found and what you are changing. Keep it free of actual passwords, account numbers and personal data, because the list describes access and does not contain it.
- **Worked example.** The input is the office manager's notes from the course. The output is a list with four serious harm rows, three of them carrying changes due that week, a note that the Instagram password is a nuisance to be moved into a password manager later, and a note that a team password manager is a project for later awaiting a decision from the managing director. - **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. The repaired row from Lesson 5 stays open in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the exposure list for your team. Include a full row for every serious harm, and at least three changes for this week." Each row has fields labelled "What is shared", "Who can get in", "Worst realistic outcome", "The change" and "Owner and date", and a final field is labelled "Nuisances and projects for later". The list passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your list names each serious harm, who can get in, the worst realistic outcome, and at least three changes for this week, each with an owner and a date." When a part fails, the feedback names it, for example: "Row two has no owner" or "Only two rows have a change for this week." After a pass, you type your name to sign the list.

## 7. Digital Change for Managers

The slug is `digital-change-for-managers`. The course runs for two hours and costs £129. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: What they stop doing, The first two weeks, How you know, and The plan.

### Who it is for

This course is for line managers whose team is about to change a tool or a way of working, such as moving from email to a shared task board, from paper forms to a system, or from one case management tool to another. The decision to change has usually been made above them, and their job is to make it work in their team. You should know the change that is coming and roughly when. You do not need to understand how the new tool works in detail.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can describe a tool change as the things your people will stop and start doing, name the old habit and the date it ends, anticipate what will be harder in the first two weeks and plan for it, tell an objection that names a real cost from one that asks for reassurance and answer each properly, and say what you will see in the work at the end of week two that tells you the change is holding. You leave with a two-week plan for your own team.

### Learning objectives

1. Restate a tool change as specific things people will stop and start doing in their week.
2. Name the old habit that must stop, and the date and way it will stop.
3. Plan for what will be harder in the first two weeks, with a named person to ask and a lighter load where it matters.
4. Distinguish an objection that names a real cost from one that asks for reassurance, and respond to each appropriately.
5. Define signs in the work that show at the end of week two whether the change is holding.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. A tool change is a change in work

- The id is `a-tool-change-is-a-change-in-work`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson sets out the idea behind the whole course. It shapes how you describe the change in every later lesson.
- **Core idea.** People do not experience a new tool as a list of features. They experience it as a set of things they used to do that they now cannot, and things they now have to do that they did not before. A sentence about the change is "Something they do" when it describes a step in someone's week, such as "you will log each call in the case record before you hang up". A sentence is "Something the tool has" when it describes a capability, such as "the system has real-time dashboards". Announcements are usually written in the second kind, because that is how the tool was sold to the organisation. Your team needs the first kind, because that is what they will feel on Monday morning.
- **Worked example.** The input is the email a manager received from the programme team: "The new case management system goes live on 3 March. It offers a single view of the customer, automated workflows and mobile access." The output is the manager's rewrite for the team: "From 3 March, you will open a case in the new system instead of starting an email thread. When a customer calls, you will find them by postcode rather than by searching your inbox. When you pass a case to a colleague, you will assign it in the system rather than forwarding the email. You will no longer keep the case notes in your own Word file." The reading is that the first email described what the organisation bought. The rewrite describes four things each person will do differently, which is what they need to know and what they may worry about.
- **Practice.** You write three sentences about the change coming to your team, each describing something a person will do differently in their week.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four sentences come from a manager's first draft about a move to a new expenses system. Mark each sentence with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Something they do" and "Something the tool has".
  - "You will photograph each receipt with the app on the day you get it." The correct label is "Something they do". The feedback for a right answer is: "This describes a step in someone's week." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Read the sentence as the person receiving it. It tells them what they will do, and when."
  - "The system includes automated policy checks." The correct label is "Something the tool has". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is a capability of the system, not a step anyone takes." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Nothing in this sentence tells a person what they will do. It describes what the system has."
  - "You will no longer fill in the monthly paper claim form." The correct label is "Something they do". The feedback for a right answer is: "Stopping something is also a change in what people do, and often the one they notice most." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This describes a habit that ends, which is a change in what someone does."
  - "It integrates seamlessly with the finance platform." The correct label is "Something the tool has". The feedback for a right answer is: "This describes the tool, and 'seamlessly' tells your team nothing about their week." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Ask what a person will do differently because of this sentence. The answer is nothing it tells them."
- **Bridge.** Of all the things people will do differently, the one that decides the change is what they stop doing, which is the next lesson.

#### Lesson 2. What they stop doing

- The id is `what-they-stop-doing`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the first module of the outline. It deals with the old habit, which is the part of a change most often left to fade on its own.
- **Core idea.** A new way of working rarely fails because people cannot use it. It fails because the old way is still available, familiar and a little faster, so under pressure people drift back to it. The old habit needs a name, a date on which it stops, and a way it stops, such as a shared inbox that sends an automatic reply pointing to the new form, or a spreadsheet made read-only. Stopping is not a punishment, and it should come after people have had support, not before. But if you never say when the old route closes, you are running two systems indefinitely, and the team will quite reasonably use whichever is easier on the day.
- **Worked example.** The input is the manager's first plan: "We'll use the new case system from 3 March, and people can keep using email while they get used to it." The output is the revised plan: "From 3 March, new cases are opened in the new system. Existing email threads can be finished by email until 17 March. From 17 March, the team inbox sends an automatic reply that gives customers the new contact form, and I will move any case that arrives by email into the system myself and tell the person who sent it." The reading is that the first plan had no end, so email would have stayed the real system. The revised plan gives two weeks of overlap, a date, and a way of stopping that is visible and kind rather than a surprise.
- **Practice.** You name the old habit your team will have to stop, and write the date and the way it will stop.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two managers wrote the same announcement about moving holiday requests to the HR system. Choose the one that says what stops." The two controls read "Announcement A" and "Announcement B". Announcement A reads: "From next month, please use the HR system for holiday requests. It's much easier and gives you a live view of your allowance." Announcement B reads: "From 1 May, please request holiday in the HR system. Until 15 May I will still accept emailed requests and enter them for you. From 15 May, I will reply to emailed requests with the link and will not approve them until they are in the system." The correct answer is Announcement B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Announcement B names the old habit, gives a period of support, and says the date and the way it stops." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Announcement A never says that emailed requests stop, so people will keep sending them."
- **Bridge.** The two weeks around the switch are when the change is won or lost, and the next lesson plans them.

#### Lesson 3. The first two weeks

- The id is `the-first-two-weeks`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It turns the date of the change into a period you have planned for.
- **Core idea.** In the first two weeks of a new tool, most people will be slower, not faster, because they are learning and because the old shortcuts no longer work. A good plan for those two weeks names what will be harder and for whom, lightens the load where it matters, names one person each team member can ask, and sets a short, regular check-in to catch problems while they are small. It is not a training timetable, although training may be part of it, and it is not a message of encouragement. In this course, a plan is "Work people can feel" when each line changes something in someone's week, and "Only an announcement" when it tells people about the change without changing anything for them.
- **Worked example.** The input is the manager's first idea for the two weeks: "Send a reminder on day one. Share the training video. Tell people to shout if they have problems." The output is the revised plan: "Week one: case targets are reduced by a fifth, because every case will take longer to open. Sam, who tested the system, sits with each person for their first two cases. We meet for ten minutes at 9.15 each day to share what went wrong. Week two: targets return to normal on Thursday if the daily check-ins show that cases are being opened in under five minutes. Anything that cannot be done in the system is written on the shared list, and I take the list to the programme team on Friday." The reading is that the first idea told people about the change. The second changes their week: a lighter load, a person beside them, and a daily place for problems to surface.
- **Practice.** You write one line of your own two-week plan that changes something in someone's week, and name who it helps.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two managers planned the first two weeks of a move to a new ordering system. Choose the plan that is work people can feel." The two controls read "Plan A" and "Plan B". Plan A reads: "Day one: all-staff email. Day two: training video link. Day five: reminder email. End of week two: survey on how it went." Plan B reads: "Week one: Priya and Joe each look after half the team and sit with them for their first three orders. Urgent orders can still be phoned through to Priya, who enters them. A ten-minute check-in each afternoon. Week two: phoned orders stop on Monday. The check-in moves to Tuesday and Thursday only." The correct answer is Plan B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Plan B changes people's week: a named helper, a fallback for urgent orders, a regular check-in, and a clear end to the fallback." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Every line of Plan A is only an announcement. Nothing in it changes what anyone does or who helps them."
- **Bridge.** During those two weeks you will hear objections, and the next lesson shows you how to tell which ones should change your plan.

#### Lesson 4. Hearing the objection

- The id is `hearing-the-objection`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson sits between the second and third modules. It prepares you for the conversations that happen in the first two weeks.
- **Core idea.** Objections to a change are information, not obstruction. An objection "Names a real cost" when it points to something the change makes worse in the work, such as a step that now takes three times as long, a case the tool cannot handle, or a customer who will be affected. That kind of objection should change your plan, or go up to the people who can change the tool. An objection "Asks for reassurance" when it expresses worry about the change itself, such as fear of making a mistake in front of others or doubt that the change will last. That kind should be answered honestly and with something concrete, such as a person to ask or a safe place to practise. Treating a real cost as mere worry is how good changes collect bad workarounds. Treating worry as a cost to be fixed leads to endless redesign.
- **Worked example.** The input is two things the manager heard in the first check-in. The first is: "Customers with two addresses can't be set up in the new system, so I've been keeping them in my old spreadsheet." The second is: "I'm worried I'll get it wrong and a customer will be left waiting." The output is the manager's response. For the first, she wrote it on the shared list, asked the programme team for a fix by Friday, and agreed that the spreadsheet could stay for those customers until then. For the second, she arranged for Sam to check that person's first five cases before they were sent. The reading is that the first objection named a real cost, a case the tool could not handle, and it changed the plan. The second asked for reassurance, and it was answered with a person and a safety net, not with a change to the system.
- **Practice.** You write down one objection you expect to hear in your team, label it, and write your response in one sentence.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A manager heard these four comments in the first week of a new rota tool. Mark each comment with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Names a real cost" and "Asks for reassurance".
  - "The tool won't let me swap a shift with someone on a different contract, so I have to ask you every time." The correct label is "Names a real cost". The feedback for a right answer is: "This points to something the tool makes worse in the work, which should change the plan or go to the people who can change the tool." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This comment names a specific step the tool cannot do. That is a real cost, not a worry."
  - "I'm not very good with apps, and I don't want to mess up everyone's rota." The correct label is "Asks for reassurance". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is a worry about making a mistake, best answered with a person to ask and a way to check." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Nothing here says the tool makes the work worse. The speaker is worried about getting it wrong."
  - "Last time we changed systems we went back after three months, so why bother?" The correct label is "Asks for reassurance". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is doubt that the change will last, which needs an honest answer about what is different this time." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This comment is about whether the change will stick, not about a step in the work."
  - "The night shift can't see the rota on their phones because the site has no signal in the warehouse." The correct label is "Names a real cost". The feedback for a right answer is: "A group of staff cannot use the tool where they work, which is a real cost to fix." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This names people who cannot reach the tool at all. That is a real cost, not a request for reassurance."
- **Bridge.** Objections tell you what is going wrong, and the next lesson shows you how to know what is going right.

#### Lesson 5. How you know

- The id is `how-you-know`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It gives you a way to tell, at the end of week two, whether the change is holding.
- **Core idea.** A "Sign in the work" is something you could see in the work itself that would only be true if people had changed what they do, such as "every new case this week was opened in the system" or "no holiday requests arrived by email". A "Count of activity" is a number about the tool, such as logins, pages viewed, or people who finished the training module. Counts of activity are easy to collect and are often what a dashboard shows, but a person can log in every day and still do the real work somewhere else. Signs in the work are what you should look for, and you should decide in advance what you expect to see by the end of week two, so that you are not persuaded afterwards by whatever number is available.
- **Worked example.** The input is the programme team's suggested measure: "Target: 95% of staff logged in by the end of week two." The output is the manager's own measure: "By the end of week two, every case opened in that week has its first note in the new system, not in an email or a Word file. I will check this by picking ten cases at random on the second Friday and looking at where their first note is." The reading is that the login target could be met by a team that was still working entirely in email. The manager's sign could only be met if the work had moved, and it names how she will check it.
- **Practice.** You write one sign in the work that would show your own change is holding at the end of week two, and how you will check it.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A manager wrote these four measures for a move to a shared task board. Mark each measure with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Sign in the work" and "Count of activity".
  - "Everyone in the team has logged in to the board." The correct label is "Count of activity". The feedback for a right answer is: "Logging in shows access, not that the work has moved." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "A person can log in and still track their tasks elsewhere. This is a count of activity."
  - "At Monday's meeting, we run through the board rather than a spoken round of updates, and nobody has a task that is not on it." The correct label is "Sign in the work". The feedback for a right answer is: "This can only be true if people are really using the board for their work." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at what would have to be true. The meeting itself runs from the board, which is a sign in the work."
  - "Ten people completed the board training module." The correct label is "Count of activity". The feedback for a right answer is: "Finishing a module shows training happened, not that the work changed." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Training completion is about the course, not the work. This is a count of activity."
  - "No task requests have arrived by email since the second Monday." The correct label is "Sign in the work". The feedback for a right answer is: "The old route has gone quiet, which is a sign the habit has changed." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This tells you the old habit has stopped, which is a sign in the work."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you put the whole two weeks together into the plan that goes on your record.

#### Lesson 6. Your two-week plan

- The id is `your-two-week-plan`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the fourth module of the outline and the final lesson. The plan you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** Your plan covers the first two weeks of a real change in your team. It states the change as things people will do, names the old habit and the date and way it stops, lists what will be harder and what you will do about it, names who each person can ask, sets the check-in, notes the objections you expect and how you will treat them, and states the sign in the work you expect at the end of week two with how you will check it. It also includes one sentence on what you will not pretend, such as "I will not tell the team it will be quicker in week one". It is not a communications plan, and it does not need the approval of the programme team, although you may share it with them.
- **Worked example.** The input is the case management manager's notes. The output is a plan whose final lines read: "Sign in the work: by the second Friday, every case opened since 3 March has its first note in the new system; I will check ten at random. What I will not pretend: I will not tell the team that the system will save time in the first fortnight, because it will not, and I will not promise that the two-address problem is fixed until the programme team confirms it." The reading is that the plan is specific enough for someone covering the manager's leave to run, and honest enough that the team will believe it.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. Your answers from the earlier practices are shown in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the two-week plan for the change coming to your team. Include every part." The fields are labelled "The change, as things people do", "What they stop doing", "What will be harder, and what you will do", "Who they ask", "The check-in", "Objections you expect", "Sign in the work" and "What you will not pretend". The plan passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your plan describes the change as work, names what stops and when, plans for the hard parts, names who to ask, and sets a sign in the work you will check." When a part fails, the feedback names it, for example: "What they stop doing has no date" or "The sign at the end of week two is a count of logins." After a pass, you type your name to sign the plan.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed plan is on your record and suggests you put the check at the end of week two in your diary.

### The artefact and the signed record

The learner produces a two-week plan with eight parts. The signed record shows the learner's signed name, the course title, the date of signing, and the plan as written.

### How learning is validated

What will be harder must name at least one specific difficulty and one action that changes someone's week, such as a lighter load, a helper or a fallback. Who they ask must be a named person or role. The check-in must have a time and a frequency. Objections must include at least one, labelled as a real cost or a request for reassurance, with a response that matches the label. ## 8. Technology Decisions for Non-Technical Leaders

### Learning objectives

2. Apply five questions to a proposal and identify which it answers and which it leaves open.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. What you are buying

- The id is `what-you-are-buying`.

- **Core idea.** When you approve a technology proposal, you are not buying a product. You are buying a change in how some work is done, and you are taking on obligations: a contract, people's time to run it, a dependence on a supplier, and a responsibility for the data it holds. A sentence in a proposal is an "Outcome in the work" when it says what will be different in the work and for whom, such as "applications will be acknowledged within one working day instead of five". It is a "Feature of the product" when it describes what the product can do, such as "AI-powered triage". A proposal made mostly of features is not necessarily a bad idea, but it leaves you to imagine the outcome, and each person in the room will imagine a different one.
- **Worked example.** The input is the summary of a proposal to a housing association's board: "We recommend Platform X, a cloud-based, AI-enabled tenant engagement platform with omnichannel messaging, a self-service portal and advanced analytics." The output is the director's restatement after one conversation with the proposer: "Tenants will be able to report repairs online at any time, and will get a text when a repair is booked and when the operative is on the way. The repairs team will stop taking most routine reports by phone. The contact centre expects to handle fewer calls chasing repairs." The reading is that the original summary contained no outcome at all. The restatement names three changes in the work, which the board can now ask about, and it exposes an assumption, fewer chasing calls, that the rest of the course will test.
- **Practice.** You take one sentence from a real proposal you have seen and rewrite it as an outcome in the work.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four sentences come from a proposal for a new finance system. Mark each sentence with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Outcome in the work" and "Feature of the product".
  - "The system uses machine learning to match transactions." The correct label is "Feature of the product". The feedback for a right answer is: "This says how the product works, not what will be different in the work." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Ask what changes for someone in the finance team. The sentence does not say."
  - "Month-end close will take three working days instead of eight, freeing the finance team for forecasting." The correct label is "Outcome in the work". The feedback for a right answer is: "It says what will be different, by how much, and for whom." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This names a change in the work and who benefits, which is an outcome."
  - "It provides real-time dashboards." The correct label is "Feature of the product". The feedback for a right answer is: "A dashboard is a capability; the sentence does not say who will use it or what they will do differently." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Nothing in this sentence tells you what anyone will do differently."
  - "Budget holders will see their spending against budget each Monday without asking finance for a report." The correct label is "Outcome in the work". The feedback for a right answer is: "It says who, when, and what they will no longer have to do." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This describes a change in the week of the budget holders and the finance team."
- **Bridge.** With the outcome clear, the next lesson gives you five questions to put to every proposal.

#### Lesson 2. Five questions

- The id is `five-questions`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It gives you one set of questions for every proposal you see, so you do not have to rely on how confident the presenter is.
- **Core idea.** The five questions are these. First, what will be different in the work, and how will we see it? Second, what does this replace, and when does the old way stop? Third, who will run it after launch, and how much of their time will it take? Fourth, what will it cost over its whole life, including people's time, and including leaving? Fifth, what happens if it fails or the supplier fails, and how would we get our data and our work back? The questions are not a test of the proposer, and a good proposal will welcome them. They are not a scoring system either, because one unanswered question can outweigh four good answers. A sentence in the proposal either "Answers a question" with something that could be checked, or "Leaves it open".
- **Worked example.** The input is four sentences from the Platform X business case: "The platform will go live in September. The supplier is a market leader. Licence costs are £38,000 a year on a three-year term. The contact centre will absorb day-to-day administration." The output is the director's reading: the licence cost partly answers the fourth question, but it leaves out implementation, staff time and the cost of leaving at the end of the term. "The contact centre will absorb administration" leaves the third question open, because it names no person and no time. "Market leader" leaves the fifth question open, because it says nothing about what happens if the supplier fails. Nothing answers the second question, because the proposal never says when phone reporting stops. The reading is that a well-presented case answered one question in part. The director now has four specific questions to send back, rather than a general unease.
- **Practice.** You apply the five questions to your own proposal, writing the sentence that answers each, or "Leaves it open".
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four sentences come from a proposal for a new learning platform. Mark each sentence with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Answers a question" and "Leaves it open".
  - "The current intranet training pages will be switched off on 31 January, and all records moved across before then." The correct label is "Answers a question". The feedback for a right answer is: "This says what is replaced and when the old way stops, which answers the second question." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This sentence names the old way and the date it stops. That is the second question answered."
  - "Our partners are highly experienced in digital transformation." The correct label is "Leaves it open". The feedback for a right answer is: "Experience is a claim about the supplier and answers none of the five questions." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check each question. This sentence does not say what changes, who runs it, what it costs or what happens if it fails."
  - "The L&D coordinator will administer the platform, estimated at one day a week in the first quarter and half a day after that." The correct label is "Answers a question". The feedback for a right answer is: "This names who runs it and how much time it takes, which answers the third question." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "A named role and an estimate of time is exactly what the third question asks for."
  - "The platform is secure and resilient." The correct label is "Leaves it open". The feedback for a right answer is: "It does not say what happens if it fails or how you would get your data back." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "'Secure and resilient' is a description. The fifth question asks what happens when something does go wrong."
- **Bridge.** The fifth question leads straight to the next lesson, which asks who owns each kind of failure.

#### Lesson 3. Who owns the failure

- The id is `who-owns-the-failure`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It makes sure that every way the project could go wrong has a person attached to it before you approve it.
- **Core idea.** A technology decision can fail in four different ways, and each needs its own owner. It can fail to be used, so the work stays where it was. It can fail to work, through outages, errors or poor performance. It can expose data, through a breach or a misconfiguration. And the supplier can fail, by going out of business, being bought, or raising prices at renewal. An owner is one named person, not a team or a supplier, who will notice the failure, decide what to do, and tell you. Ownership is not blame, and naming an owner does not mean they caused the failure. It means that when the failure happens, nobody has to ask whose job it is.
- **Worked example.** The input is the ownership section of the Platform X case: "The project will be owned by the Digital Programme Board, with support from the supplier." The output is the director's version: "Not used: the Head of Repairs, who owns the target of most routine repairs reported online and will report monthly. Not working: the IT Service Manager, who holds the supplier to the service levels in the contract. Data exposed: the Data Protection Officer, with the IT Service Manager, following the existing incident process. Supplier failure: the Head of Procurement, who will make sure the contract includes data export on exit and will review the supplier's position before renewal." The reading is that the original gave the whole project to a board that meets quarterly and to the supplier, who cannot own your failure. The revised version names four people, each of whom already has a reason to care.
- **Practice.** You name the owner for each of the four kinds of failure for your own proposal, writing "Not yet named" where you cannot.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two ownership sections were written for the same staff scheduling system. Choose the one you could approve." The two controls read "Section A" and "Section B". Section A reads: "Ownership sits with the Operations Directorate and the supplier's customer success team, who will work in partnership to ensure success." Section B reads: "Not used: the Operations Manager, who will check each month that rotas are published in the system. Not working: the IT Manager, against the supplier's service levels. Data exposed: the Data Protection Officer. Supplier failure: the Finance Director, who will confirm the exit and export terms before signing." The correct answer is Section B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Section B names one person for each of the four kinds of failure and says what each will do." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Section A gives ownership to a directorate and a supplier. Neither is one named person, and it does not separate the four kinds of failure."
- **Bridge.** Owners make failures visible, and the next lesson gives you the test that tells you whether the decision is working.

#### Lesson 4. The ninety-day test

- The id is `the-ninety-day-test`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the fourth module of the outline. It turns the outcome from Lesson 1 into something you can check ninety days after launch.
- **Core idea.** A ninety-day test has five parts: a measure that shows the outcome in the work, the starting point measured before launch, a threshold that counts as working, the date it will be checked, and the decision you will make at that date, which is to continue, to change something, or to stop. A test "Can be held to" when someone other than the proposer could collect the measure and reach the same answer. A test "Cannot be held to" when it is vague, has no starting point, or has no decision attached. The test is not a punishment for the team, and it is not a promise that everything will be perfect at ninety days. It is the moment you agreed in advance to look, while it is still cheap to change course.
- **Worked example.** The input is the Platform X case's success statement: "We expect significant improvements in tenant satisfaction." The output is the director's ninety-day test: "Measure: the share of routine repairs reported online rather than by phone. Starting point: 0%, as online reporting does not exist today; phone calls about routine repairs averaged over the three months before launch will be recorded as the baseline. Threshold: at least a third of routine repairs reported online, and chasing calls lower than the baseline. Date: ninety days after go-live. Decision: continue if both are met; if online reporting is met but chasing calls are not, change the notification texts before the next review; if online reporting is below a sixth, stop and review the contract's break clause." The reading is that the original statement could never be shown to be false. The test can, and it says in advance what the board will do with each result.
- **Practice.** You write the measure and starting point for your own ninety-day test.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four success statements come from different proposals. Mark each statement with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Can be held to" and "Cannot be held to".
  - "Staff will find it easier to collaborate." The correct label is "Cannot be held to". The feedback for a right answer is: "It has no measure, no starting point, no threshold and no decision." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Ask who could check this at ninety days and reach the same answer. Nobody could."
  - "At ninety days, at least 80% of purchase orders will be raised in the system, against 0% today, checked by finance on 30 June; below 50% we pause the rollout to other departments." The correct label is "Can be held to". The feedback for a right answer is: "It has a measure, a starting point, a threshold, a date and a decision." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check the five parts. All of them are here."
  - "Customer satisfaction will improve by 10%." The correct label is "Cannot be held to". The feedback for a right answer is: "There is no starting point, no date and no decision, so it cannot be held to." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "A number is not enough. It does not say from what, by when, or what happens if it is missed."
  - "By 30 September, the average time from job advert to offer will fall from the current 41 days to under 30; if not, HR will report why and propose a change." The correct label is "Can be held to". The feedback for a right answer is: "It names the measure, the starting point, the threshold, the date and what happens if it is missed." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Every part of the test is present, and someone else could check it."
- **Bridge.** You now have all the parts of a decision, and the next lesson shows you how they fit on one page by repairing a weak note.

#### Lesson 5. Repair a decision note

- The id is `repair-a-decision-note`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson prepares you for the final lesson. You practise the shape of the note on someone else's work before you write your own.
- **Core idea.** A decision note is one page with five sections: what we are buying, stated as outcomes in the work; the five questions, each answered or marked as open; the owners of the four kinds of failure; the ninety-day test; and the decision, which is approve, approve with conditions, or not yet, with the reason. It is written for the people who will be asked later why the decision was made, and for the person who will run the ninety-day review. It is not the business case, and it should be much shorter. A decision of "approve with conditions" must say exactly which open questions must be answered, by whom and by when.
- **Worked example.** The input is a weak note: "Approved. The board was impressed by the demonstration and the supplier's track record." The output is the repaired decision section: "Decision: approve with conditions. The proposal answers the first, third and fourth questions. Before contract signature, the Head of Repairs will confirm the date on which routine phone reporting stops, and the Head of Procurement will confirm that the contract allows full export of tenant and repair data on exit. The ninety-day review is on 15 December, chaired by the Director of Operations." The reading is that the weak note recorded an impression. The repaired one records what was approved, what was still open, who will close it, and when the board will look again.
- **Practice.** You are shown a note with every section except the decision filled in, and you write the decision with its reason.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this decision note so another leader could hold the proposer to it. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "Proposal: new CRM for the sales team. Decision: approved, as the sales director is keen and the price is within budget. Success: better customer relationships." A correct edit states at least one outcome in the work, marks each of the five questions as answered or open, names owners for the four kinds of failure, replaces the success line with a ninety-day test with all five parts, and gives a decision with a reason and any conditions. The feedback for a correct edit is: "The note now says what is being bought as work, which questions are answered, who owns each failure, how you will test it at ninety days, and why you decided as you did." The feedback when the outcome is missing is: "The note still does not say what will be different in the work." The feedback when the owners are missing is: "Name one person for each of the four kinds of failure." The feedback when the test is vague is: "'Better customer relationships' cannot be held to. Give a measure, a starting point, a threshold, a date and a decision." The feedback when the reason is weak is: "'Keen' and 'within budget' are not reasons that answer the five questions. Say which questions the proposal answers and what remains open."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you write the decision note for a real proposal of your own.

#### Lesson 6. Your decision note

- The id is `your-decision-note`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the final lesson of the course. The note you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** Your decision note covers a real proposal you are deciding on now or decided recently. It uses the five sections from Lesson 5 and draws on the work you did earlier in the course. A note that concludes "not yet" is as valid as one that approves, as long as it says which questions must be answered first. The test is whether a colleague who was not in the meeting could read the note, run the ninety-day review, and know what to do with the result.
- **Worked example.** The input is the housing director's notes on Platform X. The output is a note that restates the proposal as three outcomes in the work, marks the second and fifth questions as open, names four owners, sets the ninety-day test from Lesson 4, and records the decision to approve with two conditions to be met before signature. The reading is that the note is one page, it would survive the departure of anyone named in it, and it tells the next person exactly what they are being held to.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. Your answers from the earlier practices are shown in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the decision note for a real proposal. Include all five sections." The fields are labelled "What we are buying", "Five questions", "Who owns the failure", "The ninety-day test" and "Decision and reason". The note passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your note states the outcomes, answers or marks each question, names an owner for each kind of failure, sets a test someone can hold you to, and gives a decision with its reason." When a section fails, the feedback names it, for example: "Supplier failure has no owner" or "The ninety-day test has no starting point." After a pass, you type your name to sign the note.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed decision note is on your record and suggests you put the ninety-day review in the diary of the person who will chair it.

### The artefact and the signed record

The learner produces a one-page decision note with five sections. The signed record shows the learner's signed name, the course title, the date of signing, and the decision note as written. The learner is reminded to leave out commercially confidential figures if the note may be shared, and may describe costs in general terms instead.

### How learning is validated

The note passes only when every section does its job. What we are buying must state at least one outcome in the work, and a list of features alone is rejected. Each of the five questions must have either an answer or the words "Leaves it open". Each of the four kinds of failure must have one named person or role, and a team, board or supplier is rejected as an owner. The ninety-day test must have all five parts: measure, starting point, threshold, date and decision. The decision must be one of approve, approve with conditions, or not yet, with a reason that refers to the five questions, and any conditions must name who meets them and by when. Each check in Lessons 1 to 5 must also have been passed.

## 9. Connecting the Tools Your Team Already Uses

The slug is `connecting-the-tools-your-team-already-uses`. The course runs for two and a half hours and costs £129. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: The handoff, Connect it, Run this week's inputs, and The map.

### Who it is for

This course is for operations leads, coordinators and team managers whose work passes between two or more tools by someone retyping, copying or forwarding it, such as a web form into a CRM, a CRM into an invoicing tool, or a ticket system into a project board. You should know both tools well enough to say which fields matter, and you should have access to the integration settings of at least one of them, or to someone who does. You do not need to write code. Before connecting anything that holds personal or financial data, you should have the agreement of whoever manages your systems.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can find a handoff where work is retyped between two tools, decide which tool is the source of truth for each piece of information, map the fields from one to the other with a matching identifier, connect them using a built-in integration or a connector your organisation already allows, with only the access the connection needs, test it on this week's real inputs, and write a map that shows exactly where a person must still check. You leave with a handoff map for one real connection.

### Learning objectives

1. Identify a handoff where information moves between tools by a person retyping, copying or forwarding it.
2. Decide which tool is the source of truth for each piece of information and choose an identifier that matches records in both.
3. Connect the two tools with a built-in integration or an approved connector, granting only the access the connection needs.
4. Test the connection on this week's real inputs and record which arrived as they should and which needed a person.
5. Write a handoff map that states what a person must still check, who, and when.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. Find the handoff

- The id is `find-the-handoff`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson is the first module of the outline. It helps you find the one place where connecting two tools would save the most retyping and the most mistakes.
- **Core idea.** A "Handoff" is any point where information leaves one tool and enters another because a person moves it, by retyping, copying and pasting, exporting and importing, or forwarding an email. "Work within one tool" is a step that happens inside a single tool, however many clicks it takes. Handoffs are where errors creep in, because a person can mistype a figure, miss a record, or be on holiday. A handoff is not automatically worth connecting; it is worth connecting when it happens often, when the same fields are moved every time, and when a mistake causes real trouble later, such as an invoice sent to the wrong address. Connecting two tools does not remove the need for judgement, and later lessons are about keeping the checks that matter.
- **Worked example.** The input is an events coordinator's description of a booking: "A customer books on the website form. The form emails me. I copy their details into the CRM. When they pay, I look them up in the CRM and create an invoice in Xero by retyping their name, company and the amount. I then add them to the attendee list in a spreadsheet." The output marks three handoffs: form email to CRM, CRM to Xero, and CRM to the attendee spreadsheet. It marks looking up the customer in the CRM as work within one tool. The reading is that the coordinator moves the same name and company by hand three times for every booking. The CRM to Xero handoff is the one to connect first, because a mistake there puts a wrong invoice in front of a customer.
- **Practice.** You describe one process in your team step by step, and mark each step "Handoff" or "Work within one tool".
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A support team lead described how a complaint is handled. Mark each step with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Handoff" and "Work within one tool".
  - "The complaint arrives in the shared inbox, and the adviser copies the customer's details into a new ticket in the helpdesk." The correct label is "Handoff". The feedback for a right answer is: "Details move from the inbox to the helpdesk because a person copies them." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Two tools are involved here, and a person moves the information between them."
  - "The adviser assigns the ticket to the complaints queue in the helpdesk." The correct label is "Work within one tool". The feedback for a right answer is: "This happens entirely inside the helpdesk." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Nothing leaves the helpdesk in this step, so it is work within one tool."
  - "When a refund is agreed, the adviser emails finance the order number and amount, and finance enters the refund in the payments system." The correct label is "Handoff". The feedback for a right answer is: "The refund details move from the helpdesk to the payments system by email and retyping." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at how the amount reaches the payments system. A person retypes it from an email."
  - "The adviser adds an internal note to the ticket recording the outcome." The correct label is "Work within one tool". The feedback for a right answer is: "Adding a note happens inside the helpdesk." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The note is written and kept in the helpdesk, so no information moves between tools."
- **Bridge.** Once you have found the handoff, the next lesson decides exactly what moves across it and how the two tools will recognise the same record.

#### Lesson 2. What moves, and what must match

- The id is `what-moves-and-what-must-match`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson expands the first module. It prepares the plan you will build from in the next lesson.
- **Core idea.** For each piece of information in the handoff, decide which tool is the "source of truth", meaning the one place where it is created and corrected. Information should flow from the source of truth to the other tool, not back and forth, because two-way copying leads to each tool overwriting the other's corrections. Then write a field map: for each field in the destination, the field in the source it comes from. Finally, choose an identifier, a value that is unique to each record and the same in both tools, such as a customer number or an email address, so the connection can tell whether a record is new or already exists. A field map is not a list of everything in both tools; it lists only what needs to move. A field map is "Ready to connect" when every field has a source and the identifier is named, and "Needs more detail" when either is missing.
- **Worked example.** The input is the coordinator's CRM to Xero handoff. The output is the field map: "Source of truth: the CRM for customer details, Xero for invoices and payments. Identifier: the CRM customer ID, stored in a custom field on the Xero contact. Fields: CRM company name to Xero contact name; CRM billing email to Xero email; CRM billing address to Xero postal address; CRM booking amount to Xero invoice line amount; CRM event name to Xero invoice line description. Direction: CRM to Xero only. Not moved: CRM notes, CRM marketing preferences." The reading is that by naming the source of truth, the coordinator settles what happens when finance corrects an address in Xero: the correction must be made in the CRM, or it will be overwritten on the next booking. Choosing the CRM ID as identifier avoids two contacts for the same company with slightly different names.
- **Practice.** You write the source of truth, the identifier and three field pairs for your own handoff.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two colleagues wrote a field map for the web form to CRM handoff. Choose the map that is ready to connect." The two controls read "Map A" and "Map B". Map A reads: "Send everything from the form into the CRM and keep them in sync both ways." Map B reads: "Source of truth: the CRM for contacts. Identifier: email address. Fields: form 'Your name' to CRM full name; form 'Email' to CRM email; form 'Organisation' to CRM company; form 'Event' to CRM booking event. If the email already exists in the CRM, add the booking to that contact rather than creating a new one. Direction: form to CRM only." The correct answer is Map B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Map B names the source of truth, the identifier, each field and its source, the direction, and what happens with an existing contact." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Map A has no identifier, no field pairs and syncs both ways, so the tools could overwrite each other and create duplicates. It needs more detail."
- **Bridge.** With the map ready to connect, the next lesson builds the connection with only the access it needs.

#### Lesson 3. Connect it

- The id is `connect-it`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It covers the choice of connection and the access you grant to it.
- **Core idea.** Look first for a built-in integration between the two tools, listed in each tool's app marketplace or integration settings, because it is usually maintained by the vendor and supported when something changes. If there is none, use a connector service your organisation already licenses and allows, such as Microsoft Power Automate, Zapier or Make, rather than signing up for a new one. When you connect, the tool will ask for permissions, and the default is often far wider than your map needs. Grant only the access your field map uses: read on the source fields, write on the destination fields, and nothing that lets the connection delete records or reach unrelated data. Run the connection under an account that belongs to the team or a service account rather than your personal login where your IT team allows it, so it does not stop when you leave. In this course, a permission is "Access the connection needs" when a line of your field map uses it, and "Access it does not need" when none does.
- **Worked example.** The input is the permissions screen that appears when the coordinator connects the CRM to Xero using the CRM's built-in Xero integration: "Read and write contacts. Read and write invoices. Read bank transactions. Read and write payroll. Access organisation settings." The output is the coordinator's note to the finance lead: "The field map only needs to create and update contacts and create draft invoices. It does not need bank transactions, payroll or organisation settings. The integration offers a restricted scope for contacts and invoices, and I have chosen that. Invoices will be created as drafts, so finance still approves each one before it is sent." The reading is that the default would have let a sales tool read the bank and payroll. The restricted scope matches the field map, and creating drafts keeps a person in the loop for the step that affects customers.
- **Practice.** You list the permissions your own connection asks for, or would ask for, and label each one.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A connection will copy new helpdesk tickets tagged 'bug' into the development team's project board. Mark each permission it asks for with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Access the connection needs" and "Access it does not need".
  - "Read tickets and their tags in the helpdesk." The correct label is "Access the connection needs". The feedback for a right answer is: "The connection must read tickets and tags to find the bugs." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Without reading tickets and tags, the connection cannot find what to copy."
  - "Create cards on the development board." The correct label is "Access the connection needs". The feedback for a right answer is: "Each bug becomes a card, so the connection must be able to create them." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The field map writes a card for each bug, so this access is needed."
  - "Delete tickets in the helpdesk." The correct label is "Access it does not need". The feedback for a right answer is: "Nothing in the map deletes tickets, and this access could destroy customer records." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look at the field map. Nothing in it removes a ticket from the helpdesk."
  - "Read customer billing details in the helpdesk." The correct label is "Access it does not need". The feedback for a right answer is: "The developers need the bug description, not billing details." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Billing details play no part in copying a bug to the board, so this access should be refused."
- **Bridge.** The connection is built, and the next lesson tests it on the inputs your team actually had this week.

#### Lesson 4. Run this week's inputs

- The id is `run-this-weeks-inputs`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It tests the connection against real work before anyone relies on it.
- **Core idea.** Take the real inputs from this week that would have crossed the handoff, and put them through the connection in a test mode, a sandbox, or with the destination set to drafts, then compare each result with what a person would have entered. Include the awkward ones on purpose: a record that already exists, one with a missing field, one with an unusual character or format, and one that a person would have treated differently. An input "Arrived as it should" when every mapped field is correct and no duplicate was created. An input "Needs a person" when anything is wrong, missing or duplicated, or when the right answer needed a judgement the connection cannot make. A test is not the connection reporting success, because the most common failures are quiet: a duplicate, a truncated field, or a record that was skipped.
- **Worked example.** The input is this week's six paid bookings in the CRM. The output is the test log: "Four bookings arrived as they should, as draft invoices with the correct contact, amount and event. One booking from a returning customer created a second Xero contact, because the old contact had no CRM ID stored; it needs a person, and the fix is to add the CRM ID to existing Xero contacts once. One booking was for a charity that is invoiced without VAT; the draft used the standard rate. It needs a person, because the VAT treatment is a finance judgement." The reading is that the connection handled the routine cases and failed on two cases that were each a real risk: a duplicate contact, and an invoice at the wrong tax rate. One can be fixed in the data. The other is a check that must stay with a person, which is the subject of the next lesson.
- **Practice.** You list this week's real inputs for your own handoff, marking which one is the awkward case you most want to test.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "A connection copies new job applicants from the careers form into the recruitment tracker. Here is what happened to four of this week's applicants. Mark each result with the label it deserves." The two controls read "Arrived as it should" and "Needs a person".
  - "A new applicant's name, email, role and CV link all appeared correctly in the tracker." The correct label is "Arrived as it should". The feedback for a right answer is: "Every mapped field is correct and there is no duplicate." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Check each field. All of them arrived correctly, so this one is fine."
  - "An applicant who had applied for a different role last month now appears twice in the tracker." The correct label is "Needs a person". The feedback for a right answer is: "A duplicate was created, so the identifier or the rule for existing records needs looking at." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Two records for one person is a quiet failure. It needs a person to fix it."
  - "An applicant's surname, Ó Súilleabháin, appeared as O Suilleabhain." The correct label is "Needs a person". The feedback for a right answer is: "The name was changed on the way across, which could cause offence and mismatches later." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Look closely at the name. The accents were lost, so the record is not correct."
  - "An applicant who left the optional phone field blank appeared with every other field correct and the phone field empty." The correct label is "Arrived as it should". The feedback for a right answer is: "An empty optional field is correct when the applicant left it blank." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The applicant did not give a phone number, so an empty field is the right result."
- **Bridge.** The test shows where the connection cannot be trusted alone, and the next lesson maps what a person must still check.

#### Lesson 5. What a person still checks

- The id is `what-a-person-still-checks`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson prepares the fourth module. It turns the cases that needed a person into checks that someone owns.
- **Core idea.** A connected handoff still needs a person at the points where a judgement is required, where a mistake would reach a customer, money or someone's personal data, or where the test showed the connection can go quietly wrong. Each check has four parts: what is checked, who checks it, when, and what they do if it is wrong. A check is not a general instruction to "keep an eye on it", which nobody owns and which lapses in the first busy week. It is also not a reason to abandon the connection; a good connection with two named checks is far safer than a person retyping everything by hand.
- **Worked example.** The input is the test log from Lesson 4. The output is the checks section: "Check one: every draft invoice for a charity or overseas customer is reviewed for VAT before it is approved, by the finance assistant, when approving drafts each morning; if wrong, she corrects the VAT and tells the coordinator. Check two: on the first Monday of each month, the coordinator runs Xero's duplicate contacts report and merges any duplicates, noting which CRM record was missing its ID. Check three: on Friday, the coordinator compares the number of paid bookings in the CRM with the number of draft invoices created that week; if they differ, she finds the missing booking and raises the invoice by hand." The reading is that each case that needed a person in testing now has a named owner and a time. The third check catches the quiet failure where a booking never crosses at all, which nobody would otherwise notice.
- **Practice.** You write one check for your own connection with all four parts.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this map so that someone else could run the checks while you are away. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "The web form now feeds the CRM automatically. Someone should keep an eye on duplicates, and sales should check the leads look right." A correct edit gives each check what is checked, who checks it, when, and what they do if it is wrong, and adds a check that the number of form submissions matches the number of new CRM records. The feedback for a correct edit is: "Each check now names what is checked, who, when, and what to do if it is wrong, and there is a count that would catch a lead that never arrived." The feedback when the owner is missing is: "'Someone' and 'sales' are not one named person or role. Say who runs each check." The feedback when the timing is missing is: "Say when each check happens, such as each morning or the first Monday of the month." The feedback when the action is missing is: "Say what the person does when a check finds a problem." The feedback when the count is missing is: "Nothing here would notice a form submission that never reached the CRM. Add a count that compares the two."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you bring the handoff, the field map, the connection, the test and the checks together in the map that goes on your record.

#### Lesson 6. Your handoff map

- The id is `your-handoff-map`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the fourth module of the outline and the final lesson. The map you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** Your handoff map describes one real connection you have built and tested. It has five sections: the handoff and why it was chosen; the source of truth, identifier, direction and field map; the connection, with the account it runs under and the access it was granted and refused; the test on this week's inputs, with what arrived as it should and what needed a person; and the checks a person still makes, each with all four parts. It is not a diagram of the whole system, and it does not claim that the connection is free of errors. The test is whether a colleague could take over the connection tomorrow and know what it does, what it cannot be trusted with, and what they must check.
- **Worked example.** The input is the events coordinator's notes on the CRM to Xero connection. The output is a map that records the three handoffs she found and why she chose this one, the field map with the CRM ID as identifier, the built-in integration with restricted scope and draft invoices, the six-booking test with two cases that needed a person, and the three named checks. The reading is that the map is candid about the two failures in testing, and that candour is what lets the finance lead approve the connection.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. Your answers from the earlier practices are shown in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the map for the connection you built. Include all five sections." The fields are labelled "The handoff", "What moves and what must match", "The connection and its access", "This week's inputs" and "What a person still checks". The map passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your map names the handoff, the source of truth and identifier, the access granted and refused, the results on real inputs, and the checks a person still owns." When a section fails, the feedback names it, for example: "No identifier is named" or "The checks do not say who runs them." After a pass, you type your name to sign the map.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed map is on your record and suggests you save a copy where the people who run the checks can find it.

### The artefact and the signed record

The learner produces a handoff map with five sections for one real connection. The signed record shows the learner's signed name, the course title, the date of signing, and the handoff map as written. It does not include any records that passed through the connection.

### How learning is validated

The map passes only when every section does its job. The handoff must name two tools and the way a person moved information between them. The field map must name the source of truth, an identifier present in both tools, a direction, and at least three field pairs. The connection section must name the method, the account it runs under, and at least one permission that was refused or not needed, or state that the integration offered only the access the map uses. The test must cover at least four real inputs including at least one awkward case, each marked as arrived as it should or needs a person. Every case that needed a person must lead to either a fix and a passing retest or a named check. There must be at least one check with all four parts, and at least one check must compare counts between the two tools. The learner is reminded not to paste real personal data into the map. Each check in Lessons 1 to 5 must also have been passed.

- Zapier Help Center, the documentation on triggers, actions, filters, deduplication and troubleshooting.
- National Cyber Security Centre, *10 Steps to Cyber Security*, in particular the step on identity and access management, on granting only the access a job needs.
- Information Commissioner's Office, the guidance on data minimisation under UK GDPR, on moving only the personal data a purpose needs.

## 10. Running a Technology Rollout

The slug is `running-a-technology-rollout`. The course runs for two hours and costs £129. Its spine is the four modules in the catalogue: The behaviour, Who and when, Support, then stop, and The sign it stuck.

### Who it is for

This course is for project leads, operations managers and team leaders who have been asked to roll out a tool across a team or several teams, such as a new booking system, a shared drive, a sign-in process or a reporting tool. The tool has usually been chosen already. You should know what the tool is for and which groups of people will use it. You do not need project management qualifications or technical knowledge of the tool.

### Outcome

When you finish, you can define the rollout's success as a behaviour you can see in the work, sequence the rollout from a small first group to the whole organisation, give each group an owner and a date, plan a period of support followed by a firm stop to the old way, and name the sign in the work that will show the tool has stuck, with when and how you will check it. You leave with a rollout sheet for one tool.

### Learning objectives

1. Define the success of a rollout as a behaviour that can be seen in the work, rather than an activity.
2. Sequence a rollout so that each step learns from the one before it.
3. Assign an owner and a date to each group in the rollout.
4. Plan a support window with a named helper, followed by a date on which the old way stops.
5. Write a rollout sheet that names the sign it stuck and when it will be checked.

### Lessons

#### Lesson 1. The behaviour

- The id is `the-behaviour`.
- **Where it sits.** This opening lesson is the first module of the outline. It sets the definition of success that every other part of the rollout sheet is measured against.
- **Core idea.** A rollout succeeds when people do a piece of their work differently, and keep doing it after the attention moves elsewhere. "A behaviour you can see" is a sentence that describes that change in the work, with who does it and when, such as "every visitor is signed in on the tablet at reception before they are given a badge". "Only an activity" is a sentence about the rollout itself, such as "the training sessions are held" or "the tool is live". Activities are necessary, and a rollout sheet will list many of them. But an activity can be finished while the behaviour never changes, so success must be written as the behaviour first, and every activity must serve it.
- **Worked example.** The input is the objective in a rollout brief for a new visitor management system: "Successfully deploy the visitor system across all five sites by the end of Q2, with all reception staff trained." The output is the rollout lead's rewrite: "By the end of June, at all five sites, every visitor is signed in on the tablet before they are given a badge, and the paper visitor book is no longer at reception." The reading is that the original objective could be met with every tablet switched off and every visitor still signing the paper book. The rewrite can only be met if the behaviour at reception has changed, and it names what will have disappeared, the paper book, which is the easiest thing to check.
- **Practice.** You write the behaviour you want your rollout to produce in one sentence, naming who does it and when.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four sentences come from a rollout plan for a new room booking tool. Mark each sentence with the label it deserves." The two controls read "A behaviour you can see" and "Only an activity".
  - "The booking tool will be live on 1 September." The correct label is "Only an activity". The feedback for a right answer is: "Going live is a step in the rollout, and people could still book rooms the old way." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Being live says nothing about how people book rooms. It is only an activity."
  - "Staff book every meeting room through the tool before they use it, and the paper sheets are gone from the doors." The correct label is "A behaviour you can see". The feedback for a right answer is: "This describes what people do and what has disappeared, which you could see." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Walk past the rooms in your head. You could see whether this is true, so it is a behaviour you can see."
  - "Two training sessions will be held for each floor." The correct label is "Only an activity". The feedback for a right answer is: "Training is an activity that serves the behaviour, not the behaviour itself." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "The sessions could all be held and nobody change how they book. It is only an activity."
  - "Reception checks the tool, not the sheets, when someone asks whether a room is free." The correct label is "A behaviour you can see". The feedback for a right answer is: "This is a change in what reception does, which you could observe." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This describes a person doing their work differently. It is a behaviour you can see."
- **Bridge.** With the behaviour defined, the next lesson puts the rollout in the order that gives it the best chance.

#### Lesson 2. The order of a rollout

- The id is `the-order-of-a-rollout`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson expands the second module. The order in which a rollout happens is the skill here, so the check asks you to put the steps in sequence.
- **Core idea.** A rollout that starts everywhere at once finds every problem at once, when nobody has time to fix them. A sound rollout follows an order in which each step learns from the one before. First, a small group tries the tool on their real work for a short period. Second, the problems that group found are fixed, or a way round them is written down. Third, the next group starts, with a named helper, often someone from the first group, available to them. Fourth, the old way is stopped for that group on a date announced in advance. Fifth, you look for the sign that the behaviour has stuck before you move to the next group. Skipping the second step means the next group meets the same problems, and skipping the fifth means you are rolling on without knowing whether the last group changed at all. The order is not a rigid law, and a very small team might complete all five steps in a week, but the sequence stays the same.
- **Worked example.** The input is the visitor system rollout plan: "Week one: install tablets at all five sites and train all reception staff. Week two: go live everywhere." The output is the revised plan: "Weeks one and two: the head office reception uses the tablet for every visitor, with the paper book as a back-up. Week three: fix the two problems head office found, a badge printer that jams and contractors who arrive without an email address. Weeks four and five: the two largest sites start, with the head office receptionist on call as helper. End of week five: the paper book is removed at head office and at the two sites. Week six: check the sign at those three sites before the last two start." The reading is that the badge printer and the contractor problem would have hit all five sites on the same day under the first plan. Under the revised plan, they are found and fixed while only one site is affected.
- **Practice.** You list the groups in your own rollout and choose which should go first, with one sentence on why.
- **Check.** This is an ordering task, because the sequence is the thing being learned. The question reads: "Put these five steps of a rollout for a new expenses app in the order you would actually do them." The five steps, presented in a shuffled order, read "The finance team uses the app for their own claims for two weeks", "Fix the problems the finance team found, or write down the way round them", "The sales team starts, with a finance team member named as their helper", "Paper claim forms stop being accepted from the sales team on the announced date", and "Check that every sales claim that month came through the app before the next team starts". The correct order is the finance team trial, then the fix, then the sales team start with a helper, then the stop to paper forms, then the check. The feedback for a right answer is: "Each step learns from the one before: a small group tries it, problems are fixed, the next group starts with help, the old way stops, and you check before moving on." The feedback when the fix comes after the sales team starts is: "If the sales team starts before the fix, they meet the same problems the finance team found. Fix first, then widen." The feedback when paper forms stop before the sales team starts is: "Stopping the old way before people have the new one leaves them with no way to claim. Start them, support them, then stop." The feedback when the check comes before the stop is: "While paper forms are still accepted, a check cannot tell you whether the behaviour has changed. Stop the old way, then look for the sign."
- **Bridge.** Each group in that sequence needs an owner and a date, which is the next lesson.

#### Lesson 3. Who and when

- The id is `who-and-when`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the second module of the outline. It fills the sequence with names and dates, so that the rollout can be run and checked.
- **Core idea.** Each group in the rollout needs three things written against it: an owner, who is the person in that group responsible for the change happening there, usually its manager; a helper, who is the person the group can ask for help with the tool; and dates, for the start, for the old way stopping, and for the check. The owner is not the rollout lead, who cannot be in every team, and not the vendor, who does not manage your staff. A group line is "Ready to run" when all three are present, and "Not ready" when any is missing. Most rollouts that drift have dates for the start and nothing after it.
- **Worked example.** The input is the rollout lead's first table for the two large sites: "Site B: April. Site C: April." The output is the revised table: "Site B. Owner: the site manager, Carla. Helper: Ben, the head office receptionist, on call by phone in the first week and on site on the first Monday. Start: 8 April. Paper book removed: 22 April. Check: 26 April. Site C. Owner: the facilities lead, Omar. Helper: Ben, on site on the first Tuesday. Start: 9 April. Paper book removed: 23 April. Check: 26 April." The reading is that "April" told nobody what to do. The revised table tells each site manager what they own, who will help, and the three dates that matter, so the rollout lead can tell at a glance whether it is on track.
- **Practice.** You write the line for the first group in your own rollout, with owner, helper and the three dates.
- **Check.** This is a choice between two pieces of work. The question reads: "Two rollout leads wrote the line for the same team. Choose the line that is ready to run." The two controls read "Line A" and "Line B". Line A reads: "Customer service team: go live in May, supported by IT and the vendor." Line B reads: "Customer service team. Owner: Hana, team manager. Helper: Leo from the pilot group, sitting with the team for the first two mornings. Start: 6 May. Old shared spreadsheet made read-only: 20 May. Check: 24 May." The correct answer is Line B. The feedback for a right answer is: "Line B names the owner, the helper and all three dates, so it is ready to run." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Line A has a month but no owner in the team, no named helper, and no date for stopping the old way or checking. It is not ready."
- **Bridge.** The helper and the stop date are the two halves of the next lesson, which is about support followed by a firm end to the old way.

#### Lesson 4. Support, then stop

- The id is `support-then-stop`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the third module of the outline. It plans the period in which people move from the old way to the new one.
- **Core idea.** Every group needs a support window, a fixed period in which a named helper is easy to reach, the old way is still available as a back-up, and problems are collected and acted on. At the end of the window comes the stop, when the old way is removed or closed in a way people can see, such as a paper book taken away or a spreadsheet made read-only. Support without a stop leaves two systems running indefinitely, and people will use whichever is quicker on a busy day. A stop without support punishes people for problems the rollout did not fix. The order is always support, then stop, and the stop date is announced at the start of the window, not sprung at the end.
- **Worked example.** The input is the support section of the room booking rollout sheet: "Support: help available via the IT service desk." The output is the revised section: "Support window: 1 to 12 September. Helper: Jo from the pilot floor, at the third-floor hub from 9 to 10 each morning and on Teams all day. Back-up: the paper sheets stay on the doors, and Jo copies any paper booking into the tool at 4 each afternoon. Problems: collected in the Booking Issues list and reviewed by the rollout lead on Friday. Stop: on 15 September, the paper sheets are removed from every door; this date is in the launch email on 1 September." The reading is that the original line pointed people to a general service desk with no end. The revised section gives them a person, a back-up, a place for problems, and a stop date they know from the first day.
- **Practice.** You write the stop for your own first group: what old way stops, how people will see that it has stopped, and when they will be told.
- **Check.** This is an editing task. The question reads: "Edit this support section so that the team knows who helps them, for how long, and when the old way ends. Add every part that is missing." The document reads: "Support: the vendor's help centre is available. We will phase out the old system over time." A correct edit gives a support window with start and end dates, a named helper and how to reach them, a back-up during the window, a place where problems are collected and who reviews them, and a stop date with what stops and how people will see it, announced at the start. The feedback for a correct edit is: "The section now names a helper, gives a window with dates, keeps a back-up, collects problems, and ends with a stop people can see and were told about in advance." The feedback when the helper is missing is: "A help centre is not a person. Name a helper and how to reach them." The feedback when the window has no dates is: "Say when the support window starts and ends." The feedback when the stop is vague is: "'Phase out over time' has no date. Say what stops, on which day, and how people will see it." The feedback when the announcement is missing is: "Say when people will be told the stop date. The lesson asks for it at the start of the window."
- **Bridge.** After the stop, you need to know whether the behaviour has really stuck, which is the next lesson.

#### Lesson 5. The sign it stuck

- The id is `the-sign-it-stuck`.
- **Where it sits.** This lesson is the fourth module of the outline. It gives you the test that tells you the rollout is finished for a group.
- **Core idea.** "A sign it stuck" is evidence in the work, some weeks after the stop and after the attention has moved on, that the behaviour from Lesson 1 is still happening. It is best when it is something you can check yourself, such as picking ten recent visitors and finding them all in the tablet log, or finding no new entries in the old spreadsheet. "A sign it launched" is evidence that the rollout happened, such as the number of accounts created, attendance at training, or a high number of logins in the first week. Signs it launched are useful for running the rollout but tell you nothing about whether the change will last. You decide the sign it stuck, and the date you will check it, before the rollout begins, and you check it at least a few weeks after the support window has closed.
- **Worked example.** The input is the success measures suggested by the vendor of the visitor system: "Number of sites live. Number of reception staff trained. Number of visitor sign-ins in the first week." The output is the rollout lead's sign it stuck: "Six weeks after the paper book is removed at each site, I will ask the site owner for the list of contractors who were on site on one chosen day, from their own work schedule, and check that each one appears in the tablet log for that day." The reading is that the vendor's measures all describe the launch. The rollout lead's sign compares the tablet log with an independent record of who was really there, so it can only pass if the behaviour at reception has held.
- **Practice.** You write the sign it stuck for your own rollout, with the date you will check it and how.
- **Check.** This is a marking task. The question reads: "These four measures come from the rollout of a new shared drive. Mark each measure with the label it deserves." The two controls read "A sign it stuck" and "A sign it launched".
  - "All forty staff have been given access to the shared drive." The correct label is "A sign it launched". The feedback for a right answer is: "Access shows the drive was set up, not that people store their work there." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "Every person could have access and still save to their desktop. This shows only that it launched."
  - "Two months after the old server was made read-only, no one has asked IT to restore a file from it." The correct label is "A sign it stuck". The feedback for a right answer is: "Weeks after the stop, nobody is reaching for the old way." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This is evidence, well after the stop, that people are no longer using the old server. It is a sign it stuck."
  - "Ninety per cent of staff attended the launch session." The correct label is "A sign it launched". The feedback for a right answer is: "Attendance shows the rollout happened, not that the behaviour changed." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "A session is part of the launch. It says nothing about where people save their files now."
  - "In a random check of ten client files from last month, all ten were in the right shared folder and none were on personal drives." The correct label is "A sign it stuck". The feedback for a right answer is: "A check on real work, after the attention moved on, shows the behaviour held." The feedback for a wrong answer is: "This checks where the work actually is, weeks later. It is a sign it stuck."
- **Bridge.** In the last lesson you bring the behaviour, the order, the owners, the support and the sign together in the rollout sheet that goes on your record.

#### Lesson 6. Your rollout sheet

- The id is `your-rollout-sheet`.
- **Where it sits.** This is the final lesson of the course. The sheet you write here is what appears on your signed record.
- **Core idea.** Your rollout sheet covers one real tool you are rolling out, or are about to. It has five sections: the behaviour; the order of groups, with why the first group goes first; a line for each group with owner, helper and dates; the support window and stop for each group; and the sign it stuck, with when and how you will check it. It is not a project plan with every task, and it should fit on one page. The test is whether each group's owner could read their line and know exactly what they are responsible for and when, and whether someone else could check the sign it stuck on the date you set.
- **Worked example.** The input is the visitor system rollout lead's notes. The output is a sheet that states the reception behaviour, starts with head office because it has the most visitors and an experienced receptionist, gives each of the five sites a line with its owner, Ben as helper, and three dates, sets a two-week support window with the paper book as back-up and a stop date announced on day one, and sets the six-week contractor check as the sign it stuck. The reading is that the sheet is one page, each site manager can find their own line, and the success of the rollout will be decided by what happens at reception in August, not by what happened at launch in April.
- **Practice.** There is no separate practice in the final lesson. Your answers from the earlier practices are shown in a panel you can expand.
- **Check.** This is the writing task for the artefact. The question reads: "Write the rollout sheet for one tool. Include all five sections." The fields are labelled "The behaviour", "The order of groups", "Who and when", "Support, then stop" and "The sign it stuck". The sheet passes when it meets the validation rules below. The feedback for a pass is: "Your sheet defines a behaviour you can see, puts the groups in an order that learns, names an owner, helper and dates for each, plans support before the stop, and sets a sign it stuck with a date." When a section fails, the feedback names it, for example: "The behaviour is only an activity" or "Group two has no stop date" or "The sign it stuck is a count of logins." After a pass, you type your name to sign the sheet.
- **Bridge.** This is the last lesson. The closing screen tells you that your signed rollout sheet is on your record and suggests you put the date of the sign it stuck in your diary now.

### The artefact and the signed record

The learner produces a one-page rollout sheet with five sections for one tool. The signed record shows the learner's signed name, the course title, the date of signing, and the rollout sheet as written.

### How learning is validated

The sheet passes only when every section does its job. The behaviour must describe who does what in the work and must not be only an activity such as going live or holding training. The order must list at least two groups, with a reason why the first goes first, and must include a step for fixing problems before the next group starts. Every group line must have an owner who belongs to that group, a named helper, and dates for the start, the stop and the check. The support section must give a window with dates, a back-up, and a stop that names what stops and how people will see it, announced at the start of the window. The sign it stuck must be evidence in the work, not a count of accounts, logins or attendance, with a date at least several weeks after the stop and a description of how it will be checked. Each check in Lessons 1 to 5 must also have been passed.
